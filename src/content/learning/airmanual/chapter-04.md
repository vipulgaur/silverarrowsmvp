## Overview

Exercise 4 is about the flight controls. You will learn the three axes of movement, the primary effect of each control, the further (secondary) effect of each control, and how trim, power, flaps, and other controls affect the aeroplane. Learn the individual effects first. Smooth, coordinated flying comes later.

## The Three Axes

An aeroplane moves in three dimensions about three axes through the centre of gravity (CG):

- **Pitch** — motion about the **lateral** axis (nose up or down).
- **Roll** — motion about the **longitudinal** axis (wing up or down).
- **Yaw** — motion about the **normal** axis (nose left or right).

"Normal" means perpendicular. The yaw axis is only vertical in the cruise. In a bank or a pitch change, it is not vertical.

## Stability

Stability is the aeroplane's natural tendency to hold its attitude, or return to it, after a disturbance, without pilot action.

- Most trainers are reasonably stable in pitch. Trimmed correctly, they hold a steady nose attitude hands-off.
- They are less stable in roll and yaw. If a gust moves the wings, the aeroplane will slowly enter a descending spiral unless the pilot levels the wings.

## The Primary Effects

The pilot controls each axis with one primary control:

- **Elevator** controls **pitch**. Operated by fore-and-aft movement of the control column.
- **Ailerons** control **roll**. Operated by rotating or moving the control column sideways.
- **Rudder** controls **yaw**. Operated by the rudder pedals.

### The Elevator — Pitch

Move the control column back to raise the elevator. This lowers the pressure under the tailplane, pulls the tail down, and pitches the nose up about the CG. Move the column forward for the opposite. At normal speed the movements are small — more pressure than movement. Set the nose attitude, then centralise the column.

![Fig-04-03: The elevator controls pitching](two side profiles — left, "control column back / up elevator / downward force at tail / nose up about CG"; right, "control column forward / down elevator / upward force at tail / nose down about CG". Crisp vector line drawing.)

### The Ailerons — Roll

The ailerons are on the outer trailing edge of each wing. When one goes down, its wing gains lift; the other goes up and loses lift, so the aeroplane rolls. Move the column left and the aeroplane rolls left. The roll continues while the ailerons are deflected. Centralise the column to stop the roll.

![Fig-04-04: A roll to the left](a front view of an aeroplane rolling left, with two aerofoil cross-sections below — the left wing with an up-aileron and reduced lift, the right wing with a down-aileron and increased lift. Clear aerodynamic illustration.)

### The Rudder — Yaw

The rudder is at the rear of the fin, worked by the pedals. Push the left pedal to yaw the nose left; push the right pedal to yaw right.

Yawing is uncomfortable and inefficient — it adds drag. It is a poor way to turn, like turning a bicycle without leaning. The rudder's main job is to **prevent unwanted yaw** — to keep **balanced flight**. The balance ball shows balance. If the ball slides out, apply **same-side rudder** (ball right → right rudder) to centre it.

![Fig-04-05: Centre the balance ball](a close-up of a turn coordinator with a level aircraft symbol, a "2 MIN" rating, and the balance ball deflected to the right in its curved tube. Clean vector graphics.)

### Controls Work Relative to the Aeroplane

Each control works the same way relative to the aeroplane, whatever its attitude. Forward column always moves the nose away from the pilot — even inverted. Motion is referred to the aeroplane's own axes, not the ground.

## The Further (Secondary) Effects

Used alone, the ailerons and rudder each have a primary **and** a further effect.

### Roll Causes Yaw (further effect of the ailerons)

A bank tilts the lift vector, so a sideways component makes the aeroplane slip toward the lower wing. The airflow then strikes the keel surfaces (fin and fuselage) behind the CG and yaws the nose toward the slip. The nose drops and a spiral begins unless the pilot levels the wings. So: ailerons → **roll (primary), then yaw (further)**.

![Fig-04-06: Bank causes sideslip then yaw](two diagrams — left, a front view of an aeroplane banked left with weight vertical and lift tilted, airflow striking the side in the slip; right, a top-down view of the slipping airflow striking the fin and fuselage and yawing the nose toward the lower wing. Clean diagram style.)

### Yaw Causes Roll (further effect of the rudder)

A yaw speeds up the outer wing, so it makes more lift. Dihedral increases the outer wing's angle of attack, adding more lift; the fuselage shields the inner wing, reducing its lift. The aeroplane rolls toward the yaw. So: rudder → **yaw (primary), then roll (further)**. Unchecked, this also leads to a spiral descent.

![Fig-04-07: Rudder causes yaw then roll](two diagrams — left, a top-down view with left rudder creating a yawing moment and the outer wing moving faster for more lift; right, a three-quarter rear view where airflow and dihedral give the outer wing more lift and roll. Crisp training art.)

### Pitch Changes Airspeed (further effect of the elevator)

Raise the nose and drag increases, so the aeroplane slows. Lower the nose and drag decreases, so it speeds up. So: elevator → **pitch (primary), then airspeed change (further)**. Whether the aeroplane climbs, descends, or stays level depends on the power set.

![Fig-04-08: Elevator changes airspeed](a side profile showing a nose-up aircraft labelled "Slow" and a nose-down aircraft labelled "Fast". Simple conceptual diagram.)

## Trimming

Trim relieves the steady control pressures needed to hold an attitude. Without trim, you would have to hold a constant push or pull on the column. The trim control (a wheel, lever, or handle) moves a small trim tab on the elevator (or an equivalent device). Set the attitude and power first, hold it, then trim off the residual pressure so the aeroplane flies hands-off. Re-trim after every attitude or power change. Trim is an aid — it is not a primary control and is not used to change attitude.

## The Effect of Power

Power (set with the throttle) controls whether the aeroplane climbs, cruises, or descends at a chosen attitude. Adding power tends to raise the nose and yaw it (from slipstream and engine effects); reducing power tends to lower the nose. Anticipate these changes and correct them with the flight controls, then re-trim.

## Flaps and Other Controls

- **Flaps** lower on the inner trailing edge of each wing. They increase lift and drag, allowing a steeper, slower approach and a lower nose attitude. Lowering flap usually causes a pitch change; anticipate it and re-trim.
- **Carburettor heat, mixture, and other engine controls** are covered in the aircraft handling exercises. Use them per the POH.

## Airmanship

- Stay a visual pilot: look outside most of the time, both for attitude against the horizon and for other aircraft.
- Use the clear "You have control" / "I have control" handover every time.
- Hold the controls lightly. Use smooth pressures, not large movements, at normal flying speeds.
- Keep the aeroplane in balance with the rudder. Re-trim after every change.
