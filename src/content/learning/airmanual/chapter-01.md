## Overview

Exercise 1 introduces the parts of the training aeroplane. You will learn the main airframe sections, the cockpit controls, the engine, the electrical system, the instruments, and the basic emergency drills. Learn these names first. You will use them in every later exercise.

## The Airframe

The aeroplane has five main parts:

- **Fuselage** — the central body. It carries the crew, passengers, and load.
- **Wings (mainplanes)** — they produce lift.
- **Tail (empennage)** — it stabilises the aeroplane, like the feathers on a dart.
- **Undercarriage (landing gear)** — it supports the aeroplane on the ground.
- **Powerplant** — the engine and propeller. They produce thrust.

The propeller pulls the aeroplane through the air. The airflow over the wings then produces lift. Lift supports the aeroplane in flight. The aeroplane can still fly without thrust in a glide descent.

The tail has two parts: a vertical stabiliser (fin) and a horizontal stabiliser. Both are shaped to produce stabilising aerodynamic forces.

The crew sit in the cockpit, usually side by side. The pilot-in-command sits on the left.

## The Flight Controls

Three primary flight controls change the attitude of the aeroplane. Each one moves an airflow surface. Each surface changes an aerodynamic force.

- **Elevator** — hinged to the horizontal stabiliser. It controls pitch (nose up or down). The control column moves fore and aft.
- **Ailerons** — hinged to the outer trailing edge of each wing. They control roll. The control column moves sideways.
- **Rudder** — hinged to the fin. It controls yaw (nose left or right). The rudder pedals move it.

Two secondary flight controls assist the pilot:

- **Wing flaps** — on the inner trailing edge of each wing.
- **Elevator trim tab** — on the trailing edge of the elevator.

The throttle is not a flight control. It controls engine power. Push it forward to open it and increase power. Pull it back to close it and reduce power. The pilot operates the throttle with the right hand.

### Variations in Design

The same principles apply to all aeroplanes. The design details differ.

- The pilot input device may be a control column (stick), a control wheel (yoke), or a side-stick. In this manual, "control column" means all three.
- The wings may be high, low, or mid-set on the fuselage.
- The tailplane may sit high on the fin (a T-tail).
- A single all-moving tailplane (a stabilator) may replace the fixed stabiliser and elevator.

## The Undercarriage

Most trainers use a tricycle undercarriage: two main wheels and a nosewheel. Some aircraft use a tailwheel instead of a nosewheel.

The nosewheel is usually linked to the rudder pedals. The pedals steer it on the ground. Brakes act on the main wheels. You apply them with the tops of the rudder pedals or with a brake handle.

## The Engine and Propeller

The typical trainer has a piston engine. It runs on Avgas (aviation gasoline). The throttle sets the engine rpm. The tachometer shows the rpm in the cockpit.

A fixed-pitch propeller converts engine power into thrust. Its blade angle cannot change.

Some modern trainers use a FADEC system (Full Authority Digital Engine Control). An Engine Control Unit (ECU) controls the engine. You select power with the throttle. The ECU then sets the manifold pressure, the fuel quantity, and the propeller speed. These aircraft usually have a variable-pitch propeller and often use Jet A1 fuel. Two independent ECUs are fitted for safety.

### Fuel

Fuel management is a high-priority item.

- Fuel is usually stored in wing tanks.
- Low-wing aeroplanes use a fuel pump. High-wing aeroplanes usually use gravity.
- Cockpit gauges show the quantity. Always check the tank contents visually before flight.
- Confirm the fuel grade by its colour. Confirm it is not contaminated.
- Water is the most likely contaminant. Water is denser than fuel and gathers at the low points. Check a sample from the fuel drains before flight.
- The fuel selector chooses which tank feeds the engine. Always select a tank that contains fuel.

### Oil

Oil lubricates and cools the engine. It is stored in a sump. Check the quantity with the dipstick before flight. Two gauges show oil pressure and oil temperature when the engine runs. The normal range is often a green arc.

### Mixture, Carburettor Heat, and Ignition

A carburettor mixes fuel with air. The mixture passes to the cylinders, where combustion occurs.

- The **mixture control** sets a suitable fuel/air mixture.
- The **carburettor heat control** supplies hot air to protect the carburettor from ice.
- A fuel-injected engine has no carburettor. It has no carburettor heat control.
- A FADEC engine sets the mixture automatically. It has no manual mixture control.

The engine has dual ignition. Two magnetos, geared to the engine, generate the sparks. Dual ignition improves combustion and adds safety if one system fails.

The ignition switch normally selects BOTH. It can also select LEFT or RIGHT, and it has an OFF position. A START position connects the battery to the starter motor. Return the switch to BOTH after the engine starts.

## The Electrical System

The battery starts the engine. It also gives emergency back-up power if the alternator (or generator) fails.

The alternator or generator supplies the aircraft services: some flight instruments, the radios, the lights, the flap motor, the pitot heater, and the stall warning system. An aircraft with an alternator needs a serviceable battery to bring the alternator on line. An ammeter or a warning light confirms that current flows.

The magneto ignition systems are separate from the electrical system. A fuse or a circuit breaker protects each electrical circuit.

The radio is an important electrical service. It provides air/ground communication. It has an ON/OFF and volume control, a squelch control, a microphone, and speakers or headphones.

A FADEC aircraft depends on the electrical system, because the ECU needs electrical power to run the engine. These aircraft have an emergency battery to power the ECU if the main electrical system fails.

## The Instruments

The panel has two main instrument groups. The flight instruments sit in front of the pilot. The engine instruments sit near the throttle.

The flight instruments are:

- Airspeed indicator
- Attitude indicator
- Altimeter
- Vertical speed indicator
- Direction indicator (heading indicator)
- Turn coordinator with a balance ball

Airspeed and height instruments use air pressure from the pitot-static system. Attitude, direction, and turn instruments use spinning gyroscopes. The gyroscopes are driven electrically or by vacuum (suction). The magnetic compass is separate. It is placed away from the magnetic influence of the panel and radios.

The engine instruments are the tachometer, the oil pressure gauge, and the oil temperature gauge. Some aircraft add a cylinder head temperature gauge, an ammeter, and a suction gauge.

## Other Items

Check these items before flight:

- **Fire extinguisher** — usually a non-toxic dry-chemical type. Learn how to use it.
- **Control locks** — they stop wind damage when parked. Remove them before flight.
- **Pitot cover** — it keeps insects and water out of the pitot head. Remove it before flight, or the airspeed indicator will not read correctly.
- **Wheel chocks** — they stop the aeroplane rolling when parked.
- **Tie-down kit** — ropes, pegs, and a mallet secure the aeroplane against strong wind.
- **First-aid kit** — carried on some aircraft.

## Checklists

A written checklist confirms that a drill is complete. There are three methods:

1. Do each item as you read the checklist.
2. Do the full drill from memory, then confirm with the checklist.
3. Do the drill from memory, with no written checklist.

Commit vital drills to memory. Action them quickly, then confirm with a checklist if required. Your flying instructor will set the method for your organisation. If checklists are used, carry them in the cockpit.

## Emergency Drills

The Pilot's Operating Handbook lists the drill for each emergency. Learn the basic awareness now. You will study the serious emergencies in detail in later exercises.

**The primary task in any emergency is to maintain flying speed and control the flightpath.** Handle the emergency together with this task — not instead of it.

### Fire

A fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen, and an ignition source. Remove one of them to put the fire out. For example, dry-chemical powder starves a fire of oxygen.

Prevent fire first. Keep fuel and ignition sources apart. When refuelling: no smoking nearby, ground the aircraft and equipment against static sparks, and spill no fuel. Keep a fire extinguisher ready, and know how to use it.

### Engine Fire during Start-Up

Keep the engine turning with the starter. Move the mixture to IDLE CUT-OFF (or the fuel selector to OFF). This purges the fuel, and the fire will probably go out. If it does not:

- Fuel — OFF
- Switches — OFF
- Brakes — ON
- Evacuate, and take the extinguisher.

### Engine Fire in Flight

Shut the engine down. Do not restart it. Maintain flying speed. A leak of fuel or oil usually causes the fire. A typical drill:

- Throttle — CLOSED
- Mixture — IDLE CUT-OFF (or fuel selector OFF)
- Ignition switches — OFF
- Cabin heat — OFF (to keep fumes out of the cockpit)

### Electrical Fire

A peculiar smell often means an electrical fire. Switch off the associated circuits. A typical drill:

- Master switch — OFF
- All other switches (except ignition) — OFF
- Cabin heat — OFF
- Fire extinguisher — use as required, and open the fresh-air vents.
- Land as soon as possible.

### Cabin Fire

Identify the source. Put the fire out with the extinguisher. In flight, maintain flying speed and a safe flightpath. On the ground, secure the aeroplane (engine, switches, and fuel OFF; brakes ON) and consider an immediate evacuation.

### Brake Failure (Taxiing)

- Throttle — CLOSED
- Steer away from other aircraft and obstacles.
- If a collision is imminent: mixture to IDLE CUT-OFF, fuel OFF, ignition OFF, master switch OFF.

### Alternator Failure (FADEC Aircraft)

A FADEC engine depends on the ECU, which needs electrical power. An emergency battery powers the ECU for a limited time after a total electrical failure. If an "alternator fail" warning appears:

- Check that all circuit breakers are in.
- Essential bus — ON.
- Switch off all unnecessary electrical equipment.
