## Overview

Exercise 2 covers everything around the flight itself: preparing for flight, starting and stopping the engine, and the post-flight actions. Good preparation is the foundation of a safe flight. Build a regular, repeatable routine, based on your aircraft's Pilot's Operating Handbook (POH).

## Preparation for Flight

Preparation begins well before you enter the aeroplane. It has six parts:

- Personal preparation
- Pre-flight documentation
- Booking out with Air Traffic Services
- The pre-flight inspection
- Start-up and taxi checks
- The pre-take-off check

### Personal Preparation

The pilot is the key person on the flight. Prepare yourself first. Before leaving home, confirm:

- Am I fit to fly?
- Have I had alcohol in the last eight hours?
- Am I taking any medication that could impair me?
- Do I have a cold, or blocked nose or ears?
- Do I have the equipment this flight needs?
- Am I suitably clothed? (Natural fibres breathe and resist fire better.)

Arrive early. Work calmly and without rush.

### Pre-Flight Documentation

Thorough documentation supports flight safety. Check and complete the required records.

- **Flight Authorisation Sheet** — your training organisation must authorise each flight.
- **Flight plan and weather** — a plan is not usual for a local flight, but weather matters for every flight. Read the forecasts or discuss them with your instructor.
- **Weight and balance** — never exceed a weight limit, and keep the centre of gravity within limits. Most trainers with one or two people aboard are within limits, but always consider weight and balance before every flight.
- **Airworthiness documents** — it is your responsibility to check the Certificate of Airworthiness, the maintenance documents, the weight and balance schedule, and the technical log.

Do not accept an aeroplane with defects that make it unfit for flight. If in doubt, ask your instructor or a licensed engineer.

### Booking Out

Book out with Air Traffic Services (ATS). This makes them aware of your flight so they can record its progress and safe completion. Book out in person or by radio before taxiing.

## The Pre-Flight Inspection

The POH lists the items to check. The notes below are general; follow your own POH.

### The External Inspection (Walk-Around)

Begin as you walk to the aeroplane. Note its position, the wind direction, and the path to the take-off point. Confirm fire extinguishers and emergency equipment are available.

Keep a general overview as you go. Watch for buckled skin, popped rivets, and leaks of oil, fuel, or hydraulic fluid. With experience you develop a feel for what looks right.

Start at the cockpit door and confirm:

- Magneto switches — OFF
- Fuel contents — shown on the gauges
- Control locks — removed
- Flaps — lowered (for inspection)
- Brakes — PARK

Then check the fuel visually and refit the caps. Take a fuel drain sample into a clear container. Check for water (which sinks), sediment, and the correct fuel colour. **Do not fly with contaminated fuel.**

Work around the aeroplane and check, among other items:

- Brakes, brake lines, and disc security
- Undercarriage legs, attachments, tyres, and inflation
- Flaps and ailerons for free, correct movement (one up, one down)
- Wingtips and navigation lights
- Wing leading edges — smooth and clean (ice, frost, or insects reduce lift)
- Pitot cover — removed (or the airspeed indicator will not work)
- Fuel vents — clear; static vent — open and clean
- Propeller and spinner — no nicks, cracks, or looseness
- Engine air intake and filter — clean and unblocked
- Oil contents — checked, cap refitted securely
- Tailplane, rudder, elevator, and trim tab — free movement
- Tie-down ropes and chocks — removed (parking brake on)

Finally, stand back and check the overall appearance. This inspection is critical. Train yourself to notice anything that does not look right, and report it.

### The Internal Inspection

Confirm the brakes are parked. Then check:

- Fuel — ON
- Seat position and harness — set
- Ignition (magnetos) — OFF (engine not live)
- Master switch — ON (for electrical services)
- Flight controls — full and free (set trim to take-off position)
- Engine controls — full and free (throttle, mixture, carburettor heat)
- Instruments — scan systematically for serviceability and correct readings
- Circuit breakers and fuses — none popped or blown
- Microphone — plugged in
- Safety equipment — on board; loose articles — stowed
- Checklists — available if required

Then brief your passengers on their harnesses and the relevant emergency procedures.

## Starting and Stopping the Engine

### Before Start

Confirm the area is safe: a firm surface for taxiing, clear of buildings, fuel, and people, with no fuel spills nearby. Face the aeroplane so the propeller does not blast stones over other aircraft. Follow the POH to prepare the engine.

### The Pre-Start and Starting Check

A typical sequence:

- Brakes — ON
- Unnecessary electrical equipment — OFF
- Fuel — ON
- Carburettor heat — COLD
- Mixture — RICH
- Throttle — CLOSED (or cracked open slightly)
- Primer — LOCKED (after priming, if applicable)
- Rotating beacon — ON (a warning to others)
- Call loudly "Clear propeller!" and confirm the area is clear
- Starter — ENGAGE to crank; release once running and confirm it disengages

### After Starting

- Starter warning light — OUT
- Oil pressure — present within about 30 seconds
- Idle rpm — set (usually 1,000–1,200 rpm) for cooling
- Ammeter — shows the battery recharging
- Vacuum gauge — sufficient suction for the gyro instruments
- Magnetos — check LEFT and RIGHT individually; a small rpm drop is normal; if the engine stops, a fault exists
- Radio — ON, correct frequency, volume and squelch set

A turbocharged engine must idle for a couple of minutes before taxiing, or the turbocharger may be damaged.

### Shutting Down

- Brakes — PARKED, ideally facing any strong wind
- Let the engine cool (idle a minute or two; a turbocharged engine, about two minutes)
- Magnetos — check each individually
- Electrics — OFF
- Mixture — IDLE CUT-OFF, to starve the engine of fuel and stop it

After the engine stops, switch the ignition off and remove the key. Consider turning the fuel cock OFF.

**Dead-cut check (caution):** some operators briefly move the ignition from BOTH to OFF and back, to confirm OFF truly earths the magnetos. If OFF does not break the circuit, the propeller stays "live" and could start the engine if moved — a serious hazard. Some manufacturers advise against this check because it can damage the engine. Follow your instructor's advice.

## Problems During Start-Up

### A Flooded Engine

Too much fuel can flood the engine and strain the battery. If flooding is suspected:

- Ignition — OFF
- Throttle — FULLY OPEN
- Fuel — ON
- Mixture — IDLE CUT-OFF (no fuel supplied)
- Crank several revolutions to clear the intake, then restart without priming.

### Engine Fire on Start-Up

Rare, but possible after over-priming:

- Starter — keep cranking if still engaged
- Mixture — IDLE CUT-OFF
- Fuel selector — OFF
- Throttle — OPEN (to purge fuel from the induction system)

The fire should stop once the fuel is gone. If it continues: ignition OFF, master switch OFF, brakes ON, evacuate with an extinguisher. Do not try to restart.

## Post-Flight Actions

The flight is not complete until the aeroplane is shut down, parked, secure, and the paperwork is done. Each aeroplane passes from one pilot to the next; complete your duties so the next pilot receives a serviceable aeroplane.

### Securing the Aeroplane

- Parking brake — ON; wheel chocks — in place
- A brief external inspection
- Fit pitot covers, control locks, and tie-down ropes if required
- Secure the seat belts
- If this is the last flight of the day, consider refuelling to limit overnight condensation
- Lock the door and return the key

### Post-Flight Documentation

- Book in the flight with ATS if required
- Complete the Flight Authorisation Sheet (time and nature of the flight)
- Report any defects to your instructor or an engineer, and note them on the maintenance document
- Complete your personal logbook

Your instructor will usually debrief you after your post-flight duties.
