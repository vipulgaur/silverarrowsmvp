## Overview

Exercise 10 has two parts: slow flying and stalling. You will learn how the aeroplane handles at low speed, how to recognise an approaching stall, and how to recover with minimum height loss.

## Slow Flying

Slow flight means handling the aeroplane at an abnormally low airspeed, close to the stall. It builds awareness of degraded control and higher power demands before you practise stalls.

### The Region of Reversed Command

At normal speeds, less power means a slower speed. In the slow-flight region, the relationship reverses:

- More drag is needed to fly slowly, so you need **more power** to hold height at a lower speed.
- Here, **power controls height** and **attitude controls airspeed** — the opposite of the cruise feel.

### Degraded Control Feel

At low speed the controls feel soft and sluggish because less airflow passes over them. You need larger, slower, coordinated inputs. The aeroplane is less responsive and closer to the stall, so keep a careful lookout and a safe height.

## Stalling

A **stall** happens when the wing exceeds its critical angle of attack. The airflow separates, lift falls sharply, and drag rises. A stall is about **angle of attack, not speed** — a wing can stall at any speed and any attitude.

### Stall Speeds and Influences

- **VS1** — the clean stall speed.
- **VS0** — the stall speed in the landing configuration (full flap), which is lower.

Stall speed increases with weight, with load factor (turns and pull-ups), with a forward CG, and with wing contamination (ice, frost, snow). Flap lowers the stall speed.

### Symptoms of an Approaching Stall

- Low and decreasing airspeed
- A high nose attitude
- Light, sloppy controls
- Buffet and the stall warner
- A high rate of descent near the stall

### The HASELL Check

Before any stall or aerobatic practice, complete **HASELL**: **H**eight (enough to recover), **A**irframe (flaps/gear as required), **S**ecurity (harnesses tight, no loose articles), **E**ngine (temperatures and pressures, mixture, carburettor heat), **L**ocation (clear of cloud, built-up areas, controlled airspace), **L**ookout (clearing turns).

### Standard Stall Recovery

1. **Reduce the angle of attack** — move the control column centrally forward. This is the primary action.
2. **Apply full power** smoothly, with right rudder for balance (power recovery).
3. **Prevent a wing drop with rudder** — do not pick a dropped wing up with aileron, which can deepen the stall.
4. Ease out of the descent and return to normal flight. Avoid a secondary stall by not pulling up too soon.

An unpowered (glide) recovery uses the same angle-of-attack reduction without power.

## Flying the Exercise

- Enter slow flight by reducing power and holding height as the speed decays; trim; then manoeuvre gently.
- For stalling, complete HASELL, then reduce power and raise the nose to induce the stall, recognise the symptoms, and recover promptly.
- Note the height lost in each recovery and work to reduce it.

## Airmanship

- Keep plenty of height and stay clear of cloud and built-up areas.
- Complete HASELL every time.
- Recover at the first sign of the stall. Keep the aeroplane in balance throughout to avoid a wing drop or incipient spin.
