## Overview

Exercise 7 is the climb. You will learn to enter, hold, and level off from a steady, balanced climb on a constant heading. You will also learn the four forces in a climb, the difference between best angle and best rate, engine cooling in the climb, and the standard cockpit sequences.

## The Four Forces in a Climb

In level flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. In a steady climb along a path at angle θ:

- **Lift is less than weight.** Lift balances only part of the weight: **Lift = Weight × cos θ**.
- **Thrust carries the rest.** Thrust must overcome drag plus the backward part of weight: **Thrust = Drag + Weight × sin θ**.
- The excess of thrust over drag sets the climb: **sin θ = (Thrust − Drag) ÷ Weight**.

The steeper the climb, the more of the weight thrust supports, and the less lift the wings provide.

## Power + Attitude = Performance

Performance comes from power and attitude together:

- **Power** — usually full throttle for the climb.
- **Attitude** — the elevator sets the pitch attitude, which sets the airspeed.
- **Performance** — the resulting airspeed and rate of climb.

![Fig-07-02: Climb power plus attitude](A first-person cockpit view holding a nose-high climb attitude against the horizon, with the tachometer highlighted at full climb power and a positive rate on the VSI. Clear aviation manual art.)

## Slipstream Yaw

At high power and low speed, a single-engine aeroplane yaws left:

- The spiralling slipstream strikes the left of the fin and pushes the tail right, yawing the nose left.
- At a high angle of attack, the descending (right) propeller blade takes a bigger bite (P-factor), adding to the left yaw.
- **Apply steady right rudder** as you add power, to keep the ball centred. Use rudder trim for a long climb if fitted.

## Pitch Controls Airspeed

With climb power set, the elevator controls airspeed:

- Raise the nose → higher angle of attack → slower.
- Lower the nose → lower angle of attack → faster.
- **Do not chase the airspeed.** Set the attitude, hold it, let the speed settle, then make a small correction. Trim off the back-pressure once stable.

## Lookout in the Climb

The nose-high attitude hides the airspace ahead and below behind the cowl. Clear this blind spot:

- Every 500 ft, either lower the nose briefly to look ahead, or make shallow 15°–20° clearing turns left and right.
- Use a landmark on the horizon to return to the climb heading.

![Fig-07-06: The cowl blind spot](A profile of a trainer climbing nose-high, with a sightline over the cowl, the triangular area below the cowl shaded as "obscured", and another aeroplane hidden in that blind zone. Caption: make small clearing turns during a climb. Clean vector diagram.)

## Engine Cooling

Full power plus low airspeed heats the engine:

- Confirm the mixture is **RICH** before applying climb power — the extra fuel cools the cylinders.
- Monitor oil temperature, oil pressure, and cylinder head temperature.
- If temperatures near the red line: lower the nose to gain speed and airflow, reduce power a little, and open cowl flaps if fitted.

## Climb Profiles

- **Best angle (Vx)** — most height per unit of **ground distance** (steepest gradient). Use it to clear obstacles after take-off. Flown at the speed of maximum excess thrust. Lowest speed, highest nose, poorest view, least cooling.
- **Best rate (Vy)** — most height per unit of **time** (greatest ft/min). Use it to reach altitude quickly. Flown at the speed of maximum excess power. Moderate speed and nose attitude.
- **Cruise climb** — a compromise: higher speed, lower nose, better view and cooling, more forward distance. Use it for cross-country departures where terrain clearance is not critical.

Because thrust available falls as speed rises, **Vx is always slower than Vy**.

![Fig-07-07: Vx, Vy, and cruise climb trajectories](An altitude-versus-distance graph with three climb paths — steepest "Vx max angle", "Vy max rate" reaching the greatest height, and a flatter "normal/cruise climb" reaching the greatest distance. Crisp textbook vector art.)

## Flaps in the Climb-Out

- **Take-off flap (about 10°–15°)** lowers the stall speed, shortens the ground run, and steepens the initial climb for obstacle clearance.
- **Full flap** adds large drag for little extra lift — climb performance drops sharply, and the aeroplane may not climb.
- **Retract flap** only when clear of obstacles (about 200–300 ft AGL) and safely above the clean stall speed. Hold the climb attitude with back-pressure as the flaps come up, then re-trim.

## Weight and Density Altitude

- A **lighter** aeroplane needs less lift and makes less induced drag, so it has more excess power — a better rate and angle of climb.
- As **density altitude** rises (lower pressure, higher temperature), air density falls, so engine power and propeller efficiency drop. Climb performance decreases with altitude until, at the **absolute ceiling**, the rate of climb reaches zero.

## Flying the Climb

**Enter (P-A-T):** check traffic; **P** — mixture RICH, full power, right rudder for balance; **A** — raise the nose to the climb attitude and let the speed settle; **T** — trim off the pressures.

**Maintain (L-A-I):** **L** — lookout, clear the blind spot every 500 ft; **A** — wings level, ball centred, airspeed with elevator; **I** — cross-check ASI, altimeter, VSI, and the engine gauges.

**Level off (A-P-T):** lead the level-off by about 10% of the climb rate (e.g. 600 ft/min → start 60 ft early). **A** — lower the nose to the cruise attitude and let the speed build (leave climb power on); **P** — at cruise speed set cruise power and relax the right rudder; **T** — trim. Then do the cruise checks (lean the mixture per the POH, carburettor heat as required, cowl flaps closed, engine temperatures and pressures normal).

## Airmanship

- Set the correct QNH. Never climb into controlled airspace without a clearance, or into cloud unless properly rated, current, and cleared.
- Keep the required distance from cloud (VMC).
- Use smooth control inputs. Lead the level-off so you do not overshoot the altitude.
