## Overview

Exercise 3 is your first flight. It is not formal instruction. It lets you feel what flight is like and start building good habits. Your instructor flies and demonstrates the common sensations of flight.

## In the Cockpit

Sit comfortably and relax. Use the left-hand seat — the captain's seat. Position it so you can reach all the controls with your hands and feet. Make the harness firm.

Correct seat position matters even now. It fixes where the natural horizon sits in the windscreen. The visual pilot uses that horizon to fly accurately.

Direct fresh air from the vents for comfort. Confirm the headset is comfortable and the intercom works. Clear cockpit communication is important.

## Leaving the Ground

Help keep a good lookout while taxiing. During the take-off roll, look well ahead into the "middle distance". This keeps you straight and helps you judge height. Build good habits from the start.

As the aeroplane climbs, the ground looks different — more like a map than a profile. Towns, roads, rivers, and coastlines appear in plan. The sense of speed reduces, and the aeroplane seems to fly in slow motion.

## Following on the Controls

You may "follow through" on the controls with your instructor:

- Left hand lightly on the control wheel (or column)
- Right hand on the throttle or in your lap
- Both feet lightly on the rudder pedals, heels on the floor so the toe brakes are not applied

## Transfer of Control

Control passes between you and your instructor often. Use a clear two-part handover every time:

1. The person giving control says **"You have control."**
2. The person taking control says **"I have control."**

Never assume. Always state and respond.

![Fig-03-02: Who has control](a forward view inside a two-seat trainer, with two speech bubbles — "1. You have control" from the right seat and "2. I have control" from the left seat. Clean vector instructional graphic.)

## Keep a Good Lookout

Visual flying needs high awareness outside the cockpit. Use your eyes to:

- Relate the nose attitude to the natural horizon
- Look for other aircraft
- Check your passage over the ground
- Stay clear of cloud

![Fig-03-03: The view from the cockpit](two cockpit comparison views through the windscreen — left, straight-and-level with a flat level horizon; right, a banked turn with the horizon tilted. Clear instructional line art.)

### How to Scan

The eyes see best when still. Scan in short, regular steps:

- Move the eyes about 10° at a time.
- Hold each area for about one second.
- Then move to the next area.

![Fig-03-04: Scanning for traffic](a cockpit view through the windscreen with a dashed zig-zag scan pattern and target nodes across the sky, representing 10-degree steps and pause points. Technical training diagram.)

Each aeroplane has a blind spot under the nose. Clear it periodically with shallow turns so you can see past it through the side window. Learn your aeroplane's blind spots and clear them regularly.

### The Clock Code

Describe other traffic with the clock code, aligned to your heading:

- 12 o'clock is straight ahead; 3 o'clock is off the right wingtip; 6 o'clock is behind; 9 o'clock is off the left wingtip.
- Add height: "12 o'clock high" is ahead and above. "Left, 8 o'clock low" is behind, left, and below.

![Fig-03-05: The clock code](a top-down view of a light low-wing trainer centred in a clock face, with 12 off the nose, 3 off the right wingtip, 6 off the tail, 9 off the left wingtip, and hours 1–12 marked. Clean aviation diagram.)

Note landmarks that help you return to the aerodrome. Stay well clear of cloud at all times.

## The Instruments

The instruments give altitude, direction, airspeed, and engine information. They divide into flight instruments and engine instruments. A visual pilot needs only an occasional one-to-two-second glance. Your awareness of the world outside must not suffer.

## Landing

Workload rises through the descent, approach, and landing. Look into the "middle distance" again to judge the flare and touchdown. The landing feels busy at first, but you will soon master it.

The flight is not complete until the aeroplane is parked and secured and your post-flight duties are done. Prepare for your next lesson on the ground by reading the relevant exercise in advance.
