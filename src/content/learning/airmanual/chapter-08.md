## Overview

Exercise 8 is descending. It has four parts: the glide (no power), the powered descent, the use of flap in the descent, and the sideslip. You will learn how each one controls the descent path and rate while keeping a safe, constant airspeed.

## The Glide

With the throttle at idle, thrust is removed. Drag is now unbalanced. If you hold a level attitude, the aeroplane slows toward a stall. So you lower the nose to a descending path and let a forward component of weight replace thrust.

Three forces act in a steady glide:

- **Lift = Weight × cos γ** (lift is less than weight in a glide).
- **Drag = Weight × sin γ** (the forward part of weight balances drag).
- Combining them: **tan γ = 1 ÷ (L/D)**.

**Key law:** the glide angle depends only on the **lift-to-drag (L/D) ratio** — not on weight. A higher L/D gives a shallower glide and more range.

Still-air glide distance: **Range = Height × (L/D)**. At 10:1, you travel about 10,000 ft for every 1,000 ft lost.

Fly the **best glide speed** for maximum range:

- Too fast → more parasite drag → steeper glide, less range.
- Too slow → more induced drag → steeper glide, less range.

A lighter aeroplane glides the same distance (same angle) but at a lower speed. Wind changes the distance over the ground: a headwind shortens the glide, a tailwind extends it.

## The Powered Descent

In a powered descent, you decouple the two controls:

- **Power (throttle)** controls the rate of descent and the path.
- **Attitude (elevator)** controls the airspeed.

Set the attitude for the target speed; adjust power to set the rate of descent. A standard approach is about 3°. Add power to reduce the rate (shallower); reduce power to increase it (steeper). Re-trim after each change.

### The Aiming Point

On the approach, the point that stays still in the windscreen is your aiming point. Ground that appears to move **down and away** is ahead of where you will land; ground that moves **up toward you** is behind it. If the aiming point drifts down the windscreen, you will undershoot — add power. If it drifts up, you will overshoot — reduce power.

## Use of Flap in the Descent

Flap lets you descend more steeply **without** flying faster:

- Flap adds drag, lowering L/D and steepening the path at the same airspeed.
- The nose sits lower, improving the forward view over the cowl.
- Flap lowers the stall speed, improving the approach safety margin.

Respect the flap limiting speed (**VFE**). Only extend flap below VFE. Lower flap in stages, re-trimming each time. Expect a pitch change as flap extends or retracts; anticipate it. For a go-around, apply power, arrest the sink, then retract flap in stages while holding the climb attitude.

## The Sideslip

A sideslip is a deliberate out-of-balance condition used to **steepen the descent without flap and without gaining speed**:

- Cross the controls: bank one way with aileron, hold opposite rudder to stop the turn.
- The fuselage presents its side to the airflow, adding drag and increasing the rate of descent at a constant airspeed.

Cautions:

- The airspeed indicator can read inaccurately in a sideslip (the pitot and static are in disturbed airflow). Judge attitude visually.
- Observe airframe limits. Some aircraft restrict sideslipping with full flap — check the POH.
- Recover by levelling the wings and centring the rudder together, smoothly.

The sideslip is useful on a flapless forced landing when you are too high and need to lose height on short final.

## Flying the Descent

**Enter (P-A-T):** **P** — set the descent power (idle for a glide); **A** — lower the nose to the attitude for the target speed and let it settle; **T** — trim. Apply carburettor heat before closing the throttle, and warm the engine periodically in a long glide.

**Maintain (L-A-I):** **L** — lookout, clear the area ahead and below; **A** — wings level, ball centred, airspeed with elevator (rate/path with power in a powered descent); **I** — cross-check ASI, altimeter, and VSI.

**Level off:** lead the level-off; raise the nose to the level attitude and add power to the cruise setting, then re-trim.

## Airmanship

- In a glide, keep the engine warm and clear it periodically; use carburettor heat per the POH.
- Keep the required distance from cloud and a good lookout, especially below the nose.
- Make smooth inputs. Keep the approach stable and the airspeed under control at all times.
