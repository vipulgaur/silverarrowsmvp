## Overview

Exercise 6 is flying straight and level: a constant heading, a constant altitude, and in balance, at a constant power setting. This is the foundation of all other flight.

- **Straight** — hold an unvarying heading and a straight ground track. Keep the wings level with ailerons; prevent yaw with rudder.
- **Level** — hold a constant altitude. Match the pitch attitude to the power set. The altimeter is the primary reference, supported by the vertical speed indicator (VSI).
- **Balanced** — no slip or skid. Keep the balance ball centred with rudder pressure.

## The Four Forces

In steady, unaccelerated straight-and-level flight, the aeroplane is in equilibrium:

- **Lift = Weight**
- **Thrust = Drag**

![Fig-06-01: The four forces](a side view of a high-wing trainer in level flight with four vectors — lift up from the wing, weight down from the CG, thrust forward along the thrust line, drag rearward — plus a small downward force at the tailplane. Clean technical vector diagram.)

### Balancing Couples

The four forces do not act through one point, so the aeroplane uses balancing moments:

- **Pitching couple** — the centre of pressure (CP) is usually behind the centre of gravity (CG). This gives a nose-down couple.
- **Thrust/drag couple** — the thrust line is usually below the drag line. This gives a nose-up couple in powered flight.
- **Tailplane load** — the tailplane is rigged to make a small **downward** force that balances the remaining moment.

**Built-in safety:** if power is lost, the nose-up thrust/drag couple fades and the slipstream over the tail weakens, so the nose-down couple pitches the nose down automatically into a glide — preserving flying speed with no immediate pilot action. On a T-tail, the stabiliser sits clear of the slipstream, so power changes affect pitch trim less.

![Fig-06-02: The tailplane balancing force](a cross-section of a trainer showing CG ahead of CP [a nose-down couple] balanced by a downward force at the tailplane so moments sum to zero. Detailed schematic.)

## Stability

Stability is the aeroplane's built-in tendency to return to its trimmed attitude after a disturbance, with no pilot input.

### Longitudinal Stability (Pitch)

A gust pitches the nose up; the tailplane meets the airflow at a higher angle of attack and makes a restoring force that lowers the nose back. It works like the fins on a dart.

![Fig-06-03: Longitudinal stability after a nose-up gust](a multi-panel strip — level flight, a gust pitches the nose up, the tailplane angle of attack rises, a restoring force lowers the nose, oscillations damp, the aeroplane returns to the trimmed attitude. Instructional graphic.)

**Centre of gravity effects on pitch:**

- **Forward CG** — more stable, heavier elevator forces, less elevator authority at low speed (risk of running out of up-elevator in the flare), but easier stall recovery.
- **Aft CG** — less stable, very light and sensitive controls (easy to over-control), a strong nose-up tendency, and dangerous or impossible stall recovery. Always load within limits.

![Fig-06-04: Loading affects stability](two side profiles — top, a forward CG with a long moment arm "a" and strong restoring force; bottom, an aft CG with a short arm "b" and weak restoring force. Engineering comparison.)

### Directional Stability (Yaw)

The fin provides it. After a yaw, the fin meets the airflow at an angle and makes a side force that yaws the nose back into the relative wind.

### Lateral Stability (Roll)

Two features restore the wings to level after a wing drop:

- **High keel surfaces** — the fin and upper fuselage sit above the CG; in a sideslip, airflow on them helps roll the wings level.
- **Wing dihedral** — wings angled up toward the tips; the lower wing meets the airflow at a higher angle of attack and makes more lift, rolling the wings level.

**Caution:** roll and yaw are coupled. An uncorrected wing drop causes a sideslip, then a yaw toward the low wing, then an accelerating spiral dive if left alone. Level the wings early.

![Fig-06-05: Keel surfaces and dihedral](two front views — left, the side airflow on the high fin and fuselage above the CG; right, a banked aeroplane with dihedral where the lower wing meets the airflow at a higher angle of attack for restoring lift. Technical vector diagram.)

## Lift and Angle of Attack

Air accelerates over the cambered upper surface, lowering its pressure relative to the underside (Bernoulli). This makes lift, perpendicular to the flightpath, plus induced drag from wingtip vortices.

Lift depends on the coefficient of lift (set by the aerofoil and the angle of attack), air density, the square of the true airspeed, and the wing area:

**Lift = C_L · ½ρV² · S**

In level flight, lift balances weight, so:

- **At high speed** — dynamic pressure is high, so you need a **low** angle of attack and a **low** nose attitude.
- **At low speed** — dynamic pressure is low, so you need a **high** angle of attack and a **high** nose attitude.

![Fig-06-06: Lift depends on angle of attack](two identical aircraft in level flight — left, fast at a low angle of attack and level nose; right, slow at a high angle of attack and raised nose — with equal lift and weight arrows on both. Clean aeronautical comparison.)

## Flying Straight and Level

- Set the cruise power. Set the pitch attitude against the horizon for level flight. Confirm with the altimeter and VSI.
- Keep the wings level with small aileron inputs. Keep a straight track against a distant reference.
- Keep the ball centred with rudder pressure.
- Trim off the control pressures so the aeroplane holds the attitude hands-off. Re-trim after any power change.

## Airmanship

- Look outside most of the time: set attitude against the horizon, hold a straight track, and watch for traffic.
- Make small, smooth corrections. Lead with attitude, confirm with the instruments.
- Keep the aeroplane in balance, and keep it trimmed.
