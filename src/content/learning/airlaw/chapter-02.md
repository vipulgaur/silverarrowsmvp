# Chapter 2: International Agreements and Organizations

## The Chicago Convention

### Development of international civil aviation

Civil aviation progressed from the first powered flights to scheduled passenger services remarkably quickly. Regular commercial air services were operating by **1919**, the same year that an international conference on air navigation was held in **Paris**. Unlike most forms of transport, aviation frequently crosses political boundaries; consequently, international cooperation and consistent safety standards became essential from an early stage.

The Second World War accelerated aircraft development and demonstrated how effectively air transport could move people and supplies over long distances. As the war approached its end, governments anticipated substantial growth in civilian passenger and freight networks. They also recognized unresolved problems: whether foreign airlines could cross national airspace, the commercial rights needed to serve other countries, potential legal and economic disputes, and the financing and maintenance of navigation facilities in remote regions.

The **United States** began discussions with allied governments during the early months of **1944** and invited **55 States** to an aviation conference in **Chicago in November 1944**. Delegations from **52 States** attended. Following five weeks of discussion, they agreed on the **Convention on International Civil Aviation**, intended to establish the conditions for safe, orderly and economically viable international flying while encouraging cooperation among nations.

The organization responsible for administering the Convention's principles became the **International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO)**.

### Main principles of the Chicago Convention

The Convention contains **96 Articles** and establishes fundamental rules for relations between States in civil aviation. Its central principle is that **each State exercises complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory**. Accordingly, a scheduled international air service cannot enter or cross another Contracting State's territory without that State's prior permission.

It also provides a framework for rights and responsibilities among States and for the development of **International Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs)** dealing with:

* The regulation and safety of air navigation.
* The provision of navigation facilities by Contracting States.
* The facilitation of international air transport, including reducing unnecessary customs and immigration formalities.

## International Law, Territory and Sovereignty

### How international agreements become enforceable

No single world legislature passes laws directly for every sovereign nation. Instead, governments meet at international conferences, negotiate common rules and record them in conventions or agreements. These arrangements are translated into enforceable obligations when the States concerned **adopt and ratify** them through their own constitutional and legislative processes.

In this way, an international undertaking becomes part of national law. Associated articles, protocols, appendices and amendments can also be incorporated into domestic legal arrangements. Enforcement and punishment are then undertaken through the relevant State's legal system rather than by a universal police authority.

### Territorial airspace

A State's national aviation law applies within the territory over which it has jurisdiction. The lateral boundaries of its airspace follow its land boundaries and, for coastal States, the applicable extent of territorial waters. The chapter treats the State's territorial airspace as having no specified vertical limit.

A landlocked State such as **Switzerland** has airspace boundaries linked principally to its land frontiers. For a coastal State such as the **United Kingdom**, part of the lateral boundary is associated with territorial waters. The **1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea and the Contiguous Zone** addressed the international definition of those maritime limits.

### Airspace over the high seas

The **1958 Geneva Convention on the High Seas** established the principle of freedom of use of waters lying beyond territorial seas. Similar freedoms apply to civil aviation over these areas. The maritime idea of a **flag State**, meaning the country in which a vessel is registered, has an aviation counterpart in the **State of Registry** of an aircraft.

International cooperation also enables States without coastlines to enjoy access to maritime routes and allows aircraft of Contracting States to use the airspace privileges established through the Chicago framework. The **1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea** developed and reinforced earlier arrangements.

**Territory** refers, in this aviation context, to the area over which a State's territorial airspace extends.

**Sovereignty** is a State's authority to impose and enforce its laws within that airspace.

**Suzerainty**, as described in the Convention terminology used here, refers to acceptance of commonly agreed international rules even where every particular rule does not have a practical domestic application.

## Commercial Rights and International Air Services

### Why bilateral agreements were necessary

The Chicago conference could not obtain unanimous agreement on the commercial concessions that airlines should receive in other countries. As a result, States negotiated rights individually through **bilateral agreements**. These arrangements formed the basis of the **Freedoms of the Air**.

Two supplementary agreements were particularly important:

* **International Air Services Transit Agreement:** allows overflight of another participating State or landing there for a technical purpose.
* **International Air Transport Agreement:** addresses the carriage of passengers, mail and cargo between a State of Registration and another participating State.

### Scheduled and non-scheduled services

A **scheduled flight** operates as part of an international service agreed at government level. The arrangement can specify how frequently flights may take place, which airports may be used, the operating times and the reciprocal privileges to be exchanged. A State is not automatically obliged to authorize a proposed scheduled service.

A **non-scheduled flight** is not operated under such an agreed recurring schedule. Examples include individual charter services and one-off flights. The chapter distinguishes the treatment of these operations from scheduled traffic rights: economic or political refusal of a non-scheduled operation is restricted under the relevant aviation freedoms.

### Cabotage

**Cabotage** is the transport of passengers or goods between two locations in one State by an aircraft or vessel registered in another State. Many countries do not permit foreign operators to exercise this right. Within the European Union arrangement described here, one Member State may not deny another Member State cabotage solely on that basis.

## Customs, Excise and Immigration

States retain the authority to set customs duties, regulate imports and prohibit specified goods. International flights are therefore generally required to make their first landing in a country at an **international airport** with the appropriate customs, immigration and health facilities. Such airports are also referred to as **customs airports**.

Under the intra-EU arrangements described in this chapter, a flight between Member States may in some circumstances land first at an aerodrome that does not provide customs facilities, provided the applicable conditions are satisfied. Immigration requirements remain a separate consideration.

## Obligations of ICAO Contracting States

### Applying international standards

An ICAO Contracting State undertakes to implement the international aviation framework within its own territory through national legislation and regulation. The safety-related provisions of the **Rules of the Air** are contained in **Annex 2**; other requirements support the orderly and efficient movement of aircraft and traffic.

**Article 38** of the Chicago Convention requires a State that cannot follow an ICAO Standard to **notify ICAO of the difference** between that Standard and its national rules. The State must also publish the relevant differences in its **Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP)**. Within a State's sovereign airspace, applicable national provisions take precedence; over the high seas, the international Rules of the Air apply as described in this chapter.

### Jurisdiction over offences

If an offence takes place aboard an aircraft within a State's airspace, that State may exercise jurisdiction. For an offence committed over the high seas, jurisdiction may be exercised by the **State of Registry**. International conventions can also provide other grounds for jurisdiction and prosecution.

An example involving a bomb carried aboard a US-registered aircraft, with connections to Rome, Frankfurt, Libya and an explosion over Scotland, illustrates possible prosecution interests in the following order:

1. **United Kingdom:** the aircraft was destroyed over Scotland.
2. **United States:** the aircraft was registered there.
3. **Italy:** the device was alleged to have been placed aboard in Rome.
4. **Germany:** the aircraft made an intermediate stop at Frankfurt.
5. **Other affected States:** their citizens were killed or injured.
6. **Libya:** the suspects were Libyan nationals.

The illustration also makes the point that other eligible States may prosecute if the State with the strongest territorial connection does not do so.

### Search and rescue responsibilities

Each Contracting State must arrange **search and rescue (SAR)** services for its territory. Responsibility for high-seas areas or areas without established sovereignty is allocated through **Regional Air Navigation (RAN) agreements**.

The minimum SAR capability must suit the nature and volume of traffic for which the State is responsible. It must be available **24 hours a day**, supported by coordination with neighbouring States and readiness to assist their rescue operations when requested.

### Documents required aboard international flights

Aircraft engaged in international navigation must carry the relevant documents:

1. Certificate of Registration.
2. Certificate of Airworthiness.
3. Licences of flight crew members.
4. Journey logbook.
5. Aircraft radio station licence, when the aircraft carries a radio station.
6. Passenger list, when passengers are carried, identifying names and points of embarkation and destination.
7. Cargo manifest and detailed cargo declaration, when cargo is carried.

No **munitions of war** may be carried into or over a State without its permission. A Contracting State may also restrict or regulate photographic equipment used aboard aircraft over its territory.

### ICAO Standards, Recommended Practices and national differences

ICAO develops SARPs through consultation with Contracting States and relevant international bodies. The **Air Navigation Commission** completes the technical work and passes the proposals to the **ICAO Council**; a **two-thirds majority** of the Council is required for adoption. In the framework described by the chapter, the Convention has **18 Annexes** containing SARPs.

Differences notified under **Article 38** are made available through national AIPs and included in supplements associated with the relevant Annex.

### Customs and taxation arrangements

ICAO encourages States to adopt compatible customs and taxation policies. In particular, States are asked to:

* Exempt fuel, lubricants and certain technical supplies taken aboard abroad when intended for use during the flight.
* Reduce or remove taxes on international air fares.
* Grant reciprocal relief from income and profit taxation to international air transport operators of other States.
* Facilitate the prompt clearance of imported and transit cargo under **Annex 9 — Facilitation**, including the use of **free zones** where appropriate.

### Standardization of aircraft and personnel documentation

**Annex 7** addresses aircraft nationality and registration marks, their format, size, positioning and registration procedures; it also supplies a model registration certificate.

**Annex 8** concerns airworthiness and provides for the issue of a **Certificate of Airworthiness** indicating that the registered aircraft meets the necessary conditions to fly.

**Annex 1** establishes common requirements for licensing aviation personnel, including pilots, flight engineers, air traffic controllers and maintenance personnel. A licence issued in one State is **not automatically valid in another**: appropriate validation procedures and endorsements are required.

### Dangerous goods and inspection powers

**Annex 18 — The Safe Transport of Dangerous Goods by Air** establishes the framework for carrying dangerous goods and implementing the associated **Technical Instructions**. The chapter notes that dangerous goods represent a substantial share of transport cargo and that aviation's speed makes air carriage particularly relevant.

Contracting States may search a foreign Contracting State's aircraft when it arrives or departs, provided this does not result in unreasonable delay. They may also inspect the prescribed certificates and other documents.

## Contents and Status of ICAO Annexes

An ICAO Annex may contain different types of provisions, and not all categories have identical status.

* **Standard:** a requirement relating to matters such as physical characteristics, configuration, materials, performance, personnel or procedures whose consistent application is considered **necessary** for safe or regular international air navigation. A State unable to comply must notify the Council under Article 38.
* **Recommended Practice:** a comparable specification whose uniform application is considered **desirable** for safety, regularity or efficiency. States endeavour to comply.

## The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO)

### Status and purpose

ICAO is an **intergovernmental organization** created by the Chicago Convention and associated with the **United Nations as a specialized agency**. Its headquarters are in **Montreal**. It provides a means for States to coordinate the technical, legal and economic framework of international civil aviation.

### ICAO aims and objectives

ICAO's responsibilities include developing methods and principles for air navigation and encouraging international air transport that is safe, orderly and economically sustainable. Its aims encompass:

* Safe and systematic worldwide growth of civil aviation.
* Progress in aircraft design and operational techniques.
* Expansion of airways, airports and air navigation services.
* Safe, regular, efficient and economical air transport.
* Avoidance of wasteful or unreasonable competition.
* Protection of Contracting States' rights.
* Equal treatment of States without discrimination.
* Improved flight safety.
* Advancement of all aspects of international civil aeronautics.

### The Assembly, Council and technical bodies

**Assembly:** the sovereign body, convened by the Council **at least once every three years**. Every Contracting State has **one vote**, and Assembly decisions are normally reached by majority vote.

**Council:** the permanent governing body answerable to the Assembly. In the structure described here, it consists of **33 Contracting States**, chosen by the Assembly for **three-year terms**.

**Commissions and committees:** populated through Council appointments based on State nominations or selection from Council membership. The bodies named are:

* Air Navigation Commission.
* Air Transport Committee.
* Legal Committee.
* Committee on Joint Support of Air Navigation Services.
* Personnel Committee.
* Finance Committee.
* Committee on Unlawful Interference.

The **Air Navigation Commission** prepares and finalizes the SARPs for consideration and adoption by the Council.

**Secretariat:** headed by a **Secretary-General** appointed by the Council. Its role is to provide the Council with administrative and specialist assistance. The five principal divisions described are the **Air Navigation Bureau**, **Air Transport Bureau**, **Technical Assistance Bureau**, **Legal Bureau**, and **Bureau of Administration and Services**.

A regional approach helps States coordinate facilities, services, air route development, procedures for areas with increasing traffic density, and the introduction of new types of aircraft.

### Regional Air Navigation meetings and assistance

**Regional Air Navigation (RAN) meetings** evaluate operational requirements in particular geographic areas. Their output is a regional plan intended to produce a coordinated system when implemented by the States involved. Regional offices monitor progress and keep these plans up to date.

Financial assistance can be arranged where the cost of supporting international traffic is exceptionally high in relation to a State's resources. **Greenland and Iceland** are given as examples because of the air traffic services, navigation aids and meteorological facilities required over their areas.

### Procedures for Air Navigation Services (PANS)

**PANS** documents give practical detail for subjects that require more explanation than the SARPs alone. The distinction in this framework is that the Council **approves PANS** but **adopts SARPs**.

**PANS-OPS — Doc 8168** deals with aircraft operations. **Volume I** covers operational procedures intended for flight operations personnel; **Volume II** covers obstacle-clearance criteria and associated specialist work needed to develop instrument approach charts.

**PANS-ATM — Doc 4444** covers air traffic management and requirements associated with establishing an air traffic control service.

**PANS-RAC** concerns rules of the air and air traffic services, including pilot procedures, flight information, alerting services and ATC operations.

**Regional Supplementary Procedures — Doc 7030/4:** where worldwide procedures are unsuitable for a particular geographic region, a RAN meeting may recommend regional variations. The relevant **SUPPS** are recorded in Doc 7030/4 and approved by the Council for use in that region.

## International Air Agreements and the Freedoms of the Air

### Agreements governing overflight and traffic carriage

The **International Air Services Transit Agreement** sets out two technical freedoms, while the **International Air Transport Agreement** covers three commercial freedoms. Because these arrangements depend on State participation and bilateral understandings, ICAO established the **Chicago Standard Form for Bilateral Agreement for Regular Air Transport**.

For general aviation and non-scheduled commercial operations, an **international flight plan** provides notification of an intended flight exercising the relevant privileges.

### The five freedoms

|Freedom|Type|Meaning|
|-|-|-|
|**First**|Technical|Cross a foreign participating State's territory **without landing**.|
|**Second**|Technical|Land in a foreign participating State **only for a non-traffic purpose**, such as refuelling or repairs, without taking on or setting down commercial traffic.|
|**Third**|Commercial|Set down passengers, mail or cargo in another State after taking that traffic aboard in the airline's home State.|
|**Fourth**|Commercial|Pick up passengers, mail or cargo in a foreign State for transport back to the airline's home State.|
|**Fifth**|Commercial|On a service originating from or returning to its home State, carry traffic between two other States.|

For the commercial freedoms, the chapter illustrates third freedom by a UK operator carrying traffic to the **USA**, fourth freedom by taking traffic in the USA back to the **UK**, and fifth freedom by a UK operator carrying traffic from **Greece** to **Italy** as part of a service linking back to the UK.

Further commercial freedoms emerged as airline networks became more complex, but they are not examined in this chapter's learning objectives.

### The Bermuda Agreement

The first major bilateral air transport agreement used as a model by other States was the **1946 Bermuda Agreement between the United Kingdom and the United States**. The chapter describes approximately **3,000 air transport agreements** as operating worldwide at the time of its account.

## International Conventions Concerning Unlawful Acts

The international response to hijacking, violence aboard aircraft, sabotage and other threats to civil aviation led to the **Tokyo, Hague and Montreal Conventions** and supplementary protocols.

### Tokyo Convention, 1963

The **Convention on Offences and Certain Other Acts Committed on Board Aircraft** establishes the competence of the **State of Registry** to exercise jurisdiction over offences committed aboard an aircraft. It addresses acts endangering the aircraft, people, property, order or discipline and seeks to prevent offenders escaping legal consequences simply because an offence occurred in flight.

The aircraft commander and other authorized persons may take measures to prevent or stop such conduct and arrange delivery of offenders to competent authorities. States also agree to help restore and preserve control of an aircraft unlawfully seized or threatened with seizure.

### Hague Convention, 1970

The **Convention for the Suppression of Unlawful Seizure of Aircraft** defines aircraft hijacking as an offence, requires severe penalties and establishes provisions for jurisdiction, custody, prosecution and extradition. It followed a rise in politically motivated hijackings in the 1960s. Signed at **The Hague in December 1970**, it entered into force on **14 October 1971**.

### Montreal Convention, 1971

The **Convention for the Suppression of Unlawful Acts Against the Safety of Civil Aviation** deals principally with unlawful attacks other than the seizure of an aircraft. It covers attempted offences and complicity, with provisions for severe punishment and international cooperation over jurisdiction, custody, prosecution and extradition. It came into force on **26 January 1973**.

Relevant unlawful acts include:

* Violence aboard an aircraft that endangers persons, property or flight safety.
* Destroying an aircraft in service, or damaging it sufficiently to make it unable to fly or unsafe in flight.
* Placing on an aircraft a device intended or likely to damage, destroy or disable it.
* Damaging or destroying an air navigation facility, or interfering with its operation.
* Knowingly providing false information that puts an aircraft in flight at risk.

### Montreal supplementary protocol, 1988

The **1988 protocol** extended the categories of offences to attacks involving airports serving international civil aviation. It addresses intentional unlawful violence with devices, weapons or other substances that causes or is likely to cause serious injury or death, together with the destruction of airport facilities, damage to aircraft not in service, and disruption of airport operations. States undertake to apply substantial penalties and establish jurisdiction over these offences.

### Annex 17 and aviation security responsibilities

**Annex 17** provides the SARPs concerned with safeguarding civil aviation against unlawful interference. Contracting States are required to:

* Establish national civil aviation security programmes.
* Designate the authority responsible for aviation security.
* Continually assess the threat level.
* Coordinate with other national agencies and corresponding authorities overseas.
* Provide security training, airport security committees and contingency arrangements.
* Cooperate internationally in security research, development, equipment and methods.

### Powers of the aircraft commander

When the commander reasonably believes that a person has committed, or is about to commit, an act threatening flight safety, people, property or good order aboard the aircraft, the commander may impose **reasonable measures, including restraint**, to:

1. Protect the aircraft and those aboard.
2. Preserve order and discipline.
3. Arrange disembarkation or delivery of the person to the appropriate authority.

The commander may **order or authorize crew members** to assist and may **request or authorize passengers** to help. Passengers may **not be ordered** to participate in restraint.

## The Warsaw Convention and Carrier Liability

### Scope of the Warsaw Convention, 1929

The **Warsaw Convention of 1929** established a common legal framework for international carriage by air. It addresses contractual responsibility and limits of liability involving passengers, baggage, freight, injuries, deaths and delay. The historical discussion refers to an original limit broadly equivalent to **US$10,000** and to the **1955 Hague Protocol**, which raised the prevailing limits. It also refers to a subsequent maximum of about **US$100,000 per person**, subject to the applicable instrument and circumstances.

Compensation limits are expressed using **International Monetary Fund Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)**. Some airlines may agree to compensation levels beyond convention limits, including sums awarded under separate legal proceedings.

### Tickets and contracts of carriage

A passenger ticket, baggage ticket or cargo consignment note provides documentary evidence of the agreement between the carrier and the customer. The contract remains valid even if the ticket or consignment document is missing or defective. However, carrying passengers, baggage or cargo without the prescribed document can prevent a carrier from relying upon certain liability limitations. Where electronic ticketing is used, the operator must bring any applicable liability limits to the passenger's attention.

### Delay and carrier responsibility

Damage arising from delay in the transport of passengers, baggage or cargo may give rise to carrier liability. The carrier may avoid liability if it establishes that the carrier, its employees and agents took every reasonable step to prevent the damage, or that taking such steps was impossible.

### Limits involving delay, baggage and cargo

The figures identified in the chapter's discussion are:

|Category|Limit stated|
|-|-|
|Damage to a passenger caused by delay|**4,150 SDR per passenger**|
|Loss, destruction, damage or delay of baggage|**1,000 SDR per passenger**|
|Loss, destruction, damage or delay of cargo|**17 SDR per kilogram**|

For checked baggage or cargo, the passenger or consignor may declare a higher **special interest in delivery at destination** and, where necessary, pay a supplementary charge. The carrier may then be liable up to the declared amount unless it can establish that this exceeds the actual interest in delivery.

Where only part of a consignment is affected, the weight of the relevant package or packages is used in calculating the limit. If the loss or damage also reduces the value of other packages covered by the same airway bill or equivalent record, their weights may also be considered.

The normal limits do not protect a carrier where damage resulted from an intentional act or from reckless conduct undertaken with knowledge that damage would probably occur, subject to the requirements concerning acts by servants or agents during employment.

A court may award costs of proceedings and interest under the applicable national law. The availability of these additional costs is affected by a carrier's written settlement offer made within the prescribed **six-month period** or before proceedings commence, if later.

### Death or injury of passengers

Under the account of the Warsaw framework as amended by the **1999 Montreal Convention**, a carrier cannot exclude or limit liability for covered passenger death or injury damages up to **100,000 SDR per passenger**.

For damages above that amount, the carrier may avoid liability for the excess by proving that the damage was **not caused by its negligence or other wrongful act or omission**, including those of its staff or agents; or that the damage was **solely due to wrongful conduct of a third party**.

## The Rome Convention

The **Rome Convention of 1952** concerns harm inflicted on people or property on the ground by a **foreign aircraft**. It provides a basis for a claimant to seek redress against a foreign operator through a court in the claimant's country of residence and for the resulting judgment to be enforceable in the operator's State.

### Joint Aviation Authorities (JAA)

The **Joint Aviation Authorities** were associated with **ECAC**, bringing together aviation regulatory authorities from European States to develop compatible safety requirements and procedures. The **JAA Arrangements**, signed in **Cyprus in 1990**, established the cooperative framework.

A Secretariat assisted the Board and Committee.

## Geneva Convention — Rights in Aircraft

The **Convention on the International Recognition of Rights in Aircraft**, identified in the chapter as the **Geneva 1947** Convention, addresses the recognition of financial interests in aircraft, including a seller's or lender's security over an aircraft sold with a loan, lease or mortgage arrangement.

Its provisions include:

* Prohibition of double aircraft registration.
* Inclusion of the registration authority's address on the registration certificate.
* Provisions on aircraft salvage.
* Notification of interested parties before transfer from one aircraft register to another.
* Preservation of a State's authority to apply immigration, customs and air navigation legislation.

## EU Regulation 261/2004 — Passenger Rights

### Coverage and eligibility

The Regulation provides passenger protections for flights **departing from an EU Member State** and, under the conditions stated, for passengers travelling **from outside the EU to an EU Member State on an EU-based airline**.

To qualify, a passenger normally must hold a confirmed reservation and arrive for check-in at the notified time. Where no check-in time has been specified, the minimum stated is **45 minutes before scheduled departure**. The rules also cover passengers transferred to another flight by an airline.

Passengers using free or specially discounted tickets **not available to the general public** are excluded, but tickets issued through **frequent-flyer programmes** remain within the scope described.

### Involuntary denied boarding

Before refusing boarding to passengers who have reservations, the airline must ask for **volunteers** willing to surrender their places in exchange for negotiated benefits. Volunteers remain entitled to the prescribed choice of refund or re-routing.

If too few volunteers come forward, boarding may be denied involuntarily. Affected passengers must then receive the compensation and assistance applicable to denied boarding.

### Cancellation

When a flight is cancelled, the passenger may choose:

1. Re-routing to the destination at the earliest opportunity under comparable transport conditions.
2. Re-routing at a later date convenient to the passenger, subject to available seats.
3. Refund of the unused travel, together with reimbursement of already-used segments that no longer serve the journey's purpose and a return journey to the first departure point when applicable.

Care arrangements, such as refreshments, communications and accommodation, may also apply. If re-routing uses a different airport serving the same destination, the airline pays for transport to the originally booked airport or an agreed nearby location.

The chapter lists cases where cancellation does **not** create a cash-compensation obligation:

* Passengers were informed at least **14 days** before departure.
* They were informed between **7 and 14 days** before departure and offered an alternative leaving no more than **2 hours earlier** and arriving no more than **4 hours later**.
* They were informed less than **7 days** before departure and offered an alternative leaving no more than **1 hour earlier** and arriving no more than **2 hours later**.
* The cancellation resulted from extraordinary circumstances that could not have been avoided by reasonable measures.

The airline must explain available alternative transport arrangements.

### Delayed flights

The minimum delay that triggers refreshments and communication support depends on flight type:

|Flight type|Delay threshold|
|-|-|
|Type 1|**2 hours**|
|Type 2|**3 hours**|
|Type 3|**4 hours**|

If departure is postponed until the following day, accommodation becomes applicable. At a delay of **5 hours**, passengers may choose not to continue and may receive refunds of unused travel, refunds of travel already made if now purposeless, and where relevant a return flight to the original departure point.

### Compensation amounts and reductions

The basic cash-compensation amounts are:

|Flight type|Compensation|Reduced compensation when alternative arrival is within|
|-|-:|-|
|Type 1|**€250**|**2 hours** of the planned arrival time|
|Type 2|**€400**|**3 hours** of the planned arrival time|
|Type 3|**€600**|**4 hours** of the planned arrival time|

In the specified re-routing cases, compensation may be **reduced by 50%**. Cash compensation is not required where the applicable **extraordinary circumstances** exception is satisfied.

### Refund or re-routing

The passenger's election may comprise:

* Repayment of unused portions of the ticket, including the relevant used portion if it no longer serves the trip, and a return flight to the first departure point where required.
* Comparable transport to the intended destination at the earliest opportunity.
* Comparable transport at a later date chosen by the passenger, if seats are available.

Where an alternative flight lands at another airport serving the same city, the carrier covers the necessary onward transport to the original airport or agreed nearby destination.

### Meals, communications and accommodation

When care is due, passengers receive it **without charge**:

* Food and refreshments appropriate to the wait.
* **Two** calls, emails, fax messages or telex messages.
* Hotel accommodation and transport between the hotel and airport where an overnight or additional stay is required.

For delays, the account provides that the airline may withhold these forms of assistance where arranging them would delay departure still further.

### Upgrading and downgrading

An airline may not charge an extra fare where the passenger is accommodated in a class **higher** than the class purchased. For placement in a **lower** class, the specified refund is **30%**, **50%** or **75%** of the ticket cost for Type 1, Type 2 or Type 3 flights respectively.

### Payment and passenger notification

Refunds may be made by **cash, electronic bank transfer, bank draft or cheque**. A passenger may instead accept vouchers or services by **signed agreement**.

Airlines must display a notice at the **check-in counter** advising passengers who encounter denied boarding, cancellation or a delay of at least **two hours** to obtain information about their compensation and assistance rights. When boarding is denied, a flight is cancelled or a delay passes two hours, each affected passenger must be given written information explaining the applicable rights and identifying the national enforcement body.

|**Type 2**|Flight **within the EU over 1,500 km**, or another flight **over 1,500 km but under 3,500 km**.|
|**Type 3**|Flight **outside the EU over 3,500 km**.|
