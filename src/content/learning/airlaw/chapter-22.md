# Chapter 22 — Obstacle Marking and Aerodrome Services

## Introduction

### 22.1 Obstacles

Identifying an obstruction through conspicuous markings or lights helps pilots recognize a potential collision hazard. These measures are warning systems; installing them does not, by itself, remove the operational restrictions associated with the object. For structures situated on or near an aerodrome, the allocation of marking and lighting responsibilities must be agreed between the aerodrome licence holder and the structure owners. On the movement area, however, the licence holder remains responsible for ensuring that every obstacle is appropriately identified, regardless of who owns it.

Surveyed obstacles are taken into account when instrument arrival and departure procedures are designed. They are needed in establishing operating minima and the applicable obstacle clearance altitude or height. The concern here extends to objects near the aerodrome and along flight paths that could endanger local traffic or aircraft en route.

Objects beyond the property boundary can be as operationally significant as those on the aerodrome itself. They can reduce the distances usable for landing or take-off and restrict operations under certain meteorological conditions. Consequently, local airspace surrounding the aerodrome forms part of the environment assessed for aerodrome licensing. Maintaining adequate obstacle clearance in that airspace is as important to the granting and continued validity of the licence as the runway and runway-strip physical standards.

### 22.2 Obstacle Identification Surface (OIS)

An object qualifies as an aerodrome obstacle when it extends through the obstacle identification surface (OIS) within **15 km** of the aerodrome. Surveys establish these penetrations, which are recorded in the aerodrome's entry in the **Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP)**.

The OIS comprises interconnected sloping and level surfaces around the runway. Its starting reference is the cleared strip alongside the runway. From the cleared-strip limits, the inner transitional surface rises from ground level to **45 m** and extends as far as **3000 m** out from the strip edges. Farther out, the outer transitional portion takes the surface up to **150 m** and continues to the **15 km** outer limit. The take-off climb OIS depiction also shows a surface extending **3000 m** beyond the runway end.

## Visual Aids for Denoting Obstacles

### 22.3 Requirement for Marking/Lighting

An obstacle is marked or illuminated to make its location apparent to aircraft and thereby reduce the risk it presents. Fixed obstacles should normally bear identifying markings. They should additionally be illuminated whenever the aerodrome supports night operations. If **high-intensity obstacle lights** make an object sufficiently identifiable during daylight, separate day markings need not be applied.

### 22.4 Obstacles on the Movement Area

With the exception of aircraft, vehicles and other movable items on an aerodrome movement area are treated as obstacles. They require suitable markings and, whenever both the vehicle and aerodrome operate after dark or in low visibility, obstacle lighting. Vehicles and aircraft-handling equipment confined to aprons may qualify for an exemption. Elevated aeronautical ground lights positioned on the movement area must themselves be identified in a way that makes them readily noticeable in daylight.

### 22.5 Marking of Objects

Wherever it is practicable to do so, a fixed obstacle requiring identification should be made conspicuous by painting the structure. When colouring the object is impractical, place suitable markers or flags on it or above it instead. **Orange or red** are the preferred colours, provided they remain clearly distinguishable against the surroundings. A different colour is appropriate where red or orange would blend into the background and fail to provide adequate contrast.

### 22.6 Vehicles and Mobile Objects

A mobile obstacle that is identified by its body colour should use one highly visible colour. **Red** or **yellowish-green** are preferred for emergency-response vehicles; **yellow** is preferred for general service vehicles. The operator of the aerodrome must see that movement-area vehicles are marked and/or lit as required, even if a vehicle belongs to another organization. The requirement covers, among others, engineering and maintenance vehicles, air traffic control vehicles, **Follow Me** vehicles, tugs and other aircraft-towing vehicles, and refuelling vehicles.

Whenever an authorized vehicle enters the movement area, its obstacle lights must be switched on. Such vehicles carry **low-intensity flashing yellow** obstacle lights placed at the highest point of the prime mover. A towed trailer instead displays **low-intensity steady red** lights at its highest point. Structures with restricted movement, including **air bridges**, also use **low-intensity steady red** lights.

### 22.7 Emergency Vehicles

When responding to an emergency, aerodrome ambulances, police or security vehicles, and rescue and firefighting appliances should show **flashing blue lights** in addition to the ordinary conspicuity provisions for mobile objects. An external civilian fire or rescue vehicle summoned onto the aerodrome must also display flashing blue lights. It must be accompanied by an escort vehicle capable of radio communication with **ATC**.

### 22.8 Lighting of Objects

Where an obstacle must be illuminated, the selected arrangement may use **low-, medium- or high-intensity obstacle lights**, individually or in combination. High-intensity systems are intended to make obstacles detectable **in daylight as well as at night**.

### 22.9 Low-Intensity Lights

On a fixed structure, low-intensity obstacle lamps emit **steady red light**, rather than flashing light. The light output must enable observers to distinguish the obstacle against surrounding lighting and typical ambient illumination. The same steady-red requirement applies to obstacles having limited mobility, such as passenger boarding bridges. Selection of intensity must consider adjacent lights and the brightness of the background against which the object will be seen.

Where low-intensity lighting cannot provide adequate conspicuity, or where the hazard calls for an earlier or more prominent warning, **medium- or high-intensity** lights should be fitted instead.

### 22.10 Medium-Intensity Lights

Normally, medium-intensity obstacle lamps **flash red**. The exception occurs when they are integrated with a high-intensity obstacle-lighting installation; in that arrangement they **flash white**. The permitted flashing rate is **20–60 flashes per minute**, and units installed on the same object should flash together.

Use medium-intensity lights, either as the sole obstacle-lighting type or alongside low-intensity lamps, for an object covering an extensive area or extending **more than 45 m** above the surrounding ground.

### 22.11 High-Intensity Lights

High-intensity obstacle-lighting units are **flashing white**. Lamps associated with one object should operate simultaneously at **40–60 flashes per minute**. On a tower, however, the specified sequential presentation is **middle lamp first, top lamp second and bottom lamp last**.

Such lighting should identify an object standing **more than 150 m** above surrounding ground when an **aeronautical study** establishes that the additional conspicuity is essential to recognize that object by day.

### 22.12 Tall Objects

Where the top of an object identified with low- or medium-intensity lamps rises **more than 45 m** above the surrounding terrain, intermediate obstacle lights must be provided. For an object situated among buildings, the relevant comparison may instead be the height of the neighbouring rooftops.

Distribute these intermediate lamps as evenly as reasonably possible between the top lighting and the ground-level reference, or the surrounding roof level where that is applicable. **No vertical interval may exceed 45 m.**

### 22.13 Lighting of Aircraft

Aircraft lighting is addressed in **Chapter 6, Rules of the Air**; fuller treatment also forms part of the **Operational Procedures (OP) syllabus**. An aircraft left parked on the manoeuvring area itself constitutes an obstacle. Its extremities should be shown by its **navigation lights** or by supplementary illumination, such as **anti-collision lighting**, that establishes the outer limits of the aircraft.

### 22.14 En Route Obstacles

At distances **greater than 15 km from an aerodrome**, a structure is normally classed as an obstacle to aircraft in flight when its height **exceeds 150 m**. An object lower than this may still be important where it is conspicuous and lies on or beside a route regularly flown by helicopters.

A typical en route obstacle installation uses **steady red lighting at night** and **high-intensity flashing white lighting by day**. Environmental restrictions may prevent the installation or operation of high-intensity lights.

### 22.15 Marking of Overhead Wires or Cables

To make suspended overhead wires and cables visible to aircraft, fit them with **coloured spherical markers**.

## Visual Aids for Denoting Restricted Use Areas

### 22.16 Closed Runways and Taxiways

A runway, taxiway or subsection permanently withdrawn from **all aircraft use** must display a closure marking. A similar marking should normally identify a temporarily closed portion; it may be omitted for a short closure when **air traffic services** have given adequate warning.

On a closed **runway**, place a closure cross at each end of the closed runway or section, supplementing it with additional crosses where necessary so the gap between successive closure markings **never exceeds 300 m**. On a closed **taxiway**, the minimum arrangement places a closure mark at **each end** of the affected length. A runway closure cross is **white**; a taxiway closure cross is **yellow**.

For a temporary closure, a **frangible barrier**, non-paint marking material or another effective means of indicating the closure may replace painted crosses. Once a runway or taxiway has been closed permanently, its standard operational markings must be **removed or obliterated**. The normal lighting system of a closed runway, taxiway or section must remain **off**, except for operation needed in maintenance work.

### 22.17 Non-Load-Bearing Surfaces

An aerodrome may contain taxiway shoulders, holding-bay shoulders, apron shoulders and other areas that look similar to pavement capable of supporting an aircraft but are not designed for the aircraft's load. If an aircraft might be damaged by entering such a surface, a **taxi side-stripe marking** must clearly separate the non-load-bearing area from the load-bearing movement surface.

### 22.18 Pre-Threshold Area

Where pavement extends **more than 60 m** before a runway threshold but is **unsuitable for normal aircraft use**, mark that entire pre-threshold length with **chevrons**. Each chevron points **towards the runway**.

## Emergency and Other Services

### 22.19 Rescue and Fire Fighting (RFF)

The foremost aim of an aerodrome **Rescue and Fire Fighting (RFF)** service is to **preserve life**. RFF provision must therefore address the possibility of an aircraft accident or incident on the aerodrome or directly nearby, where timely rescue offers the best prospect of saving occupants. Personnel must be prepared to suppress a fire that begins immediately after an occurrence and equally a fire that develops during an ongoing rescue.

For an accident in which survival is possible, three factors largely determine rescue effectiveness: the **training of the responders**, the **capability of their equipment**, and the **speed with which both can be deployed**. The aerodrome RFF requirements described here do not include the separate provision needed for structural or fuel-storage-facility fires, nor runway foaming.

The RFF provider may be a properly located and equipped **public or private organization**. Its fire station would ordinarily be on the aerodrome, but a base outside the boundary is acceptable **if the stipulated response time is achieved**.

### 22.20 Level of Protection to Be Provided

The RFF protection level at an aerodrome is governed by its **RFF category**. Identify this category from the **overall length** and **maximum fuselage width** of the largest aeroplane types normally operating there. First select the length-based category from the table. If that aeroplane's fuselage exceeds the maximum width permitted in the selected row, **move up one category**.

A limited reduction is permitted where aeroplanes in the aerodrome's highest normally applicable category conduct **fewer than 700 movements in the busiest consecutive three-month period**. In that case, the available RFF protection may be reduced by **one category at most**, a provision stated as effective **from 1 January 2000**. During foreseeable intervals of reduced activity, however, the available level must still accommodate the **highest-category aeroplane scheduled to use the aerodrome during that interval**, whatever the number of movements.

### 22.21 Response Time

Under **optimum visibility and ground-surface conditions**, the operational RFF objective is to reach **the end of every runway** and **any other point on the movement area** within **3 minutes**. The response-time clock starts with the **initial notification** to the RFF service. It stops only when the **first response vehicle** has reached a position from which it can deliver foam at **not less than 50% of the specified discharge rate**.

When visibility is degraded, measures to **guide RFF vehicles** may be necessary to approach the response-time objective as closely as conditions permit.

### 22.22 Emergency Access Roads

Where the terrain allows, provide purpose-suitable roads that let emergency crews attain the shortest possible response times. Access routes should particularly support the approach areas out to **1000 m from the runway threshold**, or at minimum those portions within the aerodrome boundary. Fencing layouts must allow for convenient emergency access beyond the fence line where necessary. Ordinary aerodrome service roads may serve this function if their **position and construction** are appropriate.

### 22.23 Fire Stations

Normally, RFF vehicles are kept in an aerodrome **fire station**. If one station cannot achieve the required response time, establish **satellite fire stations**. The station layout and location should give fire vehicles the most direct unobstructed route to the runway, requiring as few turns as feasible. Location **inside** the aerodrome is not an absolute requirement: an external fire station is acceptable when it can satisfy the specified **response time**.

## Other Aerodrome Services

### 22.24 Apron Management Service

Where traffic density and operating circumstances justify it, the aerodrome must arrange an appropriate **apron management service**. This function is distinct from **Ground Control**. It can be delivered by the **aerodrome ATS unit**, the **aerodrome operating authority**, or a coordinated combination of the two. Commercial providers delivering apron management may also be responsible for **refuelling, de-icing, baggage and freight handling**, and transporting passengers by bus where buses are used.

If the tower does not perform apron management itself, specific coordination procedures must govern the transfer of aircraft between the apron management unit and the **aerodrome control tower**. A normal departure sequence illustrates this coordination. When the aircraft is operationally and technically prepared to depart, the pilot contacts **Ground Control** to request **engine start**, which also communicates that the flight is ready to begin its departure sequence. A tow tug will by then be connected to the aircraft, with the responsible ground crew member (the **crew chief**) connected to the flight deck through an external intercom.

After a **pushback clearance or approval** has been obtained, the aircraft is pushed out toward the taxiway, and the engines are started during this stage. Once the pilots are satisfied that aircraft systems are functioning correctly, the tug is disconnected and **nose-wheel steering** is re-engaged. The crew chief then advises the flight crew that relevant access panels have been secured and that the external intercom lead is being removed.

A properly equipped apron management service needs **radiotelephony communication facilities**. During **low-visibility procedures**, apron vehicle and pedestrian activity must be restricted to the **essential minimum**. Individual aircraft stands require visual surveillance so that arriving or parked aircraft maintain the **recommended clearance distances**.

The service performs three operational functions:

- Directing apron movements to avoid collisions **between aircraft** and **between aircraft and obstacles**.
- Controlling aircraft entry to the apron and coordinating departure from it with the **aerodrome control tower**.
- Managing vehicle movement safely and without unnecessary delay, while appropriately controlling other apron activities.

### 22.25 Emergency Vehicles

An emergency-response vehicle answering an emergency has **priority over all other surface-movement traffic**. A vehicle being driven on an apron must yield to an emergency vehicle, to an aircraft that is **taxiing or about to taxi**, and to an aircraft undergoing **pushback or towing**. Priority among other vehicles is determined by the applicable **local rules**.

### 22.26 Ground Servicing to Aircraft

During aircraft ground servicing, **suitable firefighting equipment** and personnel trained to use it must be readily available for the **initial response to a fuel fire**. Arrangements must also exist to summon the RFF service promptly if a fire or **major fuel spill** occurs.

When fuel is being loaded while passengers are **boarding, seated on the aircraft or leaving it**, ground-support equipment must be positioned so that a **sufficient number of exits remains usable** for rapid evacuation and an **unobstructed escape route** is available from each exit designated for emergency use.
