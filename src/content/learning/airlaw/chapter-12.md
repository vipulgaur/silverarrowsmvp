# Chapter 12: Parallel or Near-parallel Runway Operation

## 12.1 Simultaneous Operations on Parallel Runways

Airports with heavy **IFR traffic during instrument meteorological conditions (IMC)** can increase their capacity by operating two or more parallel, or nearly parallel, runways at the same time. Where each runway has an appropriate precision approach facility, such as **ILS or MLS**, aircraft may be able to arrive simultaneously rather than using a single sequence for the entire airport.

This arrangement is important both when an existing aerodrome adds another runway and when a new airport is designed around multiple parallel runways. The configurations may involve two, three or four runways. The principles described for two parallel runways also apply, with suitable adaptations, to near-parallel or more complex layouts. **Radar availability and its use are required for parallel runway operations.**

The operating method is selected according to whether the runways are used for arrivals, departures, or a combination of both, and whether the aircraft on adjacent approaches are subject to radar separation minima.

## 12.2 Modes of Parallel Runway Operation

Four principal modes are distinguished:

| Mode | Type of operation | Essential arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| **Mode 1** | Independent parallel approaches | Simultaneous instrument approaches without applying the usual radar separation minimum between aircraft on adjacent established approach tracks. |
| **Mode 2** | Dependent parallel approaches | Simultaneous instrument approaches in which radar separation between aircraft on adjacent approaches is maintained. |
| **Mode 3** | Independent parallel departures | Simultaneous instrument departures in the same general direction from adjacent runways. |
| **Mode 4** | Segregated parallel operations | One runway is devoted to arrivals and the other to departures. |

The basic modes can also be combined in semi-mixed operations.

## 12.3 Mode 1 — Independent Parallel Approaches

In **Mode 1**, aircraft make simultaneous straight-in instrument approaches to adjacent runways. Once the relevant conditions for establishment on their respective **ILS localizer courses** or **MLS final approach tracks** have been met, the normal radar separation minima are not applied *between the aircraft on those adjacent approaches*. The approaches are nevertheless continuously monitored by radar.

The control arrangements include:

- Radar controllers assigned specifically to monitor parallel approaches, regardless of visibility or cloud conditions.
- Separate, dedicated radiotelephony frequencies for the controllers performing the monitoring task.
- **Straight-in approaches only**; procedures requiring track reversal are not used.
- Control intervention when a monitored aircraft departs significantly from its established approach course.

To keep arriving aircraft separated until they are established, each pair of parallel approaches is assigned a **high side** and a **low side**. The high side remains **300 m (1,000 ft)** above the low side. That vertical separation may be reduced only after **both aircraft** are established inbound on the corresponding ILS localizer centre lines or MLS final approach tracks.

Aircraft are speed-controlled and follow prescribed intercept arrangements. The northerly **high-side hold is at 3,500 ft** and the southerly **low-side hold at 2,500 ft**. Once established, an aircraft from the high side may descend to **2,500 ft**.

## 12.4 Separation During Independent Parallel Approaches

The initial **1,000 ft (300 m)** vertical division between the high- and low-side approaches is retained until both aircraft have acquired their assigned inbound precision-approach tracks. Once that requirement is satisfied, the separate approach streams may continue without applying the usual minimum radar spacing between the aircraft established on adjacent localizers or MLS tracks. Radar controllers must continue watching the tracks and issue corrective directions when an aircraft deviates significantly.

## 12.5 Mode 2 — Dependent Parallel Approaches

**Mode 2** also permits simultaneous instrument approaches, but aircraft using adjacent ILS or MLS procedures remain **dependent on radar separation**. Radar monitoring is required in all weather, and appropriately assigned monitoring controllers use dedicated frequencies. The arrivals must be **straight-in**; track-reversal procedures are excluded.

Radar vectors to join the final approach are arranged as follows:

- The **maximum localizer intercept angle is 30°**.
- Before intercepting the localizer, the aircraft flies **at least 1 NM straight and level**.
- The aircraft is established on its localizer course for **at least 2 NM before intercepting the glide path**.
- The general minimum radar separation specified in the written procedure **until localizer establishment is 5 NM**.
- Aircraft conducting the parallel procedures carry the complete **ILS/MLS equipment** needed for the operation.

The radar sequencing scheme identifies **3 NM minimum separation before localizer establishment** and **2 NM minimum separation** between the depicted traffic positions.

## 12.6 Mode 3 — Simultaneous Instrument Departures

**Mode 3** is also described as **independent parallel departures**. Both runways are used for simultaneous instrument take-offs in the same general direction.

The essential conditions are:

- Each departing aircraft is **identified on radar within 1 NM of the departure end of runway (DER)**.
- The departure tracks begin diverging by **at least 15° immediately after take-off**.
- The example spacing between parallel instrument departure runways is **not less than 760 m**.
- Where the runways do not meet the spacing appropriate for independent treatment because of wake-turbulence considerations, the aerodrome must not simply treat the two departures as independent: the applicable wake-turbulence limitations remain relevant.

## 12.7 Mode 4 — Segregated Departures and Arrivals

In **Mode 4**, the two runways have distinct functions: **one runway is used only for landing approaches, and the adjacent runway only for take-offs**. This is termed **segregated parallel operation**. London Heathrow is the example given for this arrangement.

Separating runway functions allows incoming and outgoing traffic to follow different operating streams. The arrangement is still subject to the applicable radar, runway-spacing and procedure-design requirements.

## 12.8 Semi-mixed Parallel Runway Operations

Runway-use arrangements need not follow one mode continuously. A **semi-mixed** arrangement combines elements of the four principal modes. Possible configurations include:

- One runway used exclusively for take-offs while the second handles both take-offs and landings.
- One runway used exclusively for approaches while the other accommodates a mixture of arrivals and departures.
- Simultaneous approaches with departures scheduled into gaps on either or both runways.

Such combinations are used at a number of large North American aerodromes. The chosen arrangement depends on traffic demand and the safety restrictions associated with the particular runways.

## 12.9 Aircraft Equipment

Aircraft taking part in the parallel runway arrangements described must have the required **complete ILS or MLS installation**. Appropriate navigation guidance is essential to remaining accurately within the protected approach paths.

## 12.10 Radar Monitoring

**Radar surveillance is required for all parallel runway operations regardless of meteorological conditions.** In the circumstances specified by the procedure, radar control may also be required. If the necessary radar capability is unavailable, simultaneous parallel operations are **suspended**, and the aerodrome returns to **single-runway operations**.

## 12.11 Separation and Approach Safety

Because adjacent parallel runways often lie too close together for ordinary radar-separation minima to be maintained throughout two independent final approaches, safety depends on **precise aircraft navigation**, properly designed approach areas and rapid controller intervention if necessary.

Aircraft separation may ordinarily be expressed laterally, longitudinally or vertically. Better-defined guidance and demonstrated track-keeping accuracy permit reduced spacing within the procedures designed to protect aircraft on parallel tracks. Once the specified ILS localizer establishment requirements are met, **Mode 1** operates without the usual radar separation minima between the two approach streams, whereas **Mode 2** continues using radar separation between the streams. Both remain subject to monitoring and the applicable protective limits.

## 12.12 Normal Operating Zone (NOZ)

For **Mode 1**, a **Normal Operating Zone (NOZ)** is the protected volume of airspace extending to either side of an ILS localizer centre line or MLS final approach track within which the aircraft is expected to remain while tracking the approach correctly.

The protected tracking requirement is associated with a **half-scale course-deviation indicator (CDI) deflection**. In the independent approach arrangement, only the **inner half of each NOZ** is considered when determining separation from the centrally located protective zone.

## 12.13 No Transgression Zone (NTZ)

The **No Transgression Zone (NTZ)** is the centrally positioned buffer strip between the extended runway centre lines during **Mode 1 independent parallel approaches**. Its minimum width is **610 m**.

If one aircraft enters the NTZ, the monitoring controller must **intervene immediately** to protect the aircraft on the adjacent final approach. The threatened aircraft would ordinarily be instructed to **turn away from the intruding aircraft and climb**, either towards the appropriate **minimum sector altitude (MSA)** or according to its published missed-approach instructions. When that aircraft has been protected, the controller can assist the intruding aircraft to re-establish its approach or to execute the missed approach as necessary.

## 12.14 Missed-approach and Departure-track Divergence

For the simultaneous parallel arrangements addressed here, missed-approach tracks on adjacent runways must **diverge by at least 30°**. When a procedure calls for an early turn to establish the required separation, the pilot begins that turn **as soon as practicable**. A **30°** minimum also applies to the missed-approach/departure-track divergence in the simultaneous-operation arrangement; **Mode 3 independent departures** separately require **15° immediately after take-off**.

## 12.15 Minimum Runway Spacing

The distance required between adjacent runway centre lines depends on the operating mode and on the positions of their thresholds or departure points. When the two thresholds are abeam one another, arriving aircraft that are equally distant from touchdown tend also to be at similar heights; this is the most demanding case for runway-spacing design.

If the thresholds are **staggered**, longitudinal displacement can introduce useful height separation and may permit closer lateral spacing. In the segregated arrangement, the spacing adjustment is **30 m for each 150 m** of threshold staggering, subject to the way in which the runway is displaced.

The minimum runway separations are:

| Parallel-operation mode | Minimum centre-line spacing |
|---|---:|
| **Mode 1 — Independent parallel approaches** | **1,035 m** |
| **Mode 2 — Dependent parallel approaches** | **915 m** |
| **Mode 3 — Independent parallel departures** | **760 m** |
| **Mode 4 — Segregated parallel operations** | **760 m**, subject to relevant staggering provisions |

## 12.16 Wake Turbulence Considerations

The presence of parallel runways does not remove the risk of wake vortices generated by aircraft on the adjacent runway. This can restrict how independently the two runways may be used, especially where departing or approaching flight paths are close to one another.

The chapter requires **wake-turbulence separation** when either of these conditions applies:

- The **lateral separation between parallel runways is less than 760 m**.
- The flight paths of arriving or departing aircraft **cross at the same altitude**, or one crosses **within 1,000 ft below** the other aircraft's flight level.

These criteria can limit the capacity benefit otherwise gained from simultaneous parallel runway operations.
