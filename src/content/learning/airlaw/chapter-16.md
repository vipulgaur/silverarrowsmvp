# Chapter 16: Separation

## 1. Concept of Separation

### 16.1 Separation of Controlled Traffic

An essential function of **air traffic control (ATC)** is preventing collisions between aircraft receiving an ATC service. ICAO's PANS-ATM procedures (Doc 4444) describe the forms of separation and the minimum spacing that may be used in controlled airspace (CAS). Separation may be **vertical**, **horizontal**, or **composite** (a combination of reduced vertical and horizontal spacing, subject to the specified conditions).

These standards serve three related purposes: protecting aircraft from collision, allowing for hazardous **wake turbulence**, and organizing traffic for departures and arrivals. Under the airspace classification scheme in this course, ATC separation is provided:

- Between all aircraft in **Classes A and B**.
- Between **IFR flights** in **Classes C, D and E**.
- Between **IFR and VFR flights** in **Class C**.
- Between **IFR and special VFR flights**.
- Between **special VFR flights**, when directed by the relevant ATS authority.

The source arrangements also permit, in specified circumstances, an IFR aircraft flying in daylight VMC in Class D or E to climb or descend while maintaining separation visually under a specific clearance.

### 16.2 Maintaining the Required Minimum

An ATC clearance must not result in a pair of aircraft coming closer than the minimum prescribed for the separation method in use. Conditions such as a significant wake-turbulence risk or unlawful interference may require **greater-than-normal margins**. If the existing method cannot be sustained, controllers must ensure that a different acceptable separation method is effective before the first one ceases to provide the necessary protection.

### 16.3 Composite Separation

Composite separation is an exceptional arrangement, used where normal navigation or operating capability has seriously deteriorated and where it is allowed by the applicable regional air-navigation agreement. Instead of retaining the full standard in just one dimension, aircraft use **both horizontal and vertical spacing**, with neither reduced below half the corresponding normal requirement. The NAT radio-failure arrangements and contingency procedures for unlawful interference are examples of situations in which such procedures have been specified.

### 16.4 Essential Traffic Information

**Essential traffic** is another controlled flight from which the aircraft concerned is required to be separated but for which the applicable minimum **is not, or will not be, maintained**. When flights become essential traffic to one another, the controller passes the appropriate essential-traffic information to the pilots concerned.

## 2. Vertical Separation

### 16.5 Vertical Separation Standards

Vertical separation assigns aircraft **different altitudes or flight levels**, using a common altimeter reference, rather than relying on horizontal distance. In the framework described here:

| Airspace arrangement | Vertical separation minimum |
|---|---|
| Designated RVSM airspace | **300 m (1,000 ft)** below FL410, or another approved upper limit; **600 m (2,000 ft)** at or above the specified upper limit |
| Other airspace | **300 m (1,000 ft)** below FL290; **600 m (2,000 ft)** at or above FL290 |

### 16.6 RVSM Cruising Levels

For the domestic airspace arrangement discussed here, **Reduced Vertical Separation Minimum (RVSM)** applies between **FL290 and FL410 inclusive**. Both boundary levels belong to the RVSM band. This statement is specific to the course's treatment of RVSM and its North Atlantic regional distinction.

### 16.7 Lowest Usable Cruising Level

Controllers normally cannot assign cruising levels below the State's applicable minimum flight altitude unless specifically authorized. An Area Control Centre (ACC) may establish the **lowest usable flight level** across the whole control area or individual sectors and will pass it to pilots on request. Unless national arrangements require otherwise, the lowest usable flight level corresponds to, or is immediately above, the applicable minimum flight altitude.

### 16.8 Allocation of Cruising Levels

An ACC normally assigns a single cruising level for operation within its area, including aircraft entering another control area. **Cruise climb** is an exception: the aircraft may be authorized to operate within a specified band of levels rather than at only one.

### 16.9 Changing a Cruising Level

Following its initial clearance, an aircraft normally asks ATC before changing level. Clearance can specify a **particular position, time or rate of climb/descent**. On ATS routes continuing outside the present control area, changes are normally arranged at radio navigation aids. If an aircraft is cleared into a control area below its minimum permissible cruising level, the ACC issues an appropriate climb clearance without waiting for a request.

### 16.10 Flights to the Same Destination

Where feasible, ATC considers the required arrival sequence when assigning the cruising levels of aircraft bound for one aerodrome, so that the approach order can be established more efficiently.

### 16.11 Priority for an Occupied Level

An aircraft **already established** at a particular cruising level takes precedence over another requesting that level. If multiple aircraft are at the same level, the aircraft ahead on the route has priority.

### 16.12 Reusing a Vacated Level

Normally, a level may be allocated to another aircraft after its former occupant **reports leaving it**. During severe turbulence or cruise climb, ATC waits until the previous occupant reports a different level with the prescribed vertical interval.

### 16.13 Separation Agreed Between Pilots

When the pilots can communicate directly and both agree, ATC may authorize them to maintain a **specified vertical separation** from one another while one or both are climbing or descending.

### 16.14 Maintaining Own Separation in Daylight VMC

A controlled flight in **Class D or E** may, after requesting it and obtaining both the other pilot's agreement and ATC authorization, climb or descend visually relative to **one other aircraft**. In this chapter's procedure, the authorization is limited to the part of the flight **below 10,000 ft** and **during daylight VMC**. ATC must provide alternative instructions for a loss of VMC. If the weather deteriorates to the VMC limits, the pilot informs ATC that the alternative instructions are being followed.

## 3. Horizontal Separation

### 16.15 Two Forms of Horizontal Spacing

**Horizontal separation** keeps aircraft apart in the horizontal plane. **Lateral separation** places them on appropriately spaced routes or tracks. **Longitudinal separation** keeps sufficient time or distance between their positions along the relevant direction of flight.

### 16.16 Lateral Separation and Protected Areas

An aircraft's navigation position contains uncertainty. Therefore, safe lateral spacing does not depend only on the distance between the plotted centre lines of two routes. Each route has a **protected position-accuracy area** plus an additional **safety buffer**. The required lateral standard keeps these areas apart; the relevant authority sets the buffer and associated minima.

|---|---|
| VOR | **15°** |
| NDB | **30°** |
| Dead-reckoning (DR) fix | **45°** |

### 16.18 Aircraft Using Different Navigation Aids

If aircraft navigate using unlike ground aids, or one uses RNAV, the separation assessment still concerns the **whole protected airspace** derived for each track. Their respective protected areas, including the necessary buffers, must remain non-overlapping.

### 16.19 RNAV and Parallel Tracks

Approved RNP-based routes, including designated parallel RNAV tracks, can provide lateral spacing through **separate route centre lines**. Route spacing is designed so that the protected corridors remain apart. The chapter relates this approach to North Atlantic track allocation in the NAT MNPSA.

### 16.20 Why Longitudinal Separation Needs More Allowance

In **procedural ATC**, the controller generally knows the aircraft position from pilot reports rather than continuous radar surveillance. A report may refer to a radio aid, a geographic reporting point or a position defined by the aircraft's navigation system. Accuracy of the report, differences in navigation performance, and the elapsed time between reports must all be considered before adding a safety buffer. Routes in remote oceanic or desert regions can therefore require particularly wide spacing. The resulting longitudinal standard is specified in **time** or **distance**.

### 16.21 Application and Track Definitions

Longitudinal protection means the estimated aircraft positions must stay at least the prescribed interval apart. The controller can create the necessary interval through assigned departure times, timed arrivals at fixes, deliberate delays or holding instructions. The **Mach number technique**, when regionally authorized, can support longitudinal separation.

The track relationships are defined as follows:

| Relationship | Relative track angle |
|---|---|
| **Same track** | Less than **45°** apart, or more than **315°**, with overlapping protection areas |
| **Reciprocal tracks** | Greater than **135°** but less than **225°**, with overlapping protection areas |
| **Crossing tracks** | From **45° to 135°**, or **225° to 315°** |

### 16.22 Separation Expressed as Time

The applicable time interval depends on whether the flights are **at the same level** or one is **climbing or descending**, and on whether their tracks are the same, crossing or reciprocal.

### 16.23 Aircraft at the Same Level

On **the same track**, the initial time minimum is **15 minutes**. Where navigation facilities permit frequent reliable determination of position and speed, this can become **10 minutes**. A **5-minute** interval may apply when the aircraft depart from the same aerodrome or pass a common reporting point, with the interval assured as the following aircraft joins the route, and the leading aircraft has a **TAS at least 20 kt greater** than the follower. If the leading aircraft is **at least 40 kt faster**, a **3-minute** interval can be used under the stated conditions.

### 16.25 DME Distance-Based Separation

When **Distance Measuring Equipment (DME)** gives the necessary along-track distance, aircraft can be separated by a prescribed number of nautical miles rather than by elapsed time. The described method requires **direct two-way communication between pilot and controller**. The chapter distinguishes this from some North Atlantic HF operations where messages are handled by a radio operator.

### 16.26 DME Criteria by Track Relationship

- **Same track, same level:** normally **20 NM**, provided both aircraft use on-track DME and simultaneous DME reports are obtained regularly. The minimum may be **10 NM** when the leading aircraft maintains a TAS at least **20 kt faster**.
- **Crossing tracks, same level:** those same distance standards apply when both report DME range from the crossing-point station and the tracks meet at **less than 90°**.
- **Climb/descent through a level:** **10 NM** while vertical separation is absent, with on-track DME used by both aircraft, one aircraft remaining level and simultaneous DME reporting providing verification.
- **Reciprocal tracks:** a level change through the other's flight level may be cleared once the aircraft have positively passed and have become at least **10 NM apart**, unless the authority stipulates a different distance.

### 16.27 Mach Number Technique

When ATC assigns a **Mach number** for procedural longitudinal spacing, a turbojet aircraft must maintain it and seek approval before changing speed. If turbulence makes an immediate short-term adjustment unavoidable, the pilot advises ATC promptly. If an assigned Mach cannot be maintained during a proposed climb or descent, the pilot reports this when requesting that manoeuvre.

The specified interval is established when the aircraft have passed the **same reported point** and fly the same or steadily diverging tracks, or when other reliable surveillance can confirm that the correct interval will exist at their common point.

### 16.28 Time Intervals with Mach Control

If the leading turbojet maintains the same or a higher Mach number than the aircraft behind, the usual basic spacing is **10 minutes**. A sufficiently larger leading Mach number allows the following reductions:

| Leading Mach minus following Mach | Minimum time separation |
|---|---|
| 0.00 | 10 min |
| 0.01 | 10 min |
| 0.02 | 9 min |
| 0.03 | 8 min |
| 0.04 | 7 min |
| 0.05 | 6 min |
| 0.06 | 5 min |

### 16.29 Distance Separation Using RNAV

For suitably equipped RNAV aircraft on relevant RNAV or VOR-defined ATS routes, a **150 km (80 NM)** along-track standard can replace the normal **10-minute** time minimum. The aircraft must use common reference waypoints, provide regularly checked **simultaneous RNAV-distance reports**, maintain direct pilot–controller communications and apply the Mach number technique. If an aircraft loses the navigation performance needed for this arrangement, the normal longitudinal standard replaces it.

This **80 NM** interval applies to aircraft at the same level; also when one aircraft crosses another's level while the other remains level, with valid simultaneous readings; and, on reciprocal tracks, after the aircraft are positively confirmed to have passed by at least **80 NM**.

### 16.30 RNAV Separation Where RNP Is Specified

The RNP-dependent criteria for aircraft on the same track are:

| RNP type | Communications | Surveillance | Distance verification | Separation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RNP 20** | Direct pilot/controller | Procedural position reports | At least every **60 min** | **80 NM** |
| **RNP 10** | Direct pilot/controller | Procedural position reports | At least every **30 min** | **50 NM** |

During use of the **50 NM** standard, if a position report is missed, ATC takes action to establish contact **within 3 minutes**. If communications remain unavailable **after 8 minutes**, an alternative type of separation must be established. A level crossing can be allowed after it has been shown that the aircraft have passed one another.

### 16.31 Authorised Reductions in Separation Standards

The appropriate ATS authority may allow smaller minima after consultation with affected operators. The chapter lists supporting circumstances such as accurate airborne electronic position fixing with reliable communications; rapidly available radar-derived positions; equipment enabling the controller to predict the aircraft's track and receive frequent reliable position updates; and RNAV systems with sufficient track-keeping accuracy. For RNAV-based reductions, the specified communications requirements may also be modified when the ATS authority determines that adequate safety is retained and operators have been consulted.

## 4. Radar Separation and Wake Turbulence

### 16.32 Standard Radar Separation

Radar gives the controller timely aircraft positions, but target display, update rate, radar location and equipment limitations still introduce errors. The **normal basic radar separation is 5 NM**. In the chapter's arrangements, this standard applies at the same level and allows for safe target discrimination, including the display separation necessary on a radar screen.

### 16.33 Reduced Radar Minima

With approval and suitable radar performance, the **5 NM** standard may be reduced:

- To **3 NM** where the local radar capability supports it.
- To **2.5 NM** for aircraft established on the **same ILS localizer**, within **10 NM of the runway threshold**, subject to the stated conditions.
- To **2 NM** between contacts on **adjacent localizer courses** during simultaneous parallel approaches in **Mode 2 (dependent)**, where approved.

### 16.34 Formation and Effects of Wake Vortices

Whenever an aircraft is generating lift, rotating wingtip vortices form behind its wings. Especially at **high mass and low speed**, turbulence left in the flight path may severely disturb a following aircraft. Air curls from the higher-pressure underside of the wing toward the lower-pressure upper surface, forming trailing rotating air masses. The source applies wake separation where the following aircraft enters the generating aircraft's level or the airspace **up to 1,000 ft below it**.

### 16.35 Wake Turbulence Mass Categories

The categories are determined using the aircraft's **maximum take-off mass (MTOM)**:

| Category | MTOM |
|---|---|
| **Heavy** | **136,000 kg or more** |
| **Medium** | **More than 7,000 kg but less than 136,000 kg** |
| **Light** | **7,000 kg or less** |

The certificated MTOM is stated in the aircraft's Certificate of Airworthiness.

### 16.36 Procedural Wake-Turbulence Minima

The non-radar wake standards in the following sections concern situations in which the **following aircraft is lighter** than the aircraft ahead; the associated runway and track geometry determines whether a time interval is required.

### 16.37 Timed Arrivals

For arriving aircraft under the procedural standard:

- **Medium behind Heavy:** **2 minutes**.
- **Light behind Medium or Heavy:** **3 minutes**.

### 16.38 Departures Behind a Heavier Aircraft

A **2-minute** interval applies to a medium or light aircraft departing behind a heavy aircraft, or a light aircraft behind a medium, when the aircraft use:

- The **same runway**.
- **Parallel runways** less than **760 m** apart.
- **Crossing runways** where the projected tracks intersect at the heavier aircraft's altitude or within **1,000 ft below** it.
- Parallel runways **760 m or more apart** if the flight paths similarly intersect within the specified vertical region.

### 16.39 Runway with a Displaced Threshold

The specified **2-minute** wake separation also applies to crossing projected flight paths where a lighter aircraft departs behind the landing of a heavier aircraft, or lands after the departure of a heavier aircraft, on a runway having a displaced landing threshold. The relevant comparisons are light or medium versus heavy, and light versus medium.

### 16.40 Opposite-Direction Runway Operations

Use **2 minutes** between a lighter and a heavier aircraft when the heavier aircraft flies a low or missed approach and the lighter aircraft takes off in the opposite direction, or lands in the opposite direction on the same runway or on a parallel opposite-direction runway **less than 760 m apart**.

### 16.41 Identifying a Heavy Aircraft

On first radio contact with an ATC unit, the pilot of a **Heavy** wake-category aircraft includes **“Heavy”** after its call sign, such as *“London Control, Speedbird 216 Heavy, on 133.650.”* This alerts ATC to the separation implications of its wake.

### 16.42 Radar Wake-Turbulence Distances

When wake separation is radar-based, the required minima are given as **distance**, rather than time:

| Leading aircraft | Following aircraft | Minimum radar separation |

## 5. Visual Separation and Airport Traffic

### 16.43 Visual Separation Near Aerodromes

Outside low-visibility operating conditions, pilots or the aerodrome controller may be able to maintain spacing by **continuous visual observation**. This can increase runway and traffic-pattern capacity, because full procedural or radar intervals need not always be imposed. In the example used here, Heathrow's capacity approaches **60 take-offs and 60 landings per hour** in favorable operating conditions.

Reduced visual separation is permitted in the following circumstances: the tower controller can see and adequately separate all relevant aircraft; pilots report sustained visual contact with other aircraft; or a following pilot confirms that the aircraft ahead remains in sight and that appropriate spacing can be maintained.

### 16.44 Essential Local Traffic

**Essential local traffic** includes any aircraft, vehicle or person on or close to the runway, in the climb-out/take-off sector or near final approach, whose presence could create a collision hazard to departing or arriving aircraft. Where the controller knows of such traffic, the information must be passed to the affected flight crews **without delay**.

### 16.45 Sequencing Departures

IFR aircraft usually receive **SID-based** separation. The approach control unit normally establishes the route and clearance, which the tower communicates to the departing aircraft. For departures in VMC, a subsequent take-off clearance is normally given only after the preceding aircraft has **passed the runway's upwind end** or **turned away from the runway**.

For IFR departures the source describes a normal **5-minute** spacing. It may be reduced to **2 minutes** when the preceding aircraft's filed speed is at least **40 kt faster**, or to **1 minute** when the next aircraft's track diverges by **45° or more** from the first track immediately after departure. Parallel and diverging runway layouts may permit greater throughput, subject to the applicable procedures and wake considerations.

### 16.46 Departure Order and Taxi Planning

At a busy aerodrome, the ground-movement planner arranges aircraft to reach the runway holding point in the correct order for their assigned departure slots. Routes and wake categories may affect this sequence. A controller may suggest a departure direction other than into wind if it helps reduce delay, but the **pilot-in-command decides** whether the proposed direction can be accepted.

### 16.47 Departure Delays

Departure order may be adjusted to reduce average waiting time or accommodate operator requests. Where anticipated delays are **more than 30 minutes**, the ATC unit should inform the relevant operators.

### 16.48 Clearance for a Visual Approach

An arriving IFR flight may be authorized to carry out a visual approach when the pilot can maintain reference to terrain and either the reported ceiling is at or above the approved initial approach level, or the pilot reports conditions that give reasonable assurance the approach and landing can be completed visually.

### 16.49 Separating Departures from Arrivals

ATC remains responsible for spacing an IFR flight cleared for a visual approach from other arriving and departing traffic. An approaching aircraft is normally transferred from Approach Control to Aerodrome Control at a point that allows the tower to sequence departing aircraft and issue landing clearance. During certain radar-controlled approaches, including PAR or SRA, the approach radar controller retains control and obtains the landing clearance from the tower for transmission to the pilot.

For a **complete instrument approach** involving a procedure/base turn, a departure in **any direction** is permitted until the arriving aircraft begins that turn. A subsequent departure must diverge by at least **45° from the reciprocal of the approach direction**, and must take place **at least 3 minutes** before the arrival reaches the instrument runway's beginning.

For a **straight-in instrument approach**, a take-off in **any direction** is permitted until **5 minutes** before the arrival is expected over the instrument runway. A departure diverging by at least **45° from the reciprocal approach track** may be allowed until **3 minutes** before that point, or before the arriving aircraft reaches a designated approach fix in accordance with the relevant ATS arrangements.

## 6. Holding Stacks and Arrival Sequence

### 16.52 Order of Approaches

When several aircraft arrive, the aircraft at the **lowest level of the stack** normally receives the next approach. Aircraft awaiting a turn are placed in designated holding patterns. A later arrival may take precedence where there is an emergency affecting the aircraft or occupants; a hospital flight or a flight carrying seriously ill or injured persons; an aircraft performing search and rescue; or another priority flight designated by the authority. Appropriate emergency or medical priority radio calls include **MAYDAY**, **PAN PAN** and **PAN PAN MEDICAL**.

### 16.53 Establishing and Using a Stack

Holding stacks are usually located over the navigation aids serving as **initial approach fixes (IAFs)**. If a stack is full, a more remote **overflow holding pattern** may be used. Aircraft are normally accepted into the **lowest available holding level** and then move downward as traffic beneath them commences an approach. An aircraft that elects to continue holding for improved weather may be moved to another pattern or returned to the top so other aircraft can approach. A pilot returning after an unsuccessful approach is also normally placed at the top.

### 16.54 Expected Approach Time (EAT)

ATC issues an **EAT** to a flight entering a holding stack, and the pilot acknowledges it. In the system described, the initial EAT is not less than **30 minutes** after entering the stack. When the delay is expected to be **20 minutes or less**, ATC advises **“no delay expected.”** If the order changes, the EAT may be revised in **5-minute increments**; revised times must also be passed and acknowledged.

### 16.55 En-Route Holding

An en-route delay may be preferable to holding at low level near the destination, because aircraft can avoid the higher fuel consumption of a low-altitude holding stack. Aircraft held en route are given appropriate credit in the eventual approach order. Such a hold may be specified over a town, a latitude/longitude position or another significant point rather than over a conventional holding navaid. Unless ATC directs otherwise, the described default is a **right-hand holding pattern**.
