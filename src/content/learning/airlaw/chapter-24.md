# Chapter 24 — Search and Rescue

## Definitions and Abbreviations

### 24.1 Definitions

The following terms establish the meaning of the emergency phases, responsible personnel and search-and-rescue (SAR) organisations.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Alert phase** | An emergency stage in which concern has arisen regarding the safety of an aircraft or the people aboard it. |
| **Distress phase** | An emergency stage in which there is reasonable certainty that an aircraft and its occupants face serious, immediate danger, or that urgent assistance is needed. |
| **Emergency phase** | The collective designation for the uncertainty, alert and distress phases. Activation of an emergency phase requires air traffic services (ATS) to notify the rescue co-ordination centre (RCC) without delay. |
| **Operator** | An individual, organisation or business conducting, or offering to conduct, an aircraft operation. |
| **Pilot-in-command (PIC)** | The pilot accountable for the aircraft's safe operation throughout flight time. |
| **Rescue co-ordination centre (RCC)** | The unit that organises the SAR service effectively and directs the co-ordination of search-and-rescue activity throughout a designated SAR region. |
| **State of Registry** | The State whose aircraft register contains the aircraft's registration. |
| **Uncertainty phase** | An emergency stage in which the safety of an aircraft and its occupants cannot be established with certainty. |

## Establishment and Provision of SAR Service

### 24.2 Basis of Establishment

Within their own territories, Contracting States are required to arrange for search-and-rescue services to be set up and made available. These services must remain available **24 hours per day**. Assistance to an aircraft in distress, and to people surviving an aircraft accident, must not depend on the nationality of either the aircraft or the survivors.

### 24.3 Establishment of SAR Regions

Each Contracting State must specify the geographical search-and-rescue regions for which it will provide coverage. **No two SAR regions may overlap.** Wherever reasonably practicable, their boundaries should follow those of the corresponding **flight information regions (FIRs)**.

### 24.4 Establishment and Designation of SAR Service Units

An **RCC is required for every SAR region**. A Contracting State should additionally establish rescue sub-centres where these would make the SAR service more effective. The RCC, together with any such sub-centres, is responsible for organising the service.

Some areas lack public telecommunications capable of allowing witnesses to contact the RCC promptly and directly when an aircraft emergency is observed. In those areas, the State should appoint appropriate public-service or private-service units to act as **alerting posts**, so that emergency information can reach the rescue organisation.

## Co-operation between States

### 24.5 Requirement

Because searches and rescue missions can cross national boundaries, SAR arrangements must be co-ordinated between neighbouring Contracting States. States should also work towards common operating procedures wherever practicable, improving the ability of their rescue organisations to work together.

A Contracting State must allow rescue units from another State to enter its territory promptly to locate aircraft incidents or rescue accident survivors, subject to any conditions imposed by its authorities. To initiate such entry, the State dispatching rescue units must send its request to the destination State's RCC or another authority designated for that purpose. The request must explain the intended mission in full and why the mission is necessary.

The State receiving the request must **acknowledge it immediately** and advise, **as soon as possible**, whether any conditions apply to the proposed operation and what those conditions are.

### 24.6 Agreement with Other States

Neighbouring States should conclude reciprocal agreements specifying the circumstances in which one State's rescue units may enter the other's territory. These arrangements should facilitate rapid movement of rescue resources and limit administrative formalities to the minimum practicable.

States should empower their RCCs to:

- Ask another RCC for the assistance required for an operation, whether **aircraft, vessels, personnel or equipment**.
- Authorise the entry of those resources into their national territory where permission is necessary.
- Work with **customs, immigration and other relevant authorities** to ensure that authorised resources can enter without unnecessary delay.

### 24.7 Assistance to Other States

An RCC should be authorised by its State to support another State's RCC on request. Assistance may take the form of **aircraft, ships or other vessels, personnel, or equipment**, depending on operational needs.

## Operating Procedures

### 24.8 Information Concerning Emergencies

Contracting States should encourage anyone who witnesses an aircraft accident, or otherwise has grounds to suspect that an aircraft is experiencing an emergency, to report every available relevant detail immediately. Information may be passed to an appropriate **alerting post** or directly to the **RCC concerned**.

Any authority or component of the SAR organisation that receives, or has reason to believe, information indicating an aircraft emergency must forward all available facts to the RCC immediately. Once an RCC receives such information, it must assess it without delay and establish what extent of search-and-rescue activity is necessary.

If the RCC's initial report came from somewhere **other than an ATS unit**, the RCC itself must establish the corresponding **emergency phase** and then follow the operating procedures prescribed for that phase.

### 24.9 First Aircraft on Scene

Responsibility for co-ordinating arriving aircraft initially rests with the **PIC of the first aircraft at the accident site**, if that aircraft is not a dedicated SAR aircraft. The PIC retains this on-scene role until the **first SAR aircraft** arrives.

An exception arises when the first aircraft cannot communicate with the responsible RCC or ATS unit. Pending arrival of a dedicated SAR aircraft, responsibility should then be transferred **by mutual agreement** to another aircraft that can both establish and maintain the necessary communications.

### 24.10 Direction of Surface Craft

An aircraft assisting a distressed aircraft or surface vessel may have to guide a rescue vessel towards the location. Whenever possible, the pilot should use available communications equipment to send **precise directions**.

The chapter identifies the maritime radiotelephony facilities relevant to this task:

- Ocean-going ships maintain an HF listening watch on the maritime distress and calling frequency **2182 kHz (2.182 MHz)**.
- Shipping operating in coastal waters maintains a watch on **VHF FM Channel 16**.
- Aircraft generally do **not** carry marine **VHF FM** transmitters. Contact may nevertheless be relayed through the **Coast Guard** or coastal radio stations.

If it is impossible to establish radio contact, the aircraft must employ the relevant visual attention-getting signal. A vessel's **name and port of registration**, normally painted on its stern, can help the PIC identify it. The RCC can then communicate with that particular vessel using the maritime communications network.

### 24.11 Communication with Survivors

When the aircraft needs to pass information to survivors or surface rescue teams without an available two-way communication link, it should, where practicable, **drop communications equipment** to enable direct contact. Alternatively, the crew may communicate by **dropping a written message**.

Whenever a ground party displays a visual signal, the aircraft must indicate whether the message has been understood, using the appropriate air-to-ground acknowledgement method.

### 24.12 Signals with Surface Aircraft

An aircraft can direct a surface vessel towards the position of a distressed aircraft or vessel by making the following manoeuvres **in the specified order**. Repeating the sequence conveys the same instruction.

1. Fly a complete circle around the vessel **at least once**.
2. At low altitude, pass **close in front** of the vessel, crossing its projected track, while making **one** of these indications:
   - Rock the wings; **or**
   - Repeatedly open and close the throttle; **or**
   - Vary the propeller pitch.
3. Turn onto the heading which points in the direction the vessel is required to proceed.

### 24.13 Assistance No Longer Required

To indicate that its assistance is no longer required, the aircraft should cross the vessel's **wake close behind it at low altitude**. During this manoeuvre, the pilot should **rock the wings**, **open and close the throttle**, or **vary the propeller pitch**. This **astern** pass distinguishes the cessation signal from the **ahead-of-vessel** directional signal.

### 24.14 SAR Signals

Every specified search-and-rescue signal carries its assigned meaning when used. The established signals are reserved for their intended purposes; other signals which could be mistaken for them must not be introduced. A pilot observing a recognised signal must take the action appropriate to its meaning.

### 24.15 Ground/Air Visual Signal Code

An internationally standardised visual code allows people on the ground to convey simple messages or instructions to aircraft. There are **two distinct code sets**: one for **aircraft-accident survivors**, and the other for **ground search teams**.

### 24.16 Ground/Air Visual Signal Code for Use by Survivors

When radio communication is unavailable, survivors can construct large ground symbols in material that contrasts with the surrounding terrain. Methods include marking shapes in **snow**, pouring **oil onto sand**, or **burning grass in open ground**.

| No. | Message | Symbol |
|---:|---|:---:|
| 1 | Require assistance | **V** |
| 2 | Require medical assistance | **X** |
| 3 | No (negative) | **N** |
| 4 | Yes (affirmative) | **Y** |
| 5 | Proceeding in this direction | **→** |

### 24.17 Ground/Air from Search Parties

Ground search parties have a separate code to report the status of their operation, whether personnel have been found, and the direction of further movement or possible aircraft location.

| No. | Message | Code symbol |
|---:|---|:---:|
| 1 | Operation completed | **LLL** |
| 2 | We have found all personnel | **L̲L̲** (underlined LL) |
| 3 | We have found only some personnel | **+ +** |
| 4 | We are not able to continue; returning to base | **XX** |
| 5 | Have divided into two groups, each proceeding in the indicated direction | Diverging directional-arrow symbol |
| 6 | Information received that the aircraft is in the indicated direction | **→ →** |
| 7 | Nothing found; will continue to search | **NN** |

### 24.18 Air to Ground Signals

An aircraft acknowledges that it has **understood** a ground-to-air signal as follows:

- **In daylight:** Rock the aircraft's wings.
- **During normal darkness:** Switch the **landing lights** on and off **twice**. If the aircraft does not have landing lights, use its **navigation lights**, likewise switching them on and off **twice**.

If the aircraft does not make the prescribed acknowledgement, the ground party is to take this as indicating that its message **has not been understood**.

### 24.19 Marking of Droppable Supplies

Packages released from an aircraft for survivors use **colour-coded streamers** to identify the type of supplies they contain. The coding specified in **Doc 7333** is:

| Streamer colour | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Red** | Medical supplies and first-aid equipment. |
| **Blue** | Food and water. |
| **Yellow** | Blankets and protective clothing. |
| **Black** | Other equipment, including stoves, axes and cooking utensils. |

### 24.20 Emergency Frequencies

The designated communications frequencies for **distress, urgency and safety** traffic are:

| Service | Frequency | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| **VHF** | **121.500 MHz** | Aeronautical mobile VHF emergency communications. |
| **UHF** | **243.000 MHz** | Aeronautical mobile UHF emergency communications. |
| **HF** | **2182 kHz** | International maritime HF distress and calling. |
| **SARSAT** | **406 MHz** | Search-and-rescue beacon transmission; the beacons described also transmit on **121.5 MHz**. |

c. Medical assistance is required.

b. Command transfers automatically from 1 to 2 and then to 3.

d. Only during periods of military and commercial flying within the FIR.
