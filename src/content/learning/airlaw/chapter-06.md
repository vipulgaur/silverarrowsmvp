# Chapter 6: Rules of the Air

## 1. History and Legal Application

### 6.1 Development of the Rules of the Air

Air traffic rules developed alongside aviation itself. Aircraft in the earliest years had no practical means of radio communication, so pilots relied on visual observation, ground signals, predictable manoeuvres and established local procedures. Speeds of approximately **80–90 kt** were already sufficient to create serious collision risks in the 1920s. Individual countries gradually introduced their own enforceable arrangements during the years between the world wars. The expansion of international commercial flying made these different national systems increasingly difficult to reconcile. Harmonization became a major concern at the **Chicago Conference of 1944**.

### 6.2 ICAO Annex 2 and Its Applicability

**Annex 2 to the Chicago Convention** sets out the ICAO Rules of the Air. Many rules still accommodate aircraft without radio equipment, and they remain relevant if communications fail. The general position for an aircraft registered in an ICAO Contracting State is that ICAO's rules follow the aircraft unless they conflict with those of the State whose territory is being overflown. Over the **high seas**, meaning waters beyond the territorial jurisdiction of individual States, ICAO's rules apply **without exception**. Annex 2 was adopted in **April 1948**, with Amendment 1 adopted in **November 1951**.

For example, a UK-registered aircraft follows UK rules over the UK. Over France it must comply with applicable French requirements, which take precedence where the French and UK rules differ. When operating above the high seas, it follows ICAO rules. Historical UK requirements are published in **CAP 393 / the Air Navigation Order (ANO)**. A national rule is not necessarily identical to the corresponding international rule; the distinction between a rule and the law that gives it effect must be kept in mind.

### 6.3 Rules Applicable to Aircraft Operations

An aircraft operating **in flight or on an aerodrome movement area** must observe the general rules. Flight additionally takes place under one of two regimes:

- **Visual Flight Rules (VFR)**, when applicable visual meteorological conditions (VMC) exist; or
- **Instrument Flight Rules (IFR)**.

### 6.4 Selecting IFR or VFR

A pilot can elect to fly IFR even in VMC, and ATS may require IFR in particular circumstances. Conversely, ordinary VFR depends on the applicable VMC being maintained. A flight in IMC must be operated under IFR, except for the specifically authorized arrangements described later for **special VFR** in a control zone.

## 2. Visual Meteorological Conditions and Flight Responsibility

### 6.5 What Constitutes VMC

VMC depends on **flight visibility**, **height or flight level**, **horizontal distance from cloud**, **vertical distance from cloud**, and **airspace classification**. Flight visibility means visibility forward from an aircraft's flight deck in flight. Airspace classes determine which flight rules and ATS provisions apply. The VMC limits must be distinguished from the separate weather conditions used for special VFR.

### 6.6 VMC Criteria in Airspace Classes A, B, C, D and E

| Level | Flight visibility | Horizontal distance from cloud | Vertical distance from cloud |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| At or above **FL100 / 10,000 ft** | **8 km** | **1,500 m** | **300 m (1,000 ft)** |
| Below **FL100 / 10,000 ft** | **5 km** | **1,500 m** | **300 m (1,000 ft)** |

The cloud-distance values are the same in both rows; the visibility requirement changes at FL100.

### 6.8 Crew Roles

- **Commander:** the qualified pilot designated to command the aircraft, even if control of the flight is delegated to another suitably qualified pilot.
- **Pilot-in-command (PIC):** the pilot bearing responsibility for aircraft operation and safety throughout flight time.
- **Pilot Flying (PF):** the person currently manipulating or directing the flying controls.
- **Pilot Not Flying (PNF):** the other crew member assisting the PF under the multi-crew cooperation arrangement, where more than one pilot is required.

### 6.9 Pre-flight and In-flight Responsibility

The PIC must study all information pertinent to the planned flight before departure. For IFR flights and departures away from an aerodrome, this includes available weather reports and forecasts, fuel planning, and an alternative course of action if the flight cannot be completed as intended. The commander retains final authority over the aircraft while in command. Departure from the Rules of the Air is justified only when **absolutely necessary for flight safety**.

### 6.10 Alcohol, Narcotics and Psychoactive Drugs

No person should pilot an aircraft or act as flight crew while impaired by alcohol, narcotics or other drugs. The chapter distinguishes ICAO's broad prohibition on impairment from the historical **JAR-OPS 1** blood-alcohol limit of **0.2 promille**, equivalent to **20 mg per 100 mL** of blood. The consequences of impaired flying are particularly serious because aircrew are responsible for the safety of those aboard. ICAO also prohibits flight-crew duties while affected by psychotropic substances.

## 3. General Rules

### 6.11 Minimum Height Over Congested Areas and Assemblies

Except where take-off, landing or permission from the relevant authority requires otherwise, an aircraft must remain sufficiently high over cities, towns, settlements and open-air gatherings to permit an emergency landing **without undue danger to people or property** on the ground. This general provision does **not** establish one universal numeric height and should not be confused with the separate numerical minima for IFR and VFR. In these rules, the generic term **level** can mean a height, an altitude or a flight level.

### 6.12 Cruising Levels and Altitudes

At or above the lowest usable flight level, or above the relevant **transition altitude**, cruising is expressed using **flight levels**. Below that lowest usable flight level, or at/below the transition altitude as applicable, cruising is described in **altitudes**. The word **level** remains an umbrella term for flight level, altitude or height.

### 6.13 Collision Avoidance and Right of Way

Aircraft must never be flown close enough to others to create a collision hazard. An aircraft having right of way should retain **heading and speed**, but the PIC must still take action if needed to avoid collision, including in response to an ACAS alert. The aircraft required to give way must remain well clear and must not pass over, under or in front of the other so closely that danger or wake turbulence could result.

### 6.14 Aircraft Approaching Head-on

When two aircraft approach one another directly or almost directly and collision is possible, **both turn right**. Neither has preferential right of way.

### 6.15 Converging Flight Paths

For converging aircraft **of the same category** and at approximately the same level, the aircraft seeing the other **on its right** gives way; the usual avoidance manoeuvre is to turn right and pass **behind**. Different aircraft categories follow a hierarchy:

1. Powered heavier-than-air aircraft yield to **airships, gliders and balloons**.
2. Airships yield to **gliders and balloons**.
3. Gliders yield to **balloons**.
4. Powered aircraft yield to another aircraft **towing an aircraft or object**.

A towing combination is treated operationally as one connected flying unit controlled by the towing PIC.

### 6.16 Overtaking

An aircraft is overtaking when it closes from behind within **70° of either side of the aircraft's longitudinal plane**. From the rear at night, the overtaker would see the other aircraft's **white tail light**. The aircraft being passed has right of way; the overtaking aircraft, whether climbing, descending or level, alters course **to the right** and remains clear until the overtaking manoeuvre is complete.

### 6.17 Landing Priority

Aircraft in flight or on the ground/water give way to aircraft **landing or in the final approach stage**. If two heavier-than-air aircraft are approaching an aerodrome at different heights, the **higher** aircraft gives way to the **lower**. However, the lower aircraft must not exploit this rule to cut in ahead. Powered heavier-than-air aircraft yield to a **glider** approaching to land.

### 6.18 Emergency Landing

An aircraft known to be in an emergency and required to land is given priority by other aircraft.

### 6.19 Taking Off

Aircraft taxiing on an aerodrome manoeuvring area yield to aircraft **taking off or about to take off**.

### 6.20 Lighted Stop Bars

A taxiing aircraft must stop at an **illuminated stop bar** and may move ahead only once the lights are extinguished, as prescribed for operations in reduced visibility.

### 6.21 Avoidance During Surface Movement

When aircraft taxi near one another:

- **Head-on:** both stop, or both turn right where practicable.
- **Converging:** the aircraft with the other on its right gives way, typically by stopping or passing behind.
- **Overtaking:** the overtaking aircraft remains clear; the aircraft being overtaken retains priority.

Annex 2 requires vehicles regularly using the manoeuvring area to maintain two-way radio contact with ATC. The historical UK ANO provision cited in the course additionally requires aircraft and other manoeuvring-area traffic to yield to **vehicles towing aircraft**.

### 6.22–6.27 Aircraft Lighting Rules

The main distinction is between **navigation lights** (show direction of aircraft movement and orientation) and **anti-collision lights** (attract attention). Other lights should not be shown if they might be confused with required navigation lights.

- **6.23 From sunset to sunrise** or during other periods specified by the authority, aircraft show the required navigation and anti-collision lights.
- **6.24 On a movement area at night**, moving aircraft show navigation lights. Aircraft must also display lights marking their structural extremities unless parked and otherwise adequately illuminated, as can be the case on a lit apron. Ground marker or “glim” lights may mark aircraft parked outside the apron.
- **6.25 Engines running:** aircraft on the movement area should show lights that make them conspicuous; red anti-collision lights may indicate running engines, and illumination of the beacon commonly warns ground staff that the aircraft is occupied or may start.
- **6.26 Day as well as night:** anti-collision lights fitted to an aircraft in flight must be illuminated even during daylight.
- **6.27 Dazzle prevention:** the pilot may reduce intensity or turn off flashing lights if they impair duties or create harmful glare for others.

### 6.28 Simulated Instrument Meteorological Conditions

A pilot can practise with restricted external vision using a visor, hood or screens, but the aircraft must actually be in **VMC**. This removes the PF's outside lookout, so safeguards must compensate:

- Functioning **dual controls** are required.
- A **qualified safety pilot** occupies a control seat; the chapter specifies that this person does not necessarily have to be type-rated.
- The safety pilot must be able to see suitably ahead and to both sides. If that view is inadequate, a capable observer communicating with the safety pilot must occupy a position that fills the visual coverage gap. The observer must know how to identify airborne traffic, describe contacts clearly and operate the aircraft intercommunication system.

### 6.29 Operating Near Aerodromes

A pilot should know whether the aircraft is in the vicinity of an aerodrome and monitor other traffic, whether or not within a designated aerodrome traffic zone. Operations must:

- Avoid conflicts by observing the other aerodrome traffic.
- Follow the established traffic pattern or remain clear of it.
- Use **left-hand** circuit turns after take-off and on approach unless another pattern or instruction applies.
- Normally take off and land **into wind**, subject to runway, traffic and safety considerations.

## 4. Flight Plans, ATC Clearances and Flight Reporting

### 6.30 When a Flight Plan Is Required

Submission of a flight plan is required for the following types of operations:

1. A flight, or a part of a flight, which is to receive an **air traffic control service**.
2. An **IFR flight in advisory airspace**.
3. A flight on specified routes or through designated areas for which the appropriate ATS authority requires a flight plan to support **flight information, alerting or search and rescue**.
4. A flight for which authorities require coordination with **military units**, such as in an Air Defence Identification Zone (ADIZ), or neighbouring States' ATS units to prevent avoidable interception.
5. Any flight crossing an **international boundary**, irrespective of whether an FIR boundary is crossed.

A flight plan is submitted to an ATS reporting office before departure, or transmitted in flight to the appropriate ATS unit or air-ground control station. Repetitive flight plans may be arranged separately. Unless ATS specifies otherwise, a flight plan for a controlled flight must arrive **at least 60 minutes before departure**. When filed airborne, it must be received by the appropriate ATS unit at least **10 minutes before** entry into a control/advisory area or crossing an airway/advisory route. Additional requirements may apply to entry into an oceanic control area.

The relevant flight-plan information consists of aircraft identification; intended flight rules and type of flight; number/type of aircraft and wake-turbulence category; equipment; departure aerodrome and **estimated off-block time (EOBT)**; cruising speed (**TAS or Mach number**), cruising level and route; destination and **estimated elapsed time (EET)**; alternates; fuel endurance; persons on board (including any deceased persons); emergency and survival equipment; and other required details. These are information categories, not a numbered list of flight-plan form fields.

Unless a deviation was inadvertent, proposed changes to the flight plan for an IFR flight or controlled VFR flight must be reported to ATS as soon as practicable. Significant changes to other VFR plans are also reported. Incorrect fuel endurance or persons-on-board information at departure constitutes a **significant change**.

### 6.31 Repetitive Flight Plans (RPL)

An RPL is intended for a series of recurrent flights that share stable essential details. The operations must take place on the **same day or days of successive weeks on at least 10 occasions**, or **daily over at least 10 consecutive days**. The operator submits the information for the ATS unit to retain and reuse. Material variation in the flight details is not compatible with treating the flights as a single unchanged pattern.

### 6.32 Closing a Flight Plan

Where a report is required, flight-plan closure normally follows notification of arrival to the appropriate ATS unit, whether in person, by radio or through data link, as soon as practicable after landing. If suitable communications at the destination are unavailable, an equivalent arrival message should be transmitted by an alternative method. Failure to close a plan can trigger costly and unnecessary search-and-rescue action. The arrival report contains **aircraft identification, departure aerodrome, destination aerodrome, actual arrival aerodrome and arrival time**.

### 6.33 Delayed Departure

When the actual estimated off-block time exceeds the planned EOBT by **30 minutes for a controlled flight** or **one hour for an uncontrolled flight** with a filed plan, the operator/pilot must arrange for that flight plan to be amended or cancelled and replaced.

### 6.34 Time Convention

Use **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)**, expressed in hours and minutes on the **24-hour clock** from midnight. The terms **Zulu** and **GMT** may still be heard. An ATC time check is reported to the nearest minute; for example, **09:41:20 → 0941**, while **09:41:40 → 0942**.

### 6.35 Structure and Purpose of an ATC Clearance

Five principal elements can form a clearance: **aircraft identification; clearance limit; route; level(s); and other instructions** such as validity time, departures or communications. Clearances support traffic separation and orderly movement, taking account of air traffic and movement-area vehicles. The clearance is not a guarantee that the aircraft is safe from every obstacle or other hazard.

### 6.36 Obtaining and Reading Back a Clearance

A clearance is required before conducting a controlled flight or controlled portion of one. If the authorization is unsuitable, the PIC may **request an amended clearance**; ATS will issue one where practicable. Departing aircraft usually receive clearance before take-off, sometimes through a separate **DELIVERY** frequency.

A clearance should be copied accurately and read back **as received**. The controller may ask whether the pilot is ready to copy. Any uncertainty, incorrectly copied route point or questionable instruction should be queried and resolved before acting. The chapter emphasizes that the person relaying the clearance may not be the controller who originally approved it, making accurate readback an important error trap.

### 6.37 Route Terminology Used in Clearances

- **“Cleared by flight planned route”** means the complete route, or part of it, coincides with the filed route.
- **“Cleared via [designation] departure”** refers to a published **Standard Instrument Departure (SID)**.
- **“Cleared via [designation] arrival”** refers to a published **Standard Instrument Arrival Route (STAR)**.

The cleared route should be explicitly identified when necessary.

### 6.38 Compliance With the Filed/Current Flight Plan

A controlled flight follows the current flight plan unless an emergency requires immediate deviation, in which case ATS is informed as promptly as possible. If cruise **true airspeed changes, or is expected to change, by ±5%** relative to the planned value, ATC must be notified. A revised estimate that differs by **more than three minutes** for the next report point, FIR boundary or destination must also be passed. The historical MNPSA operational note specifies **three minutes or more** in that airspace.

### 6.39 Inadvertent Route Deviation

Controlled aircraft normally follow the airway centreline or the defined track between navigational beacons. If a designated changeover point is provided between VOR facilities, use it unless instructed otherwise; such points are commonly about **60 NM** apart. Notify ATC about deviations, and take prompt action to regain the planned track after any unintentional departure from it.

### 6.40 Weather Becomes Too Poor for VFR

If conditions no longer allow compliance with the VMC requirement for the cleared route, the pilot should take action **before entering IMC**. Available measures include asking for a new clearance permitting a VMC route to destination or an alternate, or permitting exit from controlled airspace. If a revised clearance is unavailable, advise ATS whether the aircraft will leave the airspace or land at a suitable aerodrome. Within a **control zone (CTR)**, special VFR clearance may be requested. If these choices are unsuitable and the aircraft/pilot can legally fly IFR, request an IFR clearance.

### 6.41 Position Reports

A controlled aircraft provides reports at required reporting points unless released from that obligation, as commonly happens under radar control. A complete report normally states, in order:

1. Aircraft radiotelephony identification.
2. Reporting position.
3. Time over that position.
4. Present level (altitude or flight level).
5. Next reporting point and estimated time over it.
6. The **subsequent/ensuing reporting point**.

If Mode C altitude information has been confirmed correct, ATS may permit omission of the level. An ATC-assigned speed should be included. An example using the specified sequence is: **“London Airways, GABCD, Pole Hill at 35, FL170, Dean Cross at 46, Glasgow next.”**

### 6.42 Ending Controlled Flight

Advise the ATS unit when control service no longer applies, such as after leaving controlled airspace when requested. At a controlled aerodrome this transition normally occurs as part of landing and the service provided by the tower.

### 6.43 Radiocommunications Requirements

Controlled aircraft required to maintain two-way communications observe the provisions of **ICAO Annex 10, Volume II** for aeronautical telecommunications. The chapter also references **SELCAL** and notes that availability of **controller–pilot data link communications (CPDLC)** does not remove the applicable requirement to maintain voice communication capability.

### 6.44 How ATC Checks Whether Communications Have Failed

ATS may request an aircraft to perform a radar-observable turn or acknowledge by a specified transmitted signal. If communications still appear lost, ATS can broadcast **blind instructions** on frequencies believed to be monitored by that aircraft.

### 6.45 Failure in the Circuit at a Controlled Aerodrome

An aircraft with inoperative transmitter or receiver flying in a controlled aerodrome traffic pattern must watch for **tower light and ground signals** and respond accordingly.

### 6.46 ATS Separation Assumption

Once a radio failure is established, ATC separates other traffic on the understanding that the affected aircraft will use the appropriate published **VMC or IMC lost-communications procedure**.

### 6.47 Radio Failure While in VMC

Whether the flight was originally IFR or VFR, continue in **VMC**, land at the **nearest suitable aerodrome**, and notify the appropriate ATS unit of arrival by the quickest available means.

### 6.48 Radio Failure While in IMC

The chapter specifies distinct actions according to the type of ATS environment:

- **While being radar vectored:** select **Mode A 7600**, rejoin the current flight-plan route by the most direct practical path **no later than the next significant point**, and respect minimum flight altitudes.
- **En route within radar-controlled airspace:** select **7600**, retain the current flight-plan speed/level conditions for **seven minutes**, then adjust speed and level to match the **filed** flight plan.
- **En route without radar control:** retain the current flight-plan conditions for **20 minutes after failing to report at a compulsory reporting point**, then make the speed and level adjustments needed to conform with the filed plan. This time allows ATS to accommodate the change without immediate conflict.

### 6.49 Actions After the Initial Lost-Communications Period

Proceed on the filed flight-plan route to the designated navigation aid serving the destination. If necessary, hold there until the appropriate descent time. Begin descent at, or as close as practical to, the **last acknowledged expected approach time (EAT)**. If no EAT was received and acknowledged, use the **ETA resulting from the current flight plan**. Fly the instrument approach applicable to that aid and, if possible, land **within 30 minutes after the later of that ETA or last acknowledged EAT**. Report to ATS as soon as practical after landing.

## 5. Unlawful Interference and Interception

### 6.50 Unlawful Interference

Where an aircraft is subject to hijacking or another unlawful act, the crew should try to advise the appropriate ATS unit, describe important circumstances and report intended route deviations so that the aircraft can receive priority and conflicts can be minimized. Normally the aircraft should remain on its **assigned track and level** until ATS can be contacted or radar coverage is available.

If compelled to deviate without being able to notify ATS, the source procedure calls for warning transmissions on **121.500 MHz** when possible and appropriate use of other systems, including **Mode A 7500** or data link, while observing any applicable regional in-flight contingency procedures published in **Doc 7030**. If no regional provisions apply, the source specifies a level offset from standard IFR levels of **1,000 ft above FL290**, or **500 ft below FL290**.

### 6.51 Legal Basis for Interception

A State may establish procedures to intercept and identify aircraft within its territorial airspace, particularly if an aircraft enters without authorization or penetrates sensitive or prohibited airspace. Interception visual signals must comply with **Appendix 1 of ICAO Annex 2**, while the intercepted civil aircraft follows the responses and operating standards in **Appendix 2**. Standardization is intended to improve safety during a potentially hazardous encounter.

### 6.52 Interception Information Carried on International Flights

The course describes a national requirement for internationally operated aircraft to carry the interception signal tables. They are intended to be referred to during an interception; recognition of what the tables contain is more important than memorizing every visual action.

### 6.53 Immediate Action When Intercepted

An intercepted pilot should follow the visual instructions and return the prescribed signals, notify ATS if possible, and try to establish communications with the interceptor or interception control unit by making a general call on **121.500 MHz**. If necessary and practicable, repeat the call on **243.000 MHz**. If equipped with a transponder, set **Mode A 7700 with Mode C**, unless otherwise instructed by the relevant ATS unit.

### 6.54 Conflicting Instructions or Language Barriers

When an interceptor and intercepted aircraft cannot use a common spoken language, employ the standard interception words and pronunciations. If an ATS instruction conflicts with the interceptor's direction, request prompt clarification **while continuing to follow the interceptor's instructions**.

### 6.56 ATS Duties During an Interception

If interception occurs in its own airspace, ATS should attempt communication with the intercepted aircraft, including via **121.5 MHz**; inform that pilot about the interception if necessary; contact the ATS unit communicating with the interceptor; exchange relevant aircraft details; relay messages; coordinate measures to protect the intercepted aircraft; and warn ATS units in neighbouring FIRs when needed. If interception occurs in another unit's airspace, ATS instead informs the responsible unit, passes information useful for identification and relays necessary messages.

## 6. Visual Flight Rules

### 6.59 Remaining in Visual Meteorological Conditions

Apart from an authorized **SVFR** operation, VFR is permissible only while the appropriate VMC criteria are maintained. If visibility or cloud conditions deteriorate, the pilot must select a means to remain compliant: **land in VMC**, divert or change course to stay in VMC, arrange an IFR flight plan/clearance and operate under IFR if qualified, or request SVFR within a control zone.

### 6.60 VFR Take-off and Landing in a CTR

Without specific ATC authorization, VFR aircraft must not take off from or land at an aerodrome in a CTR, nor enter its aerodrome traffic zone or traffic circuit, if **cloud ceiling is below 450 m (1,500 ft)** or **ground visibility below 5 km**.

### 6.61 Restrictions on VFR

VFR between sunset and sunrise, or in other specified periods, is subject to the authority's conditions. ATC authorization is required for VFR **above FL200** and for flights at **transonic or supersonic speed**. Under the source RVSM rules, **VFR above FL285** is not permitted in RVSM airspace.

Except for take-off, landing or special authorization, a VFR aircraft may not fly over a city, town, settlement or open-air crowd lower than **300 m (1,000 ft)** above the highest obstacle within **600 m** of the aircraft. Elsewhere the stated minimum is **150 m (500 ft)** above the ground or water.

### 6.62 VFR Cruising Flight Levels

In normal level cruise above the transition altitude, VFR flight levels are selected according to **magnetic track** and the semi-circular rule. The source notes that the transition altitude is commonly about **3,000 ft AGL**, although the applicable published altitude controls. For controlled VFR, the level actually flown follows the ATC clearance. Compliance with ATS instructions is required for VFR in airspace classes **B, C and D**, in controlled aerodrome traffic and during SVFR operations; VFR is not generally permitted in **Class A**.

### 6.63 VFR Flight Plans and Rule Changes

Enter **V** in flight-plan Item 8 for a VFR flight throughout. If the flight begins VFR and changes to IFR, enter **Z**. A controlled VFR flight requires a flight plan. For a VFR-to-IFR change, the pilot updates the submitted plan or files an IFR plan as appropriate and obtains the required IFR clearance before operating under IFR in controlled airspace.

### 6.64 VFR Estimated Elapsed Time

For VFR, the estimated elapsed time in **Item 16** runs from **take-off to overhead the destination aerodrome**.

### 6.65 Communication Requirements for VFR

Controlled VFR flights and VFR flights in designated ATS airspace establish and maintain **two-way RTF** and submit position reports as applicable. In **Class E, F and G** airspace, VFR can take place without two-way radio where not otherwise required.

## 7. Instrument Flight Rules

### 6.66 IFR and the Instrument Rating

IMC means conditions falling below the defined VMC minima. Operations under IFR follow instrument-flight rules. Under the cited **ICAO Annex 1 personnel-licensing** provision, a licence alone does not authorize service as PIC or co-pilot under IFR: the holder must also have a valid **instrument rating** applicable to the aircraft category.

### 6.67 Flight Instruments and Navigation Equipment

The aircraft must have the instruments and navigational equipment suited to the intended flight path and to IFR operations, as detailed in the operating requirements cited in the chapter (**JAR-OPS 1**).

### 6.68 IFR Minimum Levels

The applicable State's published minimum flight altitude must be respected, except during necessary take-off/landing operations or where specially authorized. Where no published minimum applies, the chapter's clearance criteria are:

- **Mountainous/high terrain:** at least **600 m (2,000 ft)** above the highest obstacle within **8 km (5 NM)** of the aircraft's estimated position.
- **Other terrain:** at least **300 m (1,000 ft)** above the highest obstacle within **8 km (5 NM)** of the aircraft's estimated position.

### 6.69 IFR Flight-plan Designators

Enter **I** in Item 8 for IFR throughout. A flight that begins IFR but plans to change to VFR uses **Y**; the planned transition is therefore identified as **I → V = Y**.

### 6.70 Cancelling IFR in Favour of VFR

If a flight plan did not already specify the change, a pilot who wishes to continue under VFR reports **“cancelling my IFR flight”** and supplies the necessary flight-plan changes. ATC acknowledges with **“IFR cancelled at [time]”**. Seeing a temporary improvement to VMC is not sufficient justification: the pilot must expect and intend to remain in uninterrupted VMC for **a reasonable period**. The change from IFR to VFR is initiated **by the pilot, not ordered by ATC**.

### 6.71 IFR Estimated Elapsed Time

For IFR, the Item 16 EET extends from **take-off to overhead the initial approach fix (IAF)** of the destination's instrument procedure, not merely to the destination airport itself.

### 6.72 IFR Within Controlled Airspace

Controlled IFR flights observe ATC's route and level instructions. Cruise levels, or permitted **cruise-climb bands**, are derived from the ICAO table of levels or, where applicable, from the modified table for altitudes above **FL410**.

### 6.73 IFR Outside Controlled Airspace

Outside controlled airspace, IFR aircraft use a cruising level consistent with **magnetic track**. In the designated areas or routes that require flight plans, the crew contacts the unit providing a **flight information service (FIS)**, maintains the required listening watch and provides position reports.

For operations away from ATS routes or within specified operating areas, the chapter describes an initial report **30 minutes after leaving controlled airspace or beginning the operation**, followed by **hourly** reports. When ordinary positional information has little meaning, an **“operations normal”** report at hourly intervals prevents unnecessary activation of alerting services. Example: **“London Control, GADRF, operations normal at 1020, 2000 ft and below; will call again at 1120.”**

## 9. Special VFR

### 6.76 Origin of Special VFR

Special VFR arose as airspace restrictions expanded around major aerodromes. The historical example in this chapter concerns **RAF Northolt**, near London Heathrow, where a visual corridor allowed traffic unable to meet normal IFR requirements to continue with ground in sight while remaining clear of cloud. The arrangement was later extended to nearby **Denham** and incorporated into internationally recognized airspace procedures.

### 6.77 Meaning and Weather Minima

**Special VFR (SVFR)** is a VFR flight specifically cleared by ATC to operate in a **control zone (CTR)** despite weather below normal VMC minima. The cited ICAO criterion requires CTR ground visibility of at least **1,500 m** for an SVFR arrival, departure, crossing or local operation. The historical **JAR-OPS 1** condition given in the chapter is more restrictive, requiring **3,000 m** visibility to commence such a flight.

## 10. Distress, Urgency and Special Transponder Codes

### 6.78 Distress Signals

A **distress** condition exists when the aircraft or vessel faces **grave and imminent danger** and requires immediate help. An RTF distress call begins with **“MAYDAY” spoken three times**. The visual aircraft distress signals cited here include a sequence of **red pyrotechnic discharges** and a **red parachute flare**.

### 6.79 Urgency Signals

An **urgency** message concerns the safety of a ship, aircraft, vehicle, other property or a person aboard or in sight, but does not necessarily involve grave and imminent danger requiring immediate rescue. Prefix the radiotelephony message **“PAN PAN” repeated three times**.

### 6.80 Serious Malfunction Without Request for Assistance

An aircraft that needs to land promptly because of equipment trouble or communications failure but is **not requesting other assistance** may repeatedly switch the landing lights, or other available lights, **on and off** to indicate its situation.

Pilots at or near an aerodrome must watch for visual signals from the tower, including when normal radio communication is available. ### 6.86 Acknowledging Tower Signals

An aircraft can acknowledge a visual command using its controls or lights:

- **In flight during daylight:** rock the wings.
