# Chapter 17 — Control of Aircraft

## Procedural ATC

### 17.1 Concept

Air traffic control became a substantial part of aviation operations mainly in the period following the Second World War. Wartime experience had demonstrated the dangers of conducting large numbers of aircraft movements around aerodromes: aircraft collided in flight, struck obstructions and sometimes flew unintentionally into the ground. Control towers consequently began managing traffic at aerodromes. Outside the limits of the aerodrome—an area subsequently represented by the aerodrome traffic zone (ATZ)—the assistance available was initially little more than monitoring a flight to confirm that the aircraft remained airborne.

A collision involving two Constellation aeroplanes above the Grand Canyon in the United States during the early 1950s brought wider attention to the possibility of aircraft encountering each other even across an immense volume of airspace. The response included the development of directional eastbound and westbound routes across the continental United States. These arrangements provided an early form of **procedural ATC**: aircraft cruising at similar heights were assigned different geographical tracks so that lateral distance kept them apart.

Controllers could not continuously observe the positions of the aircraft. Instead, pilots transmitted reports describing where they were; controllers plotted these reports, followed each flight's estimated progress and instructed changes of track when the plots suggested a possible conflict. Navigation and position-fixing facilities were comparatively imprecise, radio links were unreliable by present-day standards, and supporting material—including meteorological information—was extremely limited. Traffic volumes were small enough, however, for generous separation buffers to compensate for the uncertainty in aircraft positions and delays in communication.

### 17.2 Flight Strips

The traditional tool for tracking individual flights in an ATC centre is the **flight progress strip**, a paper record prepared from the ATS flight plan. As responsibility for an aircraft passes between air traffic control officers (ATCOs) or between control centres, the corresponding flight information must pass with it. Within a single centre, the strip itself may move from one controller's position to another. Between two centres, transfer may instead take the form of a telephone exchange, enabling the receiving centre to prepare its own strip rather than physically receiving the original.

This straightforward paper process has proved exceptionally dependable. ATC research organisations, including Eurocontrol's establishment at Brétigny, spent the preceding two decades investigating systems that could replace it electronically.

### 17.3 Procedural Separation

The applicable procedural separation requirements are set out in Chapter 16.

### 17.4 Communications

Air–ground radio contact has accompanied the development of radio-equipped aircraft. Modern ATC depends largely on two-way **VHF** radiotelephony. **HF** communication remains important over oceans and sparsely served land areas; **SELCAL** allows an aircraft crew to remain available for a call without continuously listening to the HF channel.

Both aircraft and ATC units are addressed by radio callsigns; **Oxford Approach** is an example of a unit callsign. If two aircraft callsigns are identical or liable to be confused, ATC may instruct one aircraft to use a different callsign temporarily.

## Radar Control

### 17.5 Concept

The principal benefit of radar to ATC is its contribution to more accurate aircraft separation. A radar-equipped controller can, in principle, establish an aircraft's position more precisely than the pilot can report it. This advantage is being reshaped by satellite navigation technologies capable of positional precision on the order of **±30 cm**.

Different radar installations serve different areas and tasks. Long-range surveillance supports area control of en-route flights; **terminal aerodrome radar (TAR)** serves arriving and departing aircraft around one or more aerodromes; and **surface movement radar** helps aerodrome controllers monitor aircraft and other movement-area activity at night or when visibility is poor.

A system that detects aircraft by their returned radar echoes and displays those returns is **primary surveillance radar (PSR)**. **Secondary surveillance radar (SSR)** can supplement radar surveillance with information identifying a particular flight and indicating its altitude. Radar is also capable of supporting both precision and non-precision instrument approaches.

### 17.6 Radar Services

In a procedural environment, radar information may simply provide more up-to-date positions for the controller's flight strips. That use does not fundamentally change the procedural control method. Once a particular return has been associated positively with an individual aircraft, however, the controller can use the display to establish separation directly between that aircraft and other radar contacts, at substantially greater positional accuracy.

The forms of service described here are:

- **Radar control:** control of en-route aircraft operating under a controlled flight.
- **Approach radar control:** radar assistance in controlling arrivals and departures.
- **Radar vectoring:** headings or other navigation directions supplied for a specified purpose, such as bringing an aircraft onto the ILS localizer.
- **PAR and SRA:** radar-supported instrument approach procedures.

Radar control services are provided within **controlled airspace (CAS)**. Radar information may nevertheless support **flight information service (FIS)** outside CAS under ICAO provisions and may assist the **air traffic advisory service** in Class F airspace. An aerodrome controller may consult radar to assess the distance between departures but does not use that equipment to apply the departure separation itself.

### 17.7 Radar Separation

The standard radar separation distance is **5 NM**. Chapter 16 describes the particular circumstances in which a smaller minimum is permissible. For the separation discussed here, the controller uses aircraft positions obtained from **PSR**, rather than treating another source as the basis for the radar minimum. Radar-based position information can also be used to apply the distance minima required for wake-turbulence separation, as specified in Chapter 16.

## Radar Identification

### 17.8 Requirement

A radar controller must first establish which displayed contact corresponds to the aircraft concerned; a radar service cannot begin before this identification has been achieved. Normally, the simplest and fastest method is to assign a discrete **SSR code** and associate the reply carrying that code with the aircraft. The pilot must continue selecting the assigned code unless the controller instructs otherwise. Where an aircraft is already positively identified by SSR and an emergency develops, the pilot should not automatically change to **Mode A 7700**.

When identification is achieved by observing a radar return rather than solely by using its allocated SSR code, the controller may establish the association from the aircraft's position or a distinctive manoeuvre. Acceptable means described in this chapter include:

- A geographical report, such as a position **2 NM west of Woodstock**.
- A fix described relative to a navigation aid, for instance the **230° radial of Daventry VOR at 5 NM DME**.
- Coordinates stated as **latitude and longitude**.
- A **GEOREF** position.
- An observed turn of **30° or more** away from the intended course, followed by a return to that course.
- An explicit handover from another radar controller who has already identified the aircraft.

### 17.9 Procedure

Following positive identification, the controller informs the pilot of the radar identification and provides the position or other information showing how the identification was established. The expression **“radar contact”** means that the aircraft has been identified on the radar display and will receive a service until advised otherwise. One possible transmission is **“G-CD, radar contact, 2 NM west of Oxford.”**

The position supplied by ATC should be consistent with the pilot's estimate of position. A material discrepancy must be reported promptly, since the controller may have selected the wrong radar contact.

## Radar Service

### 17.10 Commencement

After a radar identification has been established, ATC advises the aircraft of the kind of radar service being initiated and its intended result. A transmission might be: **“G-CD, radar contact, 6 NM west of Compton, radar control, expect radar vectors for ILS approach runway 26.”**

### 17.11 Termination

The service ends when the aircraft leaves available radar coverage, reaches the boundary beyond which vectoring cannot continue, or has achieved the task for which radar assistance was provided. ATC then informs the pilot that radar service has ended, states the aircraft's location and gives appropriate onward instructions or recommendations. For example: **“G-CD, radar service terminated, presently 10 NM south of Benson; resume own navigation; suggest continuing with London Information on 125.650.”**

### 17.12 Radar Vectoring

During **radar vectoring**, a controller directs an aircraft's navigation in order to produce a required ground track. The purpose may be to keep clear of weather, pass safely around another radar contact or establish an appropriate position for a straight-in instrument approach. Once the purpose has been fulfilled, the instruction **“resume own navigation”**—normally accompanied by a position and any further relevant directions—ends the vectoring and returns navigation to the pilot.

For approach-control tasks, vectors may be issued only within a designated **radar vectoring area (RVA)**. The chart for that area identifies obstacles and the protected operating limits.

### 17.13 Vectoring Procedure

The contact must be confirmed **inside the RVA** before vectoring commences. A representation of the RVA is shown on the controller's radar display as an electronically produced video map. To allow for discrepancies between the displayed chart and actual radar positions—often described as map **slippage**—vectors must keep an aircraft at least **half the relevant radar separation minimum or 2.5 NM**, whichever is greater, inside the area's boundary.

The usual starting point is a navigation fix at the **minimum sector altitude (MSA)**. Vectoring can also begin elsewhere once the aircraft has been identified on radar and its altitude is known to the controller. Terrain elevations and obstacles are depicted on the RVA chart. The controller issues **magnetic headings** chosen to establish the desired track over the ground, and makes further adjustments to compensate for wind; the pilot follows the assigned headings rather than independently applying a wind correction.

Descent below the **RVA safe altitude**—the height of the highest obstacle within the area plus the **minimum obstacle clearance (MOC)**, rounded upward—is not authorized by vectoring alone. It becomes permissible only after the aircraft is established on the ILS localizer course or another final approach track, or after the pilot reports continuing visually. Throughout the manoeuvre, the controller must consider both ground elevation and aircraft configuration so that inappropriate **ground proximity warning system (GPWS)** alerts are not provoked.

### 17.14 ILS Vectoring Requirements

For vectors intended to provide an ILS localizer interception, the final heading should meet the localizer centreline at **45°**. During parallel-runway operations using **Mode 1 or Mode 2**, the maximum interception angle is instead **30°**. The aircraft must be maintained on the intercepting heading for **at least 1 NM**.

### 17.15 Radar Controlled Approach

Radar may guide an aircraft through a **precision approach radar (PAR)** procedure or a **surveillance radar approach (SRA)**. In both, the pilot receives radar-derived instructions intended to keep the aircraft on a predetermined approach track. PAR also supplies guidance relative to a specified glide path. SRA provides a published, recommended descent profile rather than precision vertical guidance; this is sometimes called a **virtual glide path**. If the controller loses the radar contact for a significant period during the **final 2 NM** of either radar approach, the pilot is told to carry out the missed approach.

### 17.16 PAR

**Precision approach radar** is specialised equipment that supplies a dedicated approach controller with accurate information about both the aircraft's lateral position (**azimuth**) and its position relative to the glide path (**elevation**). While conducting a PAR approach, that controller handles only the aircraft receiving the service and is not assigned additional duties.

The controller talks the aircraft down using instructions to turn left or right or steer a heading, combined with vertical-position indications such as **“on,” “above”** or **“below”** the path. This continuous sequence of transmissions requires the pilot to remain on the PAR approach frequency. The pilot states the applicable **decision height (DH)** to the controller at the start of the approach; the controller continues the guidance until the aircraft reaches that height, announces that DH has been reached, and terminates radar service.

Because the aircraft stays on the PAR frequency, the PAR controller must coordinate with the aerodrome controller to obtain landing clearance and relay it to the pilot. Clearance is normally obtained at about **4 NM** from the runway threshold but may be withheld by aerodrome control until **2 NM**. If landing clearance has not reached the pilot by **2 NM**, the missed approach is commenced automatically.

During the approach, generally around **3 NM**, the pilot is required to verify that the landing gear is correctly configured; at a military aerodrome, flap configuration is checked as well. The crew also cross-checks aircraft position at some stage against independent information, for example **DME** and **radio-altimeter** indications. PAR equipment is uncommon at civilian airports, whereas newer PAR installations are widely used on military aerodromes.

### 17.17 SRA

A **surveillance radar approach** uses **TAR** to conduct a **non-precision approach**. Consequently, the procedure has a published **minimum descent altitude/height (MDA/H)**. The overall talk-down resembles PAR, but the controller reports range together with advisory altitude or height rather than providing precision glide-path corrections. A representative call is: **“5 miles from touchdown; you should be passing 1,550 ft.”**

Each SRA specifies a **radar termination range (RTR)**, meaning the distance at which radar assistance ends automatically. An **RTR 2** procedure, for example, ends radar service **2 NM before the landing threshold**. The controller gives magnetic headings and lateral corrections relative to the extended runway centreline. With **RTR 2**, the pilot receives range and advisory height information every **1 NM**; with **RTR 1**, these reports are made every **0.5 NM**.

As for PAR, the radar controller is responsible for obtaining landing clearance from the aerodrome controller and passing it to the aircraft. The example is a Gatwick SRA chart.

## Aerodrome Control

### 17.18 Introduction

Preventing collisions between aircraft and other aircraft, obstacles, vehicles or people at an aerodrome and in its immediate airspace is an aerodrome control responsibility. At a **controlled aerodrome**, the controller generally works in the **visual control room (VCR)** at the top of the tower, from which most or all of the aerodrome should be visible. Electronic surveillance or other aids may supplement that view, particularly in darkness or poor visibility.

A central task is to regulate movements onto and along the active runway. Aerodrome control is principally procedural: the controller relies on the pilot's reports of location to decide what clearances or instructions to issue. For a departing aircraft, the **take-off clearance** constitutes permission to enter the runway and depart. After leaving the ground, the pilot reports **“airborne”**, at which point another aircraft may be authorised to use the runway.

### 17.19 Use of the Runway

The aerodrome controller normally selects the **runway-in-use** for both arrivals and departures. That selection determines the direction of the visual traffic circuit and of instrument approach operations. Wind direction is usually the governing factor, although light or calm conditions may permit a different choice to accommodate ATC, airspace restrictions or noise-abatement rules.

At **London Heathrow**, departing aircraft are expected to accept a tailwind component of **5 kt** where necessary to use noise-preferential runways. The usual operating principle is that the runway should be occupied by **only one aircraft at a time**.

### 17.20 Departing Aircraft

Aircraft awaiting departure remain at a designated **runway holding point** on a taxiway, positioned sufficiently far from the runway centreline. An instruction for **immediate take-off** requires the pilot to enter the runway, align with its centreline and begin the take-off roll without stopping.

After the aircraft ahead starts its take-off run, the next aircraft may receive **“line up and wait”** and be permitted onto the runway. It cannot be cleared to take off until the preceding departure has crossed the **upwind threshold** or has begun turning away from the runway heading.

### 17.21 Arriving Aircraft

An arriving aircraft established on final is cleared to land when the runway is available. On an instrument approach, landing clearance is normally issued around **4 NM** from the threshold and must be received by **2 NM**. If no clearance is available at the **2 NM** point, the pilot initiates the published missed approach.

Following touchdown and runway exit, the pilot informs the tower that the runway has been **vacated**. The report is not made merely when the aircraft has entered the taxiway: **every part of the aircraft must have passed the relevant runway holding position**.

### 17.22 Essential Local Traffic

A controller must advise arriving and departing pilots about **essential local traffic** of which the controller is aware. This includes aircraft, ground vehicles or personnel located on or near the runway to be used, together with traffic in the departure, climb-out or final-approach areas where its position presents a possible collision hazard.

### 17.23 “Land After” Clearance

At busy periods, aerodrome control may conditionally authorise a following aircraft to **“land after the preceding aircraft”**. The procedure helps accommodate arrivals separated by as little as **1 minute**, but it transfers the practical assessment of adequate landing clearance and visual separation to the pilot of the following aircraft. That pilot must decide whether the earlier landing aircraft will have left the runway in time and whether adequate spacing can be maintained throughout the operation. The aircraft ahead must remain in sight during the landing, and the following pilot is responsible for separation; the visual spacing used may be less than normal procedural separation. **Rapid-exit taxiways** or **fast turn-off lanes** make the arrangement more practicable.

The permission is subject to all of these conditions:

- The operation takes place **in daylight**.
- The following pilot maintains visual contact with the aircraft ahead for the **entire manoeuvre**.
- The runway is **dry**, with **good braking action**.
- The pilot and operator concerned understand that runway occupancy is limited to **50 seconds**.
- The ATCO can observe the **full length of the landing runway**.

### 17.24 Flight Information

Aerodrome control also supplies flight information to aircraft moving on the aerodrome and to aircraft flying nearby, as other ATS units do within their areas of responsibility. An **ATIS** transmission can convey routine items. Nevertheless, the tower controller must ensure that departing aircraft and arriving aircraft making their initial tower call know about any restrictions affecting aerodrome radio-navigation aids or visual aids. Information about **work in progress** on the aerodrome must likewise be communicated in good time.

### 17.25 Information to Departing and Arriving Aircraft

Aircraft must receive information that is both current and sufficient for safe use of the aerodrome. Surface wind direction given by aerodrome control is expressed in **degrees magnetic**, enabling the pilot to compare it directly with magnetic compass indications.

**Before taxi:** The information supplied comprises:

- The runway selected for use.
- Surface wind direction and speed.
- Aerodrome **QNH**.
- Air temperature applicable to the departure runway, **for turbine-engined aircraft only**.
- Visibility along the intended take-off direction, or **RVR**.
- The correct time.

**Before take-off:** The pilot is updated on:

- Any important alteration in wind, temperature, visibility or runway visual range.
- Significant weather in the take-off and initial climb-out regions, unless this has already been reported to the pilot.

**Before entry into the traffic circuit or commencement of a landing approach:** The aircraft receives:

- The runway to be used.
- Surface wind direction and speed.
- Aerodrome **QNH**.

### 17.26 Alerting Service

The aerodrome controller activates aerodrome **rescue and firefighting** facilities when a situation requires them. Visual-circuit movements may be suspended for the duration of an emergency. The controller may also suspend the circuit on the direction of the **area control centre (ACC)** when an emergency aircraft is expected to land.

If an aircraft granted landing clearance has not landed **within 5 minutes of the clearance being issued**, or if an aircraft transferred to tower fails to establish contact, the aerodrome controller must report the circumstances immediately to the **ACC or flight information centre (FIC)**. The **alert phase (ALERFA)** is then declared without delay.

## Approach Control Service

### 17.27 Establishment

**Approach control** manages aircraft approaching an aerodrome and aircraft departing from it. For an IFR departure intending to enter the airways system, approach control connects the aerodrome departure arrangement with the route-joining procedure. Arrivals receive the corresponding transition in the opposite direction.

Radar is widely employed for this purpose, but **procedural approach control** also exists, as illustrated by Oxford's **VDF** and **NDB** approach procedures. An aerodrome situated in a **control zone (CTR)** must have approach control; its controller may be referred to as the **zone controller**. A single approach-control office may cover several aerodromes in the same CTR and may be located at a different aerodrome from the one being served. Outside a CTR, an established approach service—such as that at Oxford—is **advisory**.

Where instrument approaches have been published, the approach controller may assign radar vectoring and the monitoring of aircraft positioning themselves to a **radar director**. For a CTR aerodrome operating in **IMC**, or when conditions do not satisfy VMC take-off criteria, the approach controller must authorise departures. The approach controller also arranges landing clearance with the tower for IFR aircraft conducting instrument approaches in poor visibility.

### 17.28 Departing Aircraft

If departing traffic is controlled by ATC clearances, each departure clearance specifies the flight requirements needed for safe release into the route structure. These may include:

- The take-off direction and the direction of a turn after departure.
- The track to be achieved before turning onto the desired heading.
- The level at which to remain before climbing towards the assigned cruising level.
- The time, position and/or rate governing a change of level.
- Any additional manoeuvre necessary for safe aircraft operation.

### 17.29 Take-off Direction

To improve departure flow, ATC may propose a runway direction that is **not directly into wind**. The decision belongs to the **pilot-in-command**: the pilot may accept that suggestion or wait for a departure in the preferred direction.

### 17.30 Delays

An aircraft may be delayed **on the departure aerodrome** rather than sent airborne to wait at its destination. This avoids unnecessary airborne holding on arrival. ATC must advise the operator or its designated representative about significant delays, and always when the anticipated delay **exceeds 30 minutes**.

### 17.31 Information for Departing Aircraft

Approach control communicates three categories of information to departures.

**Meteorological developments.** Significant new weather information for the departure and climb-out areas is relayed **without delay** unless the aircraft is already known to have received it. Relevant changes include surface wind direction or speed, visibility, **RVR**, and air temperature for turbine-powered aircraft. Relevant phenomena comprise **thunderstorms or cumulonimbus; moderate or severe turbulence; windshear; hail; moderate or severe icing; severe squall lines; freezing precipitation; severe mountain waves; sandstorms; duststorms; blowing snow; tornadoes; and waterspouts**.

**Navigation and visual aids.** A changed serviceability or operating status of visual or non-visual aids needed for take-off and climb must be passed promptly, except where the aircraft is known to have that information already.

**Essential traffic.** Any essential local traffic known to the approach controller must be reported promptly to the departing aircraft.

### 17.32 Arriving Aircraft

Aircraft passed from area control to approach control can be instructed to report at particular stages of the arrival. ATC may ask for reports when **leaving or passing a reporting point**, **starting a procedure turn**, or **starting a base turn**. Other reports can be requested when needed to assist the handling or expeditious departure of other traffic.

### 17.33 Initial Approach Clearance

An IFR aircraft cannot be cleared to commence its initial approach **below the applicable minimum altitude** unless at least one of the following conditions is met:

- The pilot reports crossing an appropriate, radio-aid-defined position.
- The pilot reports sighting the aerodrome and being able to maintain it in sight.
- The aircraft is executing a visual approach.
- Radar has positively established the aircraft's position.

### 17.34 Visual Approach

A **visual approach** by an IFR aircraft is one in which the pilot does not complete some or all of an instrument approach procedure and instead makes the approach by reference to the terrain. Permission may be given when visual contact with the terrain can be maintained **and** either of the following criteria applies:

- The reported cloud ceiling equals or exceeds the approved **initial approach level** for that aircraft; **or**
- At the initial approach level, or later during the instrument procedure, the pilot reports weather permitting a visual approach and landing to be completed with reasonable assurance.

### 17.35 Separation

An aircraft authorised for a visual approach must still be separated from other arriving and departing traffic. When successive aircraft are conducting visual approaches, the applicable radar or procedural separation continues until the following pilot reports the preceding aircraft **in sight**. ATC then instructs the following aircraft to follow the preceding aircraft and maintain spacing from it. Communication with the next control position must be transferred early enough for a landing clearance or another necessary instruction to reach the aircraft in time.

### 17.36 Instrument Approach

Instrument arrivals remain under the supervision of approach control. If radar monitoring or vectoring is involved, a **radar director** or **radar final controller** may exercise the delegated responsibility.

**Speed control.** One means of maintaining intervals between radar-sequenced instrument approaches is to assign particular indicated airspeeds. A common speed at the **initial approach fix (IAF)** is **210 kt IAS**, which most commercial transport aeroplanes can achieve without deploying high-drag or lift-enhancing devices. An example instruction is **“Speedbird 289, leave Ockham, heading 290, speed 210 kt.”** If the succeeding aircraft begins closing the gap, ATC can require that aircraft to fly more slowly. Each adjustment is limited to **20 kt or less**, and all assigned speeds are expressed in **multiples of 10 kt**; for example, **“Speedbird 289, maintain 180 kt.”** As the aircraft nears touchdown it must slow towards **$V_{AT}$**, so speed control is discontinued **inside 4 NM from touchdown**.

**Unfamiliar approach procedure.** If the commander says the procedure is unfamiliar, or ATC can plainly see that this is the case, the controller states the initial approach level, the elapsed time after the applicable reporting point at which to start the procedure turn, the level for that turn, and the final approach track. For a **straight-in approach**, only the final approach track needs to be stated. The controller also gives missed approach instructions when considered necessary.

**Visual contact before the end of an instrument procedure.** Acquiring visual reference to the terrain does **not** itself cancel the remaining instrument procedure. The pilot completes that procedure unless a visual approach is requested and specifically approved.

**Selection of procedure.** ATC can designate a particular instrument approach to improve traffic flow. If ATC names no procedure, the pilot may select **any authorised approach**.

### 17.37 Holding

When an arrival sequence requires holding before an instrument approach, the **approach controller** manages the holding stack, although that task may be assigned to a radar director. The procedures for entering and flying a hold must be those established by the relevant ATS authority and published in the **Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP)**. If the pattern or entry has not been published, or is unfamiliar to the commander, the appropriate ATS unit must explain the procedure to be flown.

**Holding point.** Aircraft are instructed to wait at a specified holding fix. The controller maintains the necessary separation—vertical, lateral or longitudinal—according to the method applicable to that holding point.

**Separation from en-route traffic.** Unless lateral separation is established, appropriate vertical minima are maintained between aircraft in the hold and aircraft flying through the route structure whenever the latter are within **5 minutes' flying time** of the holding area.

**Allocation of holding levels.** Levels are assigned to make subsequent approach clearances possible in the appropriate order. Normally, the first aircraft arriving above the holding fix occupies the **lowest level**, while later arrivals hold at progressively higher levels. A type that uses disproportionately large amounts of fuel at low altitude, such as a **supersonic aircraft**, may be kept above its normal position in the sequence if radar or distinct descent paths permit it to descend later through levels occupied by other traffic.

**Alternative holding arrangements.** When the pilot-in-command is unable to follow the designated holding or communications procedure, alternative arrangements requested by the pilot should be authorised when existing traffic permits.

### 17.38 Stacking (Approach Sequence)

The approach sequence is organised to admit the **greatest practicable number of arrivals** while keeping the **average delay** to a minimum. Particular priority may be granted to:

- An aircraft that may be compelled to land for safety reasons, such as **engine malfunction** or **insufficient fuel**.
- A hospital aircraft, or an aircraft carrying someone who is **ill or seriously injured** and requires urgent treatment.

**Procedural release from holding.** Except when the timed-approach system described in 17.39 is in operation, the next aircraft does not leave the stack for its approach until the previous aircraft either:

- Has advised that it can finish its approach without encountering **IMC**; or
- Is communicating with the tower, has been observed by the aerodrome controller and can reasonably be expected to land normally.

**Remaining in the hold.** A pilot may ask to wait for improving weather or for another reason; ATC will approve such holding. If other aircraft wish to approach and radar is available, the aircraft that remains in the hold may be vectored to another nearby holding fix. Alternatively, the controller can place it at the **top of the stack**, by radar vector or procedural clearance, allowing the other arrivals to begin their approaches and land.

**Credit for en-route delay.** Where ATC has permitted an aircraft to absorb delay before arrival—through a reduced cruise speed or holding en route—the time already absorbed is taken into account when determining its position in the arrival stack.

### 17.39 Timed Approaches

**Timed approaches** permit the controller to release aircraft from a holding fix at shorter intervals than the normal procedural-sequencing method in 17.38. Each later aircraft is cleared to leave the fix after a predetermined time interval following its predecessor. This method requires approval by the competent authority and the following provisions:

- A clearly identifiable **timing checkpoint** is designated on the approach path. It must be a point the pilot can recognise, such as a specified **VOR radial** or **DME distance**.
- Each aircraft is assigned a **time to pass that checkpoint inbound**. The times are calculated to produce the required landing interval while preserving all applicable separation minima, including those relating to runway occupancy. The controller must issue the required time sufficiently early for the pilot to adjust the flight and meet it.

### 17.40 Expected Approach Time (EAT)

An arriving aircraft expected to hold in a stack is assigned an **expected approach time (EAT)**. ATC sends that time as early as possible, preferably **before or at the beginning of the aircraft's initial descent from cruising altitude**. For aircraft that consume fuel at an especially high rate at lower levels, the notice should reach the crew early enough to decide whether to absorb the delay in flight—for example by reducing en-route speed—and, if appropriate, request an amended flight plan.

ATC communicates a revised EAT without delay whenever it changes by **5 minutes or more**, or by a smaller threshold established by the ATS authority or agreed between the ATS units involved. When an aircraft is likely to hold for **30 minutes or more**, the EAT must be sent by the quickest available means. If there could be uncertainty about which hold the EAT applies to, the controller states the relevant **holding fix** alongside the time.

**EAT** means the time at which ATC anticipates that an aircraft delayed on arrival will depart its holding fix to complete the landing approach.

### 17.41 Information for Arriving Aircraft

Approach control supplies arriving aircraft with different information at distinct stages of the approach.

**On initial contact with approach control.** The controller gives the following in the stated sequence as early as practicable, omitting items that the aircraft is known to possess already:

1. The **runway-in-use**.
2. **Meteorological information** equivalent to that used in the arrival **ATIS**, drawn from the locally disseminated aerodrome weather reports.
3. Current **runway surface condition** information when precipitation or other temporary hazards exist.
4. Any change in the operational availability of **visual or non-visual aids** essential for approach or landing.

**At the start of final approach.** Information must be supplied about:

- Important changes in **mean surface wind direction or speed**. Significance criteria are given in **Annex 3 (Meteorological Service)**. Where wind-component tables are available to the controller, the changes treated as significant are a **10 kt mean headwind component**, a **2 kt mean tailwind component**, and a **5 kt mean crosswind component**.
- Any recent reports of **windshear** or **turbulence** affecting the final approach area.
- Current visibility representative of the landing direction, or the latest available **RVR values** and, if practicable, their trend; **slant visual range** is included where supplied.

**While the aircraft is on final approach.** The controller reports the following immediately as new information becomes available:

- Newly arising dangers, such as **unauthorised runway traffic**.
- Significant surface-wind variation, specifying the **minimum and maximum** observed values.
- Important deterioration or other change in **runway surface conditions**.
- Any change in the operating status of the **visual or non-visual aids** required for landing.
- Altered **RVR** values according to the local reporting scale, or changed visibility along the direction of approach and landing.

### 17.42 Transfer of Aircraft from One ATC Unit to Another

Transfer of responsibility between ATC units is arranged at an appropriate **position, level or time**. The chosen transfer point must give the receiving controller enough opportunity to communicate any necessary information or instructions before the aircraft needs to act on them.

## Air Traffic Advisory Service

### 17.43 Establishment and Objective

**Air traffic advisory service** operates in **Class F advisory airspace** or on an **advisory route**, for aircraft following **IFR flight plans**. It is intended to provide as much separation as is practicable between such participating aircraft. The service may be introduced when a need for greater assistance has been recognised but a decision to establish full ATC has not yet been reached. It therefore represents an interim service rather than full control.

Its purpose is to improve the practical usefulness of **collision-hazard information** beyond what would be available from a flight information service alone.

### 17.44 Operation

An advisory service provides neither the full protection nor the responsibility associated with ATC. Controllers accordingly transmit **advice and suggestions**, not binding **ATC clearances**. IFR aircraft choosing to participate are expected to operate as they would under the procedures for controlled airspace, except that a change to the flight plan does not need an ATC clearance.

A **flight plan must be filed for every IFR flight in Class F**. An aircraft entering the advisory service receives acknowledgement that its flight plan has been submitted, rather than a control clearance. An IFR aircraft crossing an advisory route must cross **at right angles** to the route's direction and choose a flight level in accordance with the **semicircular rule**.

## Aircraft Emergencies

### 17.45 Use of SSR

An aircraft requiring assistance can normally signal the nature of an emergency most rapidly by selecting the appropriate **reserved SSR Mode A code**. The usefulness of this signal depends on radar interrogation and reception being available, which can in turn depend on aircraft altitude. Military ground stations and mobile units may detect SSR signals outside civilian radar-control coverage and can help the alerting service locate or assist an aircraft.

The reserved codes are:

| SSR Mode A code | Indication |
|---|---|
| **7700** | Emergency |
| **7600** | Radio communication failure |
| **7500** | Unlawful interference |

Two other conspicuity codes are described:

| SSR Mode A code | Use |
|---|---|
| **7000** | An aircraft in an area where radar assistance is available but not currently using the radar service; the **VFR conspicuity** code. |
| **2000** | An aircraft operating where a radar service is unavailable but intending to enter radar-service coverage and request that service. An example is **eastbound North Atlantic (NAT) traffic east of 30°W** preparing to enter the **Scottish upper information region (UIR)**. |

### 17.46 Aircraft Already Identified

When a controller has already identified an aircraft by its assigned discrete SSR code and is providing radar service, changing to a general emergency code may interrupt or slow the provision of help. The pilot should therefore **retain the allocated Mode A code** rather than replace it with a reserved one.

### 17.47 ATC Contact in an Emergency

During an emergency, ATC may establish contact on **121.5 MHz**. The **123.45 MHz inter-pilot air-to-air frequency** may be used as an alternative.

### 17.48 Emergency Descent

A high-altitude pressurisation failure may require an immediate descent to a level at which atmospheric oxygen will sustain life. In that situation, ATC broadcasts a warning to other traffic near the descending aircraft. The pilot conducting the descent should, where possible, make repeated transmissions of the aircraft's **current altitude**, assisting other crews in avoiding a collision.

### 17.49 Fuel Jettison

An aircraft may need to discard fuel rapidly to reduce its mass to the permitted **maximum landing mass**. Aircraft with a **maximum take-off mass** greater than their maximum landing mass are required to carry a **fuel-jettison system**.

Before releasing fuel while in **CAS**, the crew informs the controlling ATC unit (**ATCU**). The selected track should avoid populated areas and should, preferably, pass over **water**; it must also avoid regions in which thunderstorm activity has been observed or is forecast. Fuel is not jettisoned below **6,000 ft** so that dispersed fuel droplets have sufficient height to evaporate before reaching the surface. The crew advises ATC of **how long the jettison will take**.

### 17.50 Strayed and Unidentified Aircraft

A **strayed aircraft** is an aircraft that has travelled substantially away from its intended route or whose pilot reports being lost. An **unidentified aircraft** is one whose presence is known to an ATC unit although that unit has not established which aircraft it is.
