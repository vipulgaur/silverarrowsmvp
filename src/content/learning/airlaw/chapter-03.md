# Chapter 3: Airworthiness of Aircraft

## Introduction

### 3.1 Background

The Chicago Convention established aviation safety as a fundamental international responsibility. One important part of that responsibility is ensuring that an aircraft is suitable for flight, both when it is first manufactured and throughout its subsequent service life.

**Airworthiness certification** provides formal evidence that an aircraft satisfies the relevant safety requirements. The initial approval is not sufficient on its own: the aircraft must continue to meet the requirements for the certificate to remain valid.

Before an aircraft type enters production, its design and manufacturing processes are assessed against rigorous standards. Flight testing is also required to demonstrate that the aircraft performs and behaves as intended. Once the prescribed evaluation and testing have been completed successfully, a **Type Certificate** is issued for the approved aircraft type. In the examples used here, the United States is the State of Manufacture for Boeing aircraft, while France is the State of Manufacture for Airbus aircraft.

The Type Certificate applies to later production aircraft of the approved design. An individual production aircraft receives its own certification through the **State of Registry**, following a short compliance flight test. An initial type approval may also extend to later versions or modifications, provided the differences do not require the aircraft to be certified as a substantially different type.

A significant redesign can require a fresh certification process. For example, the **Boeing 747-400** was considered sufficiently different from the original Boeing 747 by the FAA and the UK CAA to require recertification, involving substantial additional cost.

During the prototype flight-test programme, before normal certification has been completed, the aircraft may fly under a **Permit to Fly** issued by the State of Manufacture.

### 3.2 Standards

Airworthiness requirements are addressed in **Annex 8 of the Chicago Convention**. They operate alongside the requirements of **Annex 6, Part I**, which covers the operating limitations and performance standards applicable to aeroplanes.

The two Annexes deal with complementary aspects of safety:

- **Annex 8 — Airworthiness:** Establishes the standards associated with the aircraft's design, construction and engineering airworthiness.
- **Annex 6, Part I — Operation:** Establishes requirements that contribute to the safe operation of the aircraft, including performance-related operating limitations.

Meeting the engineering requirements of Annex 8 does not, by itself, establish every condition necessary for safe flight operations. The relevant operational requirements of Annex 6 must also be satisfied. Together, the requirements address both **aircraft performance** and **flying qualities**.

### 3.3 Applicability

The airworthiness standards described in **Annex 8, Part III** apply to aeroplanes that meet all of the following conditions:

- Their **certificated maximum take-off mass is greater than 5,700 kg**.
- They are intended to carry **passengers, cargo or mail** in international air navigation.
- They have **at least two engines**.

Unless a specific exemption applies, the requirements cover the **complete aeroplane**, including its power-units, equipment and systems.

The corresponding European requirements are expressed through the **EASA Certification Specifications (CS)**.

## Airworthiness

### 3.4 Certificate of Airworthiness

A **Certificate of Airworthiness (C of A)** is issued by the **State of Registry** when acceptable evidence demonstrates that the particular aircraft meets the relevant airworthiness requirements.

ICAO specifies a standard certificate format. The document identifies the aircraft and the authority issuing its approval. Its essential particulars include:

1. The aircraft's **nationality and registration marks**.
2. The **manufacturer** and the manufacturer's **aircraft designation**, identifying the type and model, such as *Boeing 747-400*.
3. The **aircraft serial number**, identifying the individual airframe in a manner comparable with a vehicle chassis number.
4. The **certification category and/or permitted operation**.
5. A formal certification statement, together with the **date of issue** and the issuing authority's **signature**.

The standard form also provides for information about the applicable certification basis and a further space for an endorsement or continuing-inspection statement.

### 3.5 Continuing Airworthiness

The **State of Registry** determines whether an aircraft remains airworthy after its original certification. Its responsibilities therefore extend beyond issuing the first Certificate of Airworthiness.

For aircraft whose **maximum take-off mass exceeds 5,700 kg**, the State of Registry must maintain a system for recording occurrences that could affect continued airworthiness. These include **faults, malfunctions, defects and other relevant events**. Recording such information supports decisions about whether the aircraft continues to meet its required safety standard.

### 3.6 Structural Integrity and Corrosion Control

For aircraft with a **maximum take-off mass greater than 5,700 kg**, the **State of Design** must ensure that a continuing **structural integrity programme** is in place.

The programme is intended to support the continued airworthiness of the aircraft structure and must include **information on corrosion control**. This responsibility belongs to the State of Design, rather than being the same function as the State of Registry's recording of operational faults and defects.

### 3.7 Validity of the Certificate of Airworthiness

A Certificate of Airworthiness may remain valid or be renewed when the aircraft's continued airworthiness has been demonstrated through a **periodic inspection**. The **State** determines the intervals between these inspections.

If the aircraft suffers damage, the **State of Registry** is responsible for deciding whether the damage makes it unairworthy. Where repair is necessary, the State of Registry must:

1. Identify or specify the **repairs required**.
2. Determine that those repairs have been **properly completed**.
3. Confirm the necessary airworthiness conditions before **reissuing the Certificate of Airworthiness**.

The presence of a previously issued certificate does not override a subsequent determination that the aircraft is no longer airworthy.

### 3.8 Aircraft Limitations and Information

Every aircraft must have an **approved flight manual**, or another suitable means of supplying the required information. This material identifies the aircraft's **approved limitations** and provides the additional information needed to operate it safely.

Where a limiting airspeed is specified in terms of **Mach number**, the aircraft must be equipped with a **Mach meter**, allowing the applicable limitation to be monitored.

### 3.9 Notification to the State of Design

When a **Contracting State** enters an aircraft on its register **for the first time** and issues or validates a Certificate of Airworthiness for that aircraft, it must notify the **State of Design** of this action.
