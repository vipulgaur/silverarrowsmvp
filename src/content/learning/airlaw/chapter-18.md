# Chapter 18 — Aeronautical Information Service (AIS)

## Introduction

### 18.1 Objectives of the AIS

International flight operations depend on the dependable exchange of current aeronautical information. Before an intended flight can be conducted, pilots and aircraft operators need to establish which aerodromes, navigation aids and air traffic services (ATS) routes are available. Controllers use equivalent operational data, together with reports supplied by pilots, to manage traffic safely. The State's Aeronautical Information Service (AIS) is responsible for assembling, managing, publishing and distributing that information.

Reliable information has become still more critical as **area navigation (RNAV)**, **required navigation performance (RNP)** and computer-based airborne navigation have been adopted. Navigation systems may act directly on aeronautical data; an inaccurate, corrupted or out-of-date data item can therefore compromise flight safety. Precision RNAV approaches based on **GLS (GPS-based landing systems)** similarly depend on continuously available information of a suitable degree of precision.

### 18.2 Annex 15

**ICAO Annex 15 to the Convention on International Civil Aviation** contains the Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) governing the provision of aeronautical information services. It is the applicable ICAO reference for the AIS material in this chapter.

## General

### 18.3 Functions

A Contracting State must ensure that an AIS is provided. It may operate the service itself, arrange a shared AIS with one or more other Contracting States, or assign its provision to a non-governmental organisation. Whichever organisational arrangement is selected, the requirements of Annex 15 must be met adequately.

### 18.4 Responsibilities

Responsibility for information published under a State's authority remains with that State, even if another organisation provides the service. Aeronautical information issued on the State's behalf must identify the State authority responsible for its publication.

Information and data covering both the State's territory and any areas outside that territory where it provides ATS must be **adequate, available in time and of the required quality**. The State must establish arrangements through which all its operationally involved services forward the necessary information to AIS without delay.

### 18.5 Availability

The normal expectation is continuous AIS provision, **24 hours per day**. Where an uninterrupted service is not offered, availability must still cover the entire time an aircraft is flying within the AIS area of responsibility, plus **at least 2 hours before and 2 hours afterwards**. Service must also be provided at other times when requested by an appropriate ground organisation.

### 18.6 Sources of Information

The service must make available information needed before departure and during flight. It obtains such material not only from domestic sources but also from other States' aeronautical information services and any additional suitable sources.

### 18.7 Requirements of the Service

A published item of aeronautical information must be traceable to, and verified by, its **State of Origin**. If verification or attribution cannot be established when the item is distributed, that limitation must be explicitly indicated. Other States must be able to obtain the aeronautical information they require for safe, orderly and efficient navigation.

The presentation of data must suit its operational users. These include flight crews, flight-planning personnel, flight-simulator services, the ATS unit delivering flight information service (FIS) in a flight information region (FIR), and services that supply pre-flight information.

### 18.8 Publication

The AIS handles information for the State's territory and for the airspace outside its borders in which that State is responsible for ATS. Its tasks encompass receiving or originating the information, collecting and combining it, editing and formatting it, publishing or storing it, and distributing it. The resulting publication system is the **Integrated Aeronautical Information Package (IAIP)**.

### 18.9 World Geodetic System — 1984 (WGS-84)

From **1 January 1998**, aeronautical latitude and longitude coordinates published for aviation have used the **World Geodetic System — 1984 (WGS-84)** as their reference. A further requirement applicable from **5 November 1998** calls for specified surveyed ground positions in the **AD section of an AIP** to include both their elevation relative to **mean sea level** and the associated **geoid undulation** relative to the WGS-84 ellipsoid.

The geoid and the WGS-84 reference ellipsoid do not coincide everywhere. The variations involved, and the reference geometry used for position data, have implications for satellite orbits and satellite-navigation systems.

## The Integrated Aeronautical Information Package

### 18.10 Definition and Contents

The **IAIP** provides the established means of distributing aeronautical material required for operational safety and the conduct of flights. Its constituent products are:

- **Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP)** and the service that maintains it by amendments;
- **AIP Supplements**;
- **NOTAM** and **pre-flight information bulletins (PIBs)**;
- **Aeronautical Information Circulars (AICs)**; and
- **checklists and summaries**.

## The Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP)

### 18.11 Use

The AIP is the authoritative repository for **permanent aeronautical information** and for temporary information expected to remain relevant for a substantial period. Its main subject matter changes relatively infrequently. Where feasible, it is presented in a form usable during flight, such as **instrument approach charts** and **standard instrument departure (SID) charts**. In the United Kingdom, the publication was formerly known as the **Air Pilot**.

The AIP is kept sufficiently accurate and current for crews and operators to use as a dependable reference. It does **not** publish the **aerodrome operating minima determined by an operator**, such as the visual references that the operator requires before continuing an instrument approach.

### 18.12 Contents

The AIP is divided into three principal parts:

| Part | Designation | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **GEN** | General information |
| 2 | **ENR** | En-route information |
| 3 | **AD** | Aerodrome data |

### 18.13 Part 1 — GEN

Part 1 is the location for regulatory and administrative information. Its five subdivisions, **GEN 0 to GEN 4**, are organised as follows. The State's published **differences from ICAO SARPs and PANS** are identified in **GEN 1.7**.

**GEN 0 — Introductory publication material.** The preface, records of AIP amendments and AIP supplements, checklist of AIP pages, list of hand amendments affecting Part 1, and Part 1 table of contents.

**GEN 1 — National regulations and requirements.** Designated authorities; requirements for aircraft entering, transiting and departing; requirements for passengers, crew and cargo entering, transiting and departing; aircraft equipment, instruments and documentation; a summary of national rules and international agreements or conventions; and differences from ICAO Standards and Recommended Practices.

**GEN 2 — Tables and codes.** Units and measuring system, aircraft markings and holidays; abbreviations appearing in AIS publications; symbols used on charts; **ICAO four-letter location indicators**; lists of radio navigation aids; conversion tables; sunrise and sunset data; and the **rate-of-climb table**.

**GEN 3 — Services.** Aeronautical information, charting, air traffic, communications and meteorological services, together with search and rescue. Information about the meteorological service, including the availability of **SIGMET**, belongs within this service grouping.

**GEN 4 — Charges.** Fees for aerodrome and heliport services and for air navigation services.

### 18.14 Part 2 — En Route (ENR)

Part 2 brings together the rules, procedures and data needed to plan and conduct flights away from an aerodrome. It also deals with administrative actions such as submitting flight plans and observing ATC requirements. Seven subdivisions, **ENR 0 through ENR 6**, organise the material.

**ENR 0 — Introductory material.** Preface, list of hand amendments affecting Part 2, and its table of contents.

**ENR 1 — General rules and procedures.** General operating rules; **VFR** and **IFR**; classification of ATS airspace; holding, approach and departure procedures; radar services and procedures; **altimeter setting procedures**; regional supplementary procedures; air traffic flow management; flight planning and addresses for flight-plan messages; interception of civil aircraft; unlawful interference; air traffic incidents; and offshore operations.

**ENR 2 — ATS airspace.** Descriptions of flight information regions (**FIRs**), upper flight information regions (**UIRs**), terminal control areas (**TMAs**) and other regulated airspace.

**ENR 3 — ATS routes.** Lower- and upper-level ATS routes, area-navigation routes, helicopter routes, other routes, en-route holding arrangements and **minimum flight altitudes**.

**ENR 4 — Radio navigation aids and systems.** En-route radio navigation facilities, specialised navigation systems, name-code designators for significant points and en-route aeronautical ground lights.

**ENR 5 — Navigation warnings.** Prohibited, restricted and danger areas; areas used for military exercises or training; other dangerous activities; en-route obstacles; aerial sport and recreational operations; bird migration; and areas with fauna requiring protection.

**ENR 6 — En-route charts.** The **ICAO en-route chart** and applicable index charts.

### 18.15 Part 3 — Aerodromes (AD)

The third AIP part covers aerodromes and heliports. It uses **four sections**, with individual aerodrome entries following a standardised schedule so that the same category of information can be located consistently. For example, **AD 2.3** gives operating hours for both **EGLL (Heathrow)** and **EGTK (Oxford)**, but with the data applicable to the particular aerodrome.

**AD 0 — Introductory material.** Preface, record of hand amendments to Part 3 and table of contents.

**AD 1 — Aerodromes and heliports: introduction.** Availability of aerodromes and heliports; rescue and firefighting facilities and snow plans; indexes of aerodromes and heliports; and their grouping.

**AD 2 — Aerodromes.** Individual aerodrome entries arranged into **24 subsections**, also covering helicopter landing areas situated at those aerodromes. The material includes aprons; taxiways and checking locations; ground-movement guidance, control systems and markings; radio navigation and landing aids; aerodrome-related charts; and refuelling facilities.

**AD 3 — Heliports.** Individual heliports that are **not located at aerodromes**, with their details provided under **23 subsections**.

### 18.16 AIP Amendments

Alterations and newly inserted material must be recognisable on AIP pages. The UK convention places a **vertical black line in the page margin** alongside revised or new text. Updating occurs at appropriate regular intervals, most commonly by substituting new pages for old ones; the AIP can also be reissued.

A **permanent change** is issued through the AIP amendment service. Amendments carry **consecutive serial numbers**, and every amended sheet—including its cover—shows the date of publication.

### 18.17 AIRAC

Where an AIP change has operational significance, the **Aeronautical Information Regulation and Control (AIRAC)** procedure is used. An AIP amendment issued this way is identified as **AIRAC**, and its effective date is printed on **every AIRAC amendment page and on the cover sheet**.

AIRAC uses internationally common effective dates spaced **28 days** apart; the cycle began on **10 January 1991**. The AIS distributes AIRAC information **at least 42 days before** the effective date. Following that date, the material should remain unchanged for another **28 days**, except where the notified circumstances are temporary and cannot continue for the whole interval. Major forthcoming alterations for which longer warning is beneficial should be published **56 days before** their effective date.

### 18.18 AIP Supplements

Material is issued as an **AIP supplement** when it concerns a temporary change lasting **3 months or more**, or a shorter event whose explanation demands extensive text or graphics. A separate supplement series is numbered **consecutively within each calendar year**.

A supplement stays filed in the AIP for as long as any portion remains applicable. If it supersedes an earlier NOTAM, it identifies that NOTAM by serial number. A checklist of supplements still in force is distributed **at least monthly**—that is, at intervals **not exceeding one month**. To distinguish supplements clearly from ordinary AIP sheets, their paper should be conspicuously coloured, **preferably yellow**.

## Notices to Airmen (NOTAM)

### 18.19 Definition

A **NOTAM** is a telecommunication notice used to bring operationally essential aeronautical information to the attention of flight-operations personnel without delay. It concerns a facility, service, procedure or hazard that has been introduced, altered or withdrawn, or whose condition has changed, where timely awareness is necessary.

### 18.20 Origination

A NOTAM is issued promptly for information that is both **temporary and relatively short-lived**. It is also appropriate for operationally important permanent changes, or long-lasting temporary changes, introduced with little advance notice. When such material needs substantial written explanation or graphics, however, it belongs in an **AIP supplement** rather than the NOTAM format. Direct operational relevance is the determining requirement for NOTAM publication.

### 18.21 AIRAC Notification

Publication of an AIRAC AIP amendment or AIRAC AIP supplement is accompanied by a NOTAM identifying the publication. The notice gives a concise indication of its subject, its effective date and its amendment or supplement reference number. Its **effective date coincides** with that of the associated AIRAC publication.

### 18.22 Notice and Validity

A NOTAM that is still applicable should continue to appear in the **pre-flight information bulletin** as a reminder until the next relevant checklist or summary has been issued. Where circumstances permit, **at least 24 hours' notice** is desirable so that notification and airspace-use planning can be completed in good time.

A notice advising that navigation equipment, another aeronautical facility or a communications service is unavailable should also state the **expected restoration time** or an **estimate of the period of unserviceability**.

### 18.23 Excluded Matter

Items lacking operational importance are not generally subjects for NOTAM. Examples include:

- Partial failures of lighting systems or ground equipment that do not have operational significance;
- Routine maintenance;
- Work on runways that are not being used, or work from which equipment can be removed rapidly if the operational runway is required;
- Temporary obstructions of no operational significance;
- Local parachuting activity of no operational significance; and
- Absence of apron marshalling or road-traffic control services where this is not operationally significant.

### 18.24 Distribution

Distribution is directed to recipients for whom the notice has immediate operational relevance and who would not otherwise have been informed **at least 7 days in advance**. Where practical, the delivery network is the **Aeronautical Fixed Telecommunication Network (AFTN)**, historically operated as a teleprinter network.

For a NOTAM transmitted through means other than AFTN, the notice is preceded by the **originator's identification** and a **six-digit date–time group** showing when it was filed.

### 18.25 NOTAM Checklists

A list identifying NOTAM currently in force must be produced at intervals **no longer than one month**. It also identifies the most recent **AIP amendment**, **AIP supplement** and **internationally distributed AICs**.

### 18.26 Errors

An inaccurate NOTAM cannot simply be amended under its original number. Either a **replacement NOTAM bearing a new number** is issued, or the faulty notice is cancelled and a **new NOTAM** is promulgated.

### 18.27 Summary

Recipients of the IAIP receive a **monthly, printed, plain-language** digest of NOTAM that remain effective. This summary also records the latest AIP amendments, the checklist of AIP supplements and AICs issued. It must be sent using the **most expeditious means** available.

## SNOWTAM

### 18.28 Description

**SNOWTAM** conveys information about **snow, ice and standing water affecting paved areas at an aerodrome**. A SNOWTAM remains valid for **no more than 24 hours**.

### 18.29 Contents of a SNOWTAM

The format specified in **Appendix 2 to ICAO Annex 15** uses lettered fields. A field with no applicable information is **omitted from transmission and left blank**. The sequence of fields and their contents is:

| Field | Information to be reported |
|---|---|
| **A** | The aerodrome's ICAO location indicator, for example **EGLL (Heathrow)**. |
| **B** | The observation's date and time, expressed in **UTC**. |
| **C** | Runway identification, such as **27R**. |
| **D** | Length actually cleared, in **metres**, where shorter than the published runway length. |
| **E** | Width actually cleared, in **metres**, where narrower than the published width. If the cleared strip is displaced from the centre, identify **L** or **R**. |
| **F** | Type of deposit across the runway's total length, using the classifications below. |
| **G** | Average depth of the deposit, in **millimetres**, for each **one-third** of the runway. |
| **H** | Friction reading for each **one-third** of the runway and the type of friction-measuring equipment used. |
| **J** | Height of critical snowbanks, in **metres**. |
| **K** | Obscuration of runway lights: report **“yes”** followed by **L**, **R** or **LR** as appropriate. |
| **L** | Further clearing work planned: give the length or width to be cleared, or **FULL** if clearance to the full published dimensions is intended. |
| **M** | Forecast completion time, in **UTC**, of that further clearance. |
| **N** | Taxiway conditions. |
| **P** | Taxiway snowbanks: if higher than **60 cm**, report **“Yes”** and the distance separating the banks, in **metres**. |
| **Q** | Apron conditions. |
| **S** | Time of the next planned observation or measurement, in **month/day/hour (UTC)** format. |
| **T** | Free-text remarks in plain language. |

For **Field F**, the recognised surface-deposit descriptions are:

- **Nil — clear and dry**;
- **Damp**;
- **Wet or water patches**;
- **Rime or frost covered**;
- **Dry snow**;
- **Wet snow**;
- **Slush**;
- **Ice**;
- **Compacted or rolled snow**; and
- **Frozen ruts or ridges**.

For **Field H**, reported friction measurements or estimates are interpreted using the following codes:

| Friction coefficient | Estimated surface friction | Code |
|---|---|---:|
| **0.4 and above** | Good | **5** |
| **0.39–0.36** | Medium to good | **4** |
| **0.35–0.3** | Medium | **3** |
| **0.29–0.26** | Medium to poor | **2** |
| **0.25 and below** | Poor | **1** |
| Reading unreliable | Unreliable | **9** |

### 18.31 Wheel Braking on Wet Runways

Although the underlying friction characteristics of a runway pavement generally deteriorate gradually, the **braking action on a wet runway can change rapidly** as the water depth changes. Seasonal differences in surface friction may also appear over longer intervals, including variations between **six-monthly** periods. Because these two influences act together, repeated friction measurements during wet conditions do not, by themselves, provide a useful continuous measure for operational decision-making.

For this discussion, a **wet runway** includes the surface states from **Damp** through **Flooded**. The term does not cover ice, deposits of snow or slush, or water associated with slush. At civil aerodromes licensed for public use, paved runways of **1,200 m or more** have been calibrated to ensure that their pavement friction is of a standard capable of providing **good braking action when wet**.

Runway water conditions are communicated by radiotelephony (R/T) using the five descriptions set out below.

| Description | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dry** | No moisture on the runway surface. |
| **Damp** | Moisture is present and has changed the surface's colour. |
| **Wet** | The pavement is thoroughly wet, but no substantial areas of standing water are visible. |
| **Water Patches** | Noticeable, significant areas of standing water can be seen. |
| **Flooded** | Standing water covers extensive areas of the surface. |

### 18.32 Interpretation

A runway described as **DAMP** or **WET** can be expected to provide an acceptable level of wheel-braking friction. The reports **WATER PATCHES** and **FLOODED** require greater caution because standing water can produce **aquaplaning**, with consequent effects on braking and any necessary operating adjustments. The description **Water Patches** applies when **at least 25% of the runway length** is covered by standing water.

If an aerodrome advises that its runway may become **slippery when wet**, take-off or landing in wet conditions should be contemplated only if the distances available meet or exceed the aeroplane **Flight Manual** performance requirements for a **very slippery or icy runway**.

At **UK military aerodromes**, runway conditions are described in ordinary language. If braking-action measuring equipment has been used, the resulting assessment is expressed as **good**, **medium** or **poor**.

## ASHTAM

### 18.33 Purpose

**Volcanic ash** is particularly hazardous to aircraft powered by turbine engines. Flight operations in volcanic regions therefore depend on early identification of existing or possible ash clouds. An **ASHTAM** communicates operationally important developments involving **volcanic activity**, an **eruption**, or an associated **ash cloud**.

### 18.34 Description

An ASHTAM reports the activity status of a volcano when a change is, or could become, operationally important. It represents the alert level through a **volcanic alert colour code**. If an eruption has generated an ash cloud relevant to aviation, the notice also details the cloud's **location, size or extent, movement, affected air routes and affected flight levels**.

The notice is valid for a **maximum of 24 hours**. An alteration of volcanic alert level requires publication of a **new ASHTAM**, even if the preceding one has not yet reached the end of its validity.

### 18.35 ASHTAM Colour Code

The alert colour is entered in **Field E** of the ASHTAM. It signifies the following conditions:

| Colour | Volcanic activity and operational meaning |
|---|---|
| **Red** | Either an eruption is occurring with a pyroclastic ash cloud or plume **above FL250**, or the volcano is considered dangerous with eruption likely and ash expected to rise **above FL250**. |
| **Orange** | An eruption is taking place but its pyroclastic ash plume or cloud **does not reach and is not expected to reach FL250**; alternatively, the volcano is dangerous and likely to erupt without its expected ash plume reaching **FL250**. |
| **Yellow** | A volcano that is intermittently active has shown a **significant recent increase** in activity but is not currently considered dangerous; caution is nevertheless necessary. Yellow also applies **after an eruption**, when the alert has been reduced from **red or orange** because activity has declined significantly, although caution remains advisable. |
| **Green** | The volcanic episode is regarded as finished and the volcano has returned to its **normal level of activity**. |

## Aeronautical Information Circulars (AICs)

### 18.36 Description

An **Aeronautical Information Circular (AIC)** is used for aeronautical material that does not belong in the AIP and is unsuitable for distribution by NOTAM. It provides interested parties with information such as:

- Advance information about major anticipated **long-term changes** in legislation, regulations, procedures or facilities;
- **Explanatory or advisory** information that could influence flight safety; and
- Advisory or explanatory material on **technical, legal or administrative** matters.

### 18.37 General Specifications of AICs

AICs are issued as **printed publications** and may contain diagrams as well as text. The State producing the circular decides which of its AICs will be circulated internationally. Circulars carry **consecutive serial numbers within the calendar year**; if more than one series is used, each series has a different **letter identifier**.

Subjects are ordinarily distinguished using **different paper colours**, making the type of information immediately recognisable. At least **once every year**, a checklist of AICs still current is issued and distributed by the same means as the circulars. In the **United Kingdom**, AIC publication occurs on a **28-day cycle**, on **Thursdays**.

### 18.38 Colour Coding of UK AICs

ICAO Annex 15 recommends subject-based colour identification for AICs. The UK scheme is:

| Colour | Subject of circular |
|---|---|
| **Pink** | Safety-related matters. |
| **Yellow** | Operations, including **ATS facilities and operating requirements**. |
| **White** | Administration, including matters such as examination dates and fees. |
| **Mauve (Purple)** | **UK airspace reservations** established under the applicable regulations. |
| **Green** | Maps and aeronautical charts. |

## Pre-flight and Post-flight Information

### 18.39 Pre-flight Information

At an aerodrome normally serving **international air operations**, the information needed to undertake flights from that aerodrome must be accessible to crews, operational staff and organisations preparing flights. The information must support **safety, orderly operations and efficiency**, and must cover the route stages beginning at the aerodrome concerned.

Pre-flight material available at the aerodrome includes applicable parts of the **IAIP**, together with relevant **maps and charts**. The aerodrome need not retain a complete collection of foreign publications on site: it may provide its own State's documents and, where practicable, those of neighbouring States, **provided** that a complete central aeronautical-information library is available and the local AIS unit can communicate directly with it.

### 18.40 Aerodrome Information

Crews departing an aerodrome must also be able to obtain up-to-date local operational information. This includes:

- **Construction or maintenance** activity on the manoeuvring area or directly beside it;
- **Damaged or uneven pavement**, even if not specially marked, such as broken areas of runways and taxiways;
- **Snow, ice or water** on runways and taxiways, its depth, and any reduction in surface friction;
- Snow **drifts and accumulations** on or close to runways or taxiways;
- **Parked aircraft or other objects** on taxiways or immediately beside them;
- Other **temporary hazards**, including bird-related dangers;
- Complete or partial **failure or irregular operation of aerodrome lighting**, including approach, threshold, runway, taxiway, obstruction and manoeuvring-area unserviceability lights, as well as faults in the aerodrome electrical supply;
- **Failures, abnormal operation or changes of operational status** affecting **ILS and its markers, SRE, PAR, DME, SSR, VOR, NDB, VHF aeronautical mobile channels, RVR observation equipment or secondary power supply**; and
- **Humanitarian relief flights and operations**, including missions organised under **United Nations** auspices, with any procedures or operating limitations associated with them.

### 18.41 PIBs

The **pre-flight information bulletin (PIB)** provides flight crews with a **plain-language consolidation of valid NOTAM and other urgent information** relevant to their operations.

### 18.42 Post-flight Information

States must provide a means at **aerodromes and heliports** for receiving crew reports about the **condition or functioning of air-navigation facilities** observed in flight. These reports must then reach the AIS so that they can be circulated wherever operational circumstances require.
