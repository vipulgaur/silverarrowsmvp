# Chapter 8: Approach Procedures

## 1. Procedure Basics

### 8.1 Purpose and design of an instrument approach

An instrument arrival must bring an aircraft from the en-route environment to a point where a landing can safely be completed using the prescribed visual references. **Obstacle protection** is therefore central to its design, especially as the aircraft descends toward the aerodrome. The location and elevation of surrounding terrain, the traffic and aircraft types expected to use the procedure, the intended operation and available airspace all influence the approach layout. These considerations also determine where the supporting navigation facilities should be positioned relative to the runway.

### 8.2 Assistance from air traffic control

When a pilot states that an approach procedure is unfamiliar—or when that lack of familiarity is apparent—the air traffic control unit must describe the procedure. The exception is an aircraft cleared for a **straight-in approach**.

### 8.3 Approach categories and applicable speeds

The aircraft's speed governs the amount of protected airspace needed for turning and other manoeuvres. The speed used to classify the aircraft, **$V_{AT}$**, is its threshold crossing speed; it also affects approach sequencing. The speed ranges applied during the initial and final approach, and the maximum circling speeds, define how much space the aircraft needs and consequently influence obstacle-clearance calculations.

| Aircraft category | $V_{AT}$ (kt) | Initial-approach range (kt) | Final-approach range (kt) | Maximum visual-circling speed (kt) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | Below 91 | 90–150 (110*) | 70–100 | 100 |
| B | 91–120 | 120–180 (140*) | 85–130 | 135 |
| C | 121–140 | 160–240 | 115–160 | 180 |
| D | 141–165 | 185–250 | 130–185 | 205 |
| E | 166–210 | 185–250 | 155–230 | 240 |

The threshold speed is based on **1.3 times $V_{S0}$**, or **1.23 times $V_{S1g}$**, in the landing configuration at the maximum certified landing mass. Values marked with an asterisk are the maximum speeds for a track-reversal or racetrack procedure, not the general initial-approach speed limits.

### 8.4 Minimum Sector Altitude (MSA) and Terminal Arrival Altitude (TAA)

MSA/TAA provide at least **300 m (984 ft)** of obstacle clearance within **25 NM** of the relevant navigation aid, initial approach fix (IAF) or intermediate fix (IF). The appropriate altitude is displayed on the instrument approach chart and serves as the minimum for the applicable sector or arrival area. The aircraft may leave that minimum only in one of these circumstances: the aerodrome and underlying terrain can be seen and will remain visible; ATC is providing radar vectors; or the aircraft is established on a published approach procedure.

### 8.5 Classification of instrument approaches

Instrument approaches are differentiated by the guidance available during the approach:

- **Precision approaches**, including Categories I, II and III, provide both lateral (azimuth) and vertical (elevation) guidance and are described here as runway approaches.
- **Non-precision approaches** provide the designated lateral guidance without the corresponding precision vertical path; they are also referred to here as aerodrome approaches.

### 8.6 Precision approach guidance and decision altitude/height

A precision approach relies on an external guidance system to define both the approach track and descent path. Examples include **ILS**, **MLS** and **Precision Approach Radar (PAR)**. The chapter also identifies satellite-based GPS landing systems (GLS) in use in the United States, with early reported centreline accuracy of approximately **±30 cm**.

When the pilot keeps the aircraft within **half-scale course-deviation indication** and follows the specified glide path, the protected approach volume provides the intended obstacle clearance above the applicable OCA/H. Because the path continues to touchdown—or to the missed approach decision point—this is a runway approach. The operator specifies in its Operations Manual both the **decision altitude/height (DA/H)** and the required visual references. At DA/H, the pilot must start a missed approach unless the prescribed visual references have been established.

### 8.7 Unserviceable glideslope

Loss of usable glide-path information changes a previously precision-guided approach into a **non-precision approach**; the pilot can no longer use precision vertical guidance.

### 8.8 Categories of precision approach

At decision height, an aircraft that has remained on the approved procedure is in the protected position intended by the procedure design. Unless the installed ground-and-aircraft system supports landing without outside visual cues, the concluding portion of the approach and the ground roll still need adequate visibility and runway-centreline reference. Increasingly capable guidance and onboard flight-management systems reduce—but do not automatically eliminate—the necessary visual component.

The ICAO categories and limits presented here are:

| Category | System minimum | Decision-height/altitude condition | Visibility requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| CAT I | 60 m (200 ft) | DH at least 60 m (200 ft) | RVR at least 550 m **or** ground visibility at least 800 m |
| CAT II | 30 m (100 ft) | DH below 60 m (200 ft) but at least 30 m (100 ft) | RVR at least 350 m |
| CAT IIIA | No system minimum | DH below 30 m (100 ft), or no DH | RVR at least 200 m |
| CAT IIIB | No system minimum | DH below 15 m (50 ft), or no DH | RVR below 200 m but above 50 m |
| CAT IIIC | No system minimum | No DH | No RVR requirement |

ICAO and the historical JAR-OPS provisions are not necessarily identical; the categories above follow the ICAO distinctions used in this chapter.

### 8.9 Non-precision approaches

A procedure remains non-precision when the supporting ground equipment cannot provide precision vertical guidance, even if its lateral direction guidance is highly accurate. Examples include approaches based on **VOR**, **NDB**, **localizer-only ILS**, azimuth-only PAR and short-range **surveillance radar approaches (SRA)**. A non-precision approach typically ends above touchdown, with a published minimum descent altitude/height rather than precision descent guidance. Some procedures lead to the aerodrome and then require **visual manoeuvring (circling)** to land on a runway different from the direction of the incoming final-approach track.

### 8.10 Visual continuation of an IFR approach

After becoming established on final approach, the pilot may complete the approach visually once the operator's specified references are available. This **does not convert the flight into VFR**: it remains the visual continuation of an IFR approach. ATC continues to be responsible for the applicable traffic separation, including aircraft ahead and behind on instrument approaches.

### 8.11 Completing an established procedure

An instrument approach normally has to be flown as published unless ATC gives alternative instructions. Even after the approach becomes visual, its applicable conditions remain in force. The procedure concludes either in a landing or through its published missed approach. If the approach is discontinued after the IAF, the missed-approach procedure is followed from the MAPt and the aircraft is climbed toward the prescribed missed-approach altitude, normally related to the applicable MSA or the specified IAF entry altitude.

### 8.12 Visibility restrictions during approach

The reported visibility or controlling RVR must meet the specified minimum **before passing the outer marker or equivalent fix on a precision approach**, or **before descending below 300 m (1,000 ft) above the aerodrome on a non-precision approach**. If visibility subsequently deteriorates after the relevant point has been passed, the approach may continue to DA/H or MDA/H. Landing remains dependent on obtaining the required visual criteria at the appropriate decision or minimum descent point.

## 2. Approach Procedure Design

### 8.13 Track, protected airspace and design tolerances

Instrument-approach design must give the aircraft a track and height profile that can realistically be achieved while ensuring obstacle separation. Protection becomes especially critical as the aircraft nears the ground. Ground-based navigation installations have measurable guidance errors, so the airspace boundaries are calculated with their tolerances in mind. The aircraft must be flown sufficiently accurately to remain within those boundaries. Procedures specify **tracks to be made good**; the pilot must therefore correct for wind rather than merely maintain an uncorrected heading.

An approach is divided into **five segments**, usually separated by published fixes. Where a suitable fix is unavailable, a precisely described point can be used instead—for example, the intersection between intermediate approach altitude and the nominal glide path at the beginning of the precision final segment.

### 8.14 Five segments

The sequence comprises the **arrival route**, **initial approach**, **intermediate approach**, **final approach** and **missed approach**. Each portion has a particular navigation and obstacle-protection purpose. Boundaries, descent permissions, equipment tolerances and aircraft configuration expectations change along the route.

### 8.15 Straight-in versus non-straight-in geometry

For a **straight-in approach**, the track is arranged to coincide with the extended runway centreline. A non-precision procedure may still qualify as straight-in when the final track approaches the runway heading within **30° or less**. Turning or offset procedures require wind correction to maintain the published ground track. Where terrain or airspace prevents a straightforward arrival, the procedure may contain a reversal, racetrack, circling or other suitable manoeuvre. Procedure design must account for the MSA and the limitations of the navigation equipment used to define the route.

### 8.16 Primary and secondary obstacle-clearance areas

Protection is assessed through **primary** and **secondary** minimum obstacle clearance (MOC) regions:

- The **primary region** receives the full specified obstacle-clearance allowance.
- **Secondary regions** border the primary area and allow obstacle-clearance reduction toward the outside limits of the protected corridor.

These lateral allowances accommodate track-guidance inaccuracies and possible variation in the aircraft's actual path.

### 8.17 Fix definition and accuracy

A navigation fix can represent crossing a ground station directly, or reaching the intersection of information from two different aids—for example, a **VOR radial and DME range**. A fix has an uncertainty area, and its size influences the dimensions of the protected approach corridor. VOR is useful for track guidance but relatively imprecise immediately overhead because of its cone of confusion. NDB offers a better overhead indication but less accurate directional guidance. A **75 MHz marker beacon** can supply a more accurately defined crossing point, such as an outer marker on a CAT I ILS. Where two systems define a fix, their error allowances must be combined.

For determining fix tolerance, use these assumed **bearing errors**:

| Aid | Assumed fix-bearing error |
|---|---:|
| VOR | ±4.5° |
| ILS localizer | ±1.4° |
| NDB | ±10.3° |

### 8.20 Arrival route or arrival segment

The arrival segment starts when the aircraft leaves the en-route airways system to begin its instrument arrival, commonly at a radio navigation facility. If that point lies **25 NM or more** from the aerodrome, the arrival is usually defined by a published **standard terminal arrival route (STAR)**. At a shorter distance, the aircraft generally proceeds directly to the initial approach facility. During this segment the en-route MOC applies, and the IAF crossing altitude may not be below the highest MSA for the aerodrome. Radar vectors to the final approach track are also common.

### 8.21 Initial approach segment

From the **IAF** to the **IF**, the initial segment positions the aircraft so it can join the intermediate track. Aircraft speed and configuration depend on the distance remaining and descent requirements. Full primary-area MOC is **300 m (984 ft)**. Normally the track to the IF can intercept at up to **90° on precision approaches** or **120° on non-precision approaches**. If reliable track guidance is not provided, a **dead-reckoning (DR) segment** may be specified: its intercept to the intermediate track is limited to **45°**, and its length to **10 NM**. Where a straight-in connection is unavailable, a reversal, racetrack or holding pattern is used.

### 8.22 Intermediate approach segment

From the **IF** to the **FAF**, this segment allows the crew to configure and slow the aircraft for the last stage of the approach, usually while minimising further descent. When there is no FAF, the intermediate segment ends on becoming established on the final inbound track. Its primary-area MOC progressively reduces from **300 m at the IF to 150 m** at the end.

### 8.23 Final approach segment

The final segment is where the aircraft completes its landing configuration, becomes aligned with the runway or prescribed final approach track, and descends toward landing minima. Its starting point depends on whether the procedure is precision or non-precision and on whether a final approach fix has been defined.

### 8.24 Non-precision final approach with a FAF

For a non-precision procedure that includes a **FAF**, the final portion begins at that fix and ends at the **MAPt**. The FAF must allow adequate distance to configure for landing and descend from the intermediate altitude to **MDA/H**. Obstacle-clearance allowance is incorporated into MDA/H. The preferred position of the FAF is **5 NM** from the runway threshold and the maximum is **10 NM**. A desirable descent profile is about **300 ft per NM**, equivalent to roughly **3°**. A **step-down fix (SDF)** may provide additional protection in difficult terrain; this results in two different OCA/H values being published for relevant sections.

### 8.25 Non-precision final approach without a FAF

This arrangement can occur where a single aid at or near the aerodrome acts as both **IAF** and **MAPt**. In these circumstances, the inbound approach course may be offset from the runway centreline. The descent toward **MDA/H** begins once the aircraft is established inbound on the defined final track.

### 8.26 Precision final approach

For ILS or MLS, the final approach starts at the **Final Approach Point (FAP)**: the location where the intermediate segment altitude meets the nominal glideslope on the localizer centreline or specified MLS azimuth. This point can lie between **300 and 900 m (1,000–3,000 ft)** above the reference datum. With a **3° (approximately 300 ft/NM)** path, that corresponds to approximately **3–10 NM** from touchdown. The DA/H calculation includes obstacle margins, but the aircraft must be flown within **half-scale CDI deviation**; a defined point along final approach allows the glide-path indication to be checked.

### 8.27 Constant-slope or stabilized approaches

A **stabilized approach** aims to maintain a consistent descent from the IAF through the subsequent approach stages. The method is intended to reduce the effects of wake turbulence, limit noise and avoid unnecessary fuel use. Wake-turbulence separation requirements discussed in this material apply where a following aircraft is at the same altitude as, or within **1,000 ft below**, the leading aircraft. Maintaining the following aircraft above the preceding aircraft helps avoid that particular exposure. The descent rate can be managed through attitude with a comparatively constant power setting, reducing large thrust changes.

### 8.28 Function of the missed approach

If the required visual references are not acquired by DA/H or MDA/H, the missed approach provides the planned safe route away from the runway environment. It can lead back toward the IAF for a repeat attempt or onto a departure profile toward an alternate aerodrome.

## 3. Obstacle Clearance Altitude/Height

### 8.29 Establishing OCA/H

**Obstacle Clearance Altitude/Height (OCA/H)** is determined during procedure development by the competent State authority through obstacle assessment and survey. It combines the relevant controlling obstacle's elevation/height with the required **minimum obstacle clearance (MOC)**. The result varies where necessary by aircraft approach category and is published on the procedure chart. For applicable procedures, **MDA/H may not be lower than OCA/H**.

### 8.30 Precision approach OCA/H and DA/H

For a precision approach, the applicable obstacle-clearance criterion establishes the lowest permissible altitude/height at which an unsuccessful approach can be converted safely into a missed approach. The height reference is the **landing runway threshold**. The operator determines the actual **DA/H** by taking the applicable obstacle clearance and any operating margins and system minimum into account.

### 8.31 OCA/H on non-precision approaches

For non-precision procedures, OCA/H denotes the lowest altitude or height that may be used without entering the obstacle-protected volume beneath the design limit. The height reference is normally **aerodrome elevation**; it changes to the relevant runway threshold when that threshold is **more than 2 m (7 ft) below aerodrome elevation**.

### 8.32 OCA/H for visual manoeuvring (circling)

On a circling approach, the reference is **aerodrome elevation**. The published circling OCA/H is based on the highest relevant obstacle within the designated visual manoeuvring area and the applicable clearance requirement. Descending beneath that level would infringe the protected obstacle-clearance margin.

## 4. Operating Minima

### 8.33 Derivation of aerodrome operating minima

Under the Annex 6 and JAR-OPS 1 framework described here, the **operator** establishes aerodrome operating minima for every aerodrome used in its operation. DA/H or MDA/H must be calculated from the published OCA/H with an additional margin where required. That **upper margin** can be zero, but otherwise accounts for operational variability, including crew qualification, outside air temperature, instrumentation and equipment differences, aircraft handling and performance, runway dimensions, the availability of visual and instrument aids, obstacles along the approach and missed-approach paths, and meteorological observation and reporting arrangements.

### 8.34 System minima

Each instrument approach system has an independent lower limit intended to prevent descent to unrealistically low minima. This **system minimum** takes precedence whenever the OCA/H plus operating margin would otherwise give a lower value. For example, a CAT I ILS calculation of **170 ft** cannot produce a permitted DH of 170 ft when its system minimum is **200 ft**; the minimum governs. A calculated **230 ft** DH, by contrast, remains 230 ft because it is already above the system minimum.

| ILS category | ICAO system minimum |
|---|---|
| CAT I | 60 m (200 ft) |
| CAT II | 30 m (100 ft) |
| CAT III | Not applicable |

## 5. Descent Gradients

### 8.39 Permissible and preferred gradients

An approach needs enough distance for the aircraft to descend from the procedure altitude to the runway. Excessive gradient is most critical on final because the aircraft must reach the correct threshold speed and retain the ability to decelerate. The chapter gives a desirable upper final-approach gradient of **5.2% (50 m/km; about 300 ft/NM)**, approximately **3°**, and a maximum normally allowable final gradient of **6.5% (65 m/km; about 400 ft/NM)**, approximately **3.8°**. A **3° glide path** is the operationally preferred precision path and is mandatory in the CAT II/III criteria stated here. ILS glide paths steeper than 3° are adopted only when other obstacle-clearance arrangements are impracticable.

### 8.40 High-rate descent approvals

A descent exceeding **6.5%** can produce unusually high rates of descent. It cannot be imposed simply to meet noise-abatement aims. Where an exceptional procedure is approved—for example the historical **9.5% / 5.5°** London City profile—it needs **specific State approval**, a suitably approved operator and aircraft, and **specially trained pilots**.

## 6. Track Reversal and Racetrack Procedures

### 8.41 Why track reversal may be required

Some aerodromes do not have the navigation-aid layout or surrounding terrain needed for a direct straight-in arrival. When a single facility is used both as the **IAF** and **MAPt**, an aircraft may need to depart the facility on an outbound heading and then turn back to intercept the inbound final approach track. The reversal may be accomplished using a **procedure turn** or **base turn**.

### 8.42 Procedure-turn geometry

A procedure turn reverses the aircraft from an outbound course to an **inbound course that is reciprocal** to it. The two standard types are the **45°/180°** and **80°/260°** turns.

## 7. Missed Approach Segment and Procedure

### 8.47 Purpose, initiation and phases

A missed approach is mandatory when the required visual criteria are unavailable at DA/H or MDA/H, or when a safe landing cannot be continued for any other reason. The **published instrument approach plate** specifies the missed-approach route and the related loss-of-RTF instructions. The pilot should initiate the prescribed climb promptly when the missed approach is selected, targeting at least the indicated MSA unless already at that height. The normal design climb gradient is **2.5%**. A missed approach comprises **initial**, **intermediate** and **final** phases.

### 8.48 Initial missed approach phase

The initial phase begins at **MAPt** and continues until a positive climb has been established. The crew is occupied with the go-around manoeuvre, climb initiation and aircraft reconfiguration; accordingly **no turns are specified** in this phase. The design assumes initiation no lower than **DA/H** for precision approaches, or at the specified MAPt and not below **MDA/H** for non-precision approaches.

When the MAPt is based on a facility or fix such as a middle marker, its distance from the FAF is generally published, and **timing may be used** to locate it if authorised. Otherwise the plate must explicitly prohibit defining MAPt by timing. The MAPt can be identified by the intersection of glideslope and DA/H, a navigation facility, a designated fix or a stated distance from the FAF.

### 8.49 Navigation when going around

If the required outside references are absent on reaching MAPt, the pilot must begin the missed approach immediately to remain within protected airspace. Where the aircraft starts climbing **before** MAPt, it normally continues to MAPt before using the published lateral missed-approach path. Crossing MAPt **above** the stated minimum altitude/height is permissible, provided the applicable procedure is followed.

### 8.50 Intermediate missed approach phase

The climb continues, usually straight ahead, although limited turning of up to **15°** is allowed under the straight-departure criteria. Minimum obstacle clearance during this part is **30 m**. This phase ends at the earliest point where **50 m (164 ft)** of clearance is both attained **and sustainable**. The nominal climb gradient is **2.5%**, parallel to the obstacle-identification surface.

### 8.51 Final missed approach phase

The final stage starts after sustained **50 m (164 ft)** obstacle clearance has been established. It finishes when the aircraft enters another approach, a hold, or the en-route phase. **Full turns** can be included in this stage.

## 9. RNAV Approaches Based on VOR/DME

### 8.53 Nature of VOR/DME RNAV approaches

The VOR/DME RNAV approach is treated as a **non-precision procedure**. Its reference station normally consists of a **VOR and collocated DME**. Aircraft equipped with suitable RNAV systems approved by the State of the Operator may fly such a procedure when three conditions have been satisfied **before departure**: the RNAV equipment is operating correctly, the pilot knows how to operate it accurately and remains current in its use, and the published VOR/DME installation underlying the procedure is serviceable.

### 8.54 Risk of navigation-data entry error

The onboard navigation computer relies on **current, correctly programmed procedure information** entered or supplied through the operator or crew. The installation described here does not independently verify whether every inserted value is correct. A wrong input can therefore create plausible-looking but false **computed aircraft position information**.

### 8.55 Accuracy considerations and conventional instrument procedures

Overall VOR/DME RNAV accuracy depends on **five contributions**: tolerance of the ground navigation facility, tolerance of the airborne receiver, the pilot/autopilot's flight technical accuracy, the computational tolerance of the RNAV system and the aircraft's distance from the reference station.

RNAV equipment may also assist during a conventional (non-RNAV) procedure. In that case, the pilot must still monitor progress using the **underlying raw-data display** for that approach and observe the tolerances appropriate to the traditional navigation aid.
