# Chapter 7: Instrument Procedures — Departures

## 7.1 Instrument Procedures: General Introduction

Instrument procedures establish a protected route for an aircraft when external visual references cannot be relied upon. A departure procedure provides lateral guidance and protection from terrain and obstacles during the climb away from an aerodrome. An arrival procedure must also provide a defined route and obstacle clearance; where the type of approach equipment permits, it supplies vertical guidance as well.

In the terminology used here, **low-visibility operations** are take-offs and landings conducted with **runway visual range (RVR) below 800 m**. The operating limits for a particular procedure depend on the visibility available and, for an approach, the altitude or height to which descent is permitted—**DA/H** or **MDA/H**. The underlying design priority is to keep the aircraft safely clear of obstacles.

The operating minima referred to for a take-off from an aerodrome within a control zone are **ground visibility of at least 5 km** and a **cloud ceiling of at least 1,500 ft**. These values are distinct from the description of low-visibility operations.

An instrument departure or arrival is designed for conditions in which visual meteorological references may be insufficient. At a controlled aerodrome, the IFR flight is subject to air traffic control. Consequently, the pilot must receive an **ATC clearance before commencing the instrument procedure**.

Departure and arrival instructions are published as procedure charts, commonly called *plates*. The flight crew must have the necessary charts available in the cockpit. If an aircraft is diverted to an unfamiliar aerodrome and its crew does not have the relevant plate, ATC may communicate the procedure by radio, including applicable **lost-communications** and **missed-approach** instructions.

## 7.3 Obstacle Clearance

A published instrument procedure must provide an acceptable margin between the aircraft and the surrounding terrain or obstacles. Protection may be achieved by keeping the aircraft **laterally away** from hazards, by maintaining sufficient **vertical separation**, or by combining both methods.

A designer first defines the route and surveys the ground and obstacles around it. The area protected by the procedure cannot be limited to the exact nominal track: navigation equipment and the pilot's ability to follow guidance both have tolerances. The protected area therefore accounts for expected deviations. Near the nominal track the obstacle-clearance allowance is greater; toward the outer limits of the applicable protection area it progressively reduces. These criteria define the **minimum obstacle clearance (MOC)** provision.

One method of establishing a safe minimum altitude is to find the controlling obstacle and add the specified clearance margin. The resulting **obstacle clearance altitude or height (OCA/H)** contributes to the determination of **MSA** and, with further procedural criteria, the **minimum descent altitude or height (MDA/H)** for a non-precision approach.

For a precision approach, the aircraft is guided vertically, so an obstacle's **distance from the runway threshold** matters as well as its elevation. For example, on an illustrative path descending by about **300 ft per nautical mile**, an obstacle **1,000 ft high at 10 NM** from the threshold presents a different clearance problem from a **150 ft obstacle only 1 NM** from the threshold. Consequently, precision-approach OCA/H is related to distance along the approach and is generally lower than the corresponding non-precision minimum.

The protection margins used by the procedure designer represent the minimum acceptable margins for the procedure. An operator may impose more restrictive **aerodrome operating minima**. These commercial operating limits can therefore be higher than the published OCA/H.

## 7.4 PANS-OPS — ICAO Document 8168

International criteria for instrument procedures are developed in **ICAO Doc 8168**, usually referred to as **PANS-OPS**, meaning *Procedures for Air Navigation Services — Aircraft Operations*. It contains two volumes with different purposes:

- **Volume I — Flight Procedures:** operational guidance for flight crews and other flight-operations personnel. It explains the procedures as they are applied in flight and the principal parameters underlying the design rules.
- **Volume II — Construction of Visual and Instrument Flight Procedures:** guidance for specialists designing procedures, including protected areas and obstacle-clearance requirements.

The two volumes supplement the ICAO **Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs)** where more detailed, internationally consistent procedures are desirable. They extend the practical treatment of the requirements associated with **Annex 6**, principally for departures and arrivals, and also address relevant en-route obstacle-clearance matters.

## 7.5 Instrument Departure Procedures: General Criteria

The basic departure designs assume **all engines are operating**. The characteristics of the ground around an aerodrome normally exert the greatest influence on the departure track. Other constraints may include nearby ATS routes, restricted or prohibited airspace, danger areas, other aerodromes and the needs of ATC.

These constraints determine which navigation aids are needed and where they must be positioned. The flight crew and operator must also account for matters such as **automatic take-off thrust control systems (ATTCS)** and **noise-abatement procedures**.

A departure aligned with the runway is preferred where the terrain and airspace allow it. A change of direction of **more than 15°** calls for a **turning-departure** design. If suitable navigation aids cannot provide an accurately defined track, an **omni-directional departure** can be established instead.

## 7.6 Departure Requirements

Where instrument departures are necessary, procedures are prepared for the runways concerned and for the applicable aircraft categories. The normal, **all-engines-operating procedure design gradient (PDG)** is **3.3%**, although a steeper climb gradient can be published where obstacles make it necessary.

A departure defined by a **track to be made good** requires the pilot to compensate for known or estimated wind in order to remain on that track. This is different from **radar vectors**: when ATC issues a heading, the pilot flies the assigned heading rather than adding an independent wind correction to that heading.

## 7.7 Departure Obstacle Clearance and Procedure Design Gradient

The assumed standard PDG is **3.3%** unless another value is published. It consists of:

- **2.5%** for the obstacle-identification-surface (OIS) gradient, or a greater value if the limiting obstacle makes that necessary; plus
- **0.8%** for the progressively increasing obstacle-clearance allowance.

Thus, for the standard design case:

$$
\mathrm{PDG} = 2.5\% + 0.8\% = \boxed{3.3\%}
$$

If the departure initially needs a higher gradient, its chart specifies the altitude or height to which that higher climb must be maintained. The standard **3.3%** gradient applies once the limiting obstacle conditions permit it. The published departure continues until clearance can be established for the next phase of flight—**en route, holding or approach**—and finishes at a designated significant point.

At the **departure end of runway (DER)**, the assumed minimum obstacle clearance is **zero**. Beyond the DER it increases by **0.8% of horizontal distance travelled** along the flight direction, using a maximum lateral divergence assumption of **15°**.

$$
\mathrm{MOC\ increase} = 0.008 \times \mathrm{horizontal\ distance}
$$

For turning departures, the **turn-initiation area** must provide at least **90 m (295 ft)** of obstacle clearance. Additional protection is applied in mountainous terrain. Where **DME** is available, the published procedure can provide supplementary distance and height information.

## 7.8 Mountainous Terrain

The criteria do not give a single universal definition of **mountainous terrain**. In deciding whether to apply additional clearance, a procedure designer considers the local wind environment. Where an **average wind of 37 km/h or more** produces significant downdraughts, increased obstacle-clearance protection is applied.

## 7.9 Aircraft Category and Departure Speed

An aircraft's speed affects the space needed to make a turn and therefore the boundaries of the protected departure area. For the turning-departure criteria, categories are associated with maximum permitted speeds. Where a different limiting speed is published for a particular procedure, **that published limit takes precedence**. An aircraft unable to comply with the speed limit must obtain an alternative departure procedure.

## 7.10 Standard Instrument Departures (SIDs)

Instrument departure routes are generally organized as either **straight** or **turning** departures. The procedure is designed so that pilots acquire adequate track guidance within the specified distance from the runway or from completion of the turn:

- **Straight departure:** track guidance acquired within **20 km (10.8 NM) of the DER**.
- **Turning departure:** track guidance acquired within **10 km (5.4 NM) after completing the turn**.

Aircraft fly the published ground tracks, making the necessary corrections for wind, within the protected airspace.

## 7.11 Straight Departures

For a **straight departure**, the initial track is no more than **15°** from the runway alignment. Suitable track guidance may be supplied by **VOR, NDB or RNAV**.

## 7.12 Turning Departures

When the desired departure path requires a turn exceeding **15°**, the procedure establishes an appropriate turning area. The aircraft begins that turn at a published **altitude/height**, **navigation fix**, or **navigation facility** such as a VOR or NDB.

The design assumes that the aircraft flies straight until reaching at least **120 m (394 ft) above DER elevation**. The turning area also provides a minimum **90 m (295 ft)** obstacle-clearance margin at turn initiation.

## 7.14 Omni-directional Departures

An **omni-directional departure** is used where no suitable system supplies continuous track guidance for a specific initial departure track. Instead of following a defined SID track from the outset, the aircraft climbs along the extended runway centreline and can then turn toward the route required, subject to the protected sectors and any published restrictions.

Obstacles may make some directions unavailable. In that case, the departure information identifies sectors to avoid or gives a minimum gradient, altitude or height for the relevant sector.

The normal conditions are:

- The aircraft initially climbs on the **extended runway centreline** until reaching **120 m (394 ft) above aerodrome elevation** before turning.
- Before initiating turns **greater than 15°**, at least **90 m (295 ft)** of obstacle clearance must be available.
- A turn must **not begin within 600 m of the runway beginning**.

If obstacles prevent a safe omni-directional departure, the pilot must use a defined straight or turning departure route, or be able to avoid obstacles visually under suitable cloud-ceiling and visibility conditions.

## 7.15 Publication and Interpretation of Departure Procedures

Departure routes and SID charts are published under the charting and ATS arrangements associated with **ICAO Annexes 4 and 11**. A procedure is designated **RNAV** when area navigation, such as VOR/DME-based or satellite-based navigation, is its principal method of route guidance.

For omni-directional procedures, charts describe **prohibited sectors**, or give **minimum climb gradients and minimum altitudes/heights** necessary to clear obstacles. A published **Minimum Sector Altitude (MSA)** gives the lowest safe altitude within the stated sector, normally extending to **25 NM** from the named aerodrome or navigation facility.

A **SID** begins at the **DER** and finishes where the aircraft joins the **ATS en-route structure**. SIDs have distinct identifiers, such as **CPT3G**. An ATC clearance may authorize the aircraft to fly a particular SID to the first en-route point; the controller identifies the required route by its designated name.

The Compton departures from **London Heathrow**, for example, lead toward the **Compton VOR (CPT)**. Navigation references may include the **London (LON)** and **Compton (CPT) VORs**, **Woodley (WOD) NDB** and **DME**. The chart describes the required sequence using VOR radials, NDB QDMs, DME distances and any necessary altitude restrictions.

Restrictions may be expressed as **“Above …”** or **“At …”**. In addition to the route picture, the published plate includes a written route description in **English**, enabling the crew to follow the procedure and subsequently enter the airway or continue under radar control. ATC can replace or shorten part of the SID by **radar vectoring**. When that happens, the controller informs the pilot that the aircraft is under radar control.

## 7.16 RNAV- and RNP-Based Departure Procedures

RNAV departures may use **VOR/DME**, **DME/DME**, **basic GNSS** or **RNP** criteria. Many aircraft equipped with a **flight management system (FMS)** can fly an RNAV departure, but individual procedures may limit which navigation systems are acceptable.

When an **RNP-based** procedure is to be flown, the aircraft's RNAV system must be **approved for the published RNP requirement**. Before departure, the pilot must confirm the availability of relevant ground navigation facilities where required, establish that the aircraft can meet the RNP performance needed for every segment and continue to **monitor navigation-system accuracy** throughout the procedure.

## 7.17 Types of RNAV Turns

An RNAV departure procedure may specify four types of turn:

1. A turn at a **fly-by waypoint**.
2. A turn at a **fly-over waypoint**.
3. A turn at a specified **altitude or height**.
4. A **fixed-radius turn**, generally used in RNP-based procedures.

## 7.18 Using an FMS on Conventional Departures

An FMS may be used to navigate a published **conventional** departure, provided the crew also monitors progress on the basic navigation display normally associated with that procedure. The aircraft must continue to meet the **same track-keeping tolerances that apply when flying the procedure using the underlying raw navigation data**.
