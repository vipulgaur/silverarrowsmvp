# Chapter 25 — Security

## Introduction

### Definitions

The following security terms have specific meanings and are examinable.

| Item | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Airside** | The airport movement area, adjoining land, and all or part of the associated buildings for which access is controlled. This area was previously termed the *aeronautical part*. |
| **Screening** | The use of technical equipment or other methods to discover or identify weapons, explosives, and any other devices, articles or substances capable of being employed in an act of unlawful interference. |
| **Security** | The protection of civil aviation against unlawful interference, achieved through combined protective measures and the deployment of people and physical resources. |
| **Security restricted area** | A part of an airport's airside designated as a higher-priority risk area. Access is controlled and additional security measures apply. Such areas normally encompass commercial passenger departure areas between screening and aircraft boarding, ramps, baggage assembly areas, locations containing aircraft being prepared for service or screened baggage or cargo, cargo sheds, mail centres, airside catering facilities, and aircraft-cleaning premises. |
| **Unidentified baggage** | An item of baggage at an airport, whether tagged or untagged, which has neither been collected by a passenger nor linked to an identifiable passenger. |

### 25.1 Annex 17

**ICAO Annex 17 — Security** contains the Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) applicable to aviation security under the Chicago Convention. Other ICAO publications also provide material concerning the practical implementation of aviation security policy.

## Objectives

### 25.2 General

Preventing unlawful interference with civil aviation operations is the central purpose of aviation security. In giving effect to this purpose, each Contracting State must place the protection of passengers, flight crews, personnel working on the ground and members of the public foremost in its civil aviation security arrangements.

### 25.3 Organization

Contracting States must create an organizational structure supported by plans and operating procedures that provides a consistent security standard for international flights under normal conditions. These arrangements must also be capable of being intensified quickly when the level of threat increases.

### 25.4 Facilitation

Insofar as practicable, States should organize security procedures to avoid unnecessary interruption or delay to international civil aviation. Security protection and the efficient movement of international air traffic must therefore be considered together.

## Organization

### 25.5 National Organization

A **national civil aviation security programme** must be established by every Contracting State. Its purpose is to protect civil aviation operations from unlawful interference through a framework of regulations, operating practices and procedures, while taking account of flight safety, regularity and efficiency.

The national arrangements must include the following functions:

- Appoint an appropriate authority with responsibility for coordinating civil aviation security activities.
- Give effect to the national security programme and ensure that it satisfies the requirements of international traffic.
- Establish security committees at airports.
- Prepare contingency plans for incidents involving unlawful interference.
- Ensure that suitable security-training programmes are put into operation.

The State may also apply the programme to domestic aviation services. A copy of the national programme must be supplied to ICAO.

### 25.6 Airport Design

Airport infrastructure must be planned with aviation security in mind. Contracting States must see that the physical, architectural and infrastructural requirements needed for security controls are incorporated when new airport facilities are designed and constructed, as well as when existing airport facilities are modified.

### 25.7 International Co-operation

A common, coordinated approach among States is fundamental to countering international terrorism directed at aviation. Each Contracting State is therefore required to cooperate with others in the following ways:

- Modify or align its national civil aviation security arrangements where necessary in cooperation with other States.
- Provide, when another State requests them, the relevant portions of the national civil aviation security programme in written form.
- Incorporate an aviation security provision into bilateral air transport agreements.
- Meet, as far as practicable, requests made by other States for additional protective measures on an individual flight or specified flights conducted by operators from those States.
- Collaborate in the development of security-training programmes and exchange information about those programmes.
- Participate in joint research and development aimed at producing better security equipment suited to international civil aviation requirements.

## Preventative Security Measures

### 25.8 Prohibited Objects

Each Contracting State must prevent unauthorized weapons, explosives and other hazardous devices capable of being used for unlawful interference from reaching an aircraft employed in international civil aviation, irrespective of the means by which their introduction might be attempted.

Particular attention is necessary where explosive devices could be disguised within, or employ, electrical, electronic or battery-operated articles. This applies to items carried in the cabin and to checked baggage.

### 25.9 Protection of Cargo, Mail and Other Goods

The protective arrangements applicable to cargo, mail and other goods include three principal controls:

1. **Pre-loading screening:** cargo and other goods are screened before being placed aboard the aircraft.
2. **Control of the supply chain:** agents and known consignors are approved and subjected to appropriate security screening.
3. **Restricted access:** entry to protected cargo and goods areas is limited to persons with authorization.

### 25.10 Law Enforcement Officers

When police officers or other duly authorized persons are required to carry weapons aboard an aircraft in the course of their duties, the relevant Contracting States should require specific authorization consistent with the laws of the States involved.

The pilot-in-command must be told both **how many armed persons are aboard** and **where they are seated**. Approval for the transit of armed security personnel is also required from the States of departure and destination, every State along the route, and the aerodrome authorities concerned.

### 25.11 Passengers and Their Baggage

Contracting States must provide controls for passengers in transit or transferring between flights, together with their cabin baggage, so that prohibited articles cannot be brought onto aircraft operating international services.

After security screening has taken place, persons who have passed through those controls must remain segregated from individuals who have not been screened. If the two groups mix or have contact in circumstances that compromise this separation, the affected passengers and their baggage must undergo screening again before they board.

### 25.12 Deportees and Persons in Custody

States must have procedures under which the aircraft operator and pilot-in-command are informed whenever deportees or persons under custody are to travel, allowing the necessary security measures to be applied.

Such individuals are normally boarded ahead of the other passengers. The operator must have established carriage procedures for deportees and persons in custody, including passengers considered potentially disruptive because they are obliged to travel as a result of judicial or administrative action.

### 25.13 Checked Baggage and Other Goods

Measures must be in place to prevent an operator from transporting checked baggage belonging to a passenger who is not travelling on that aircraft. Such baggage may be accepted only where **both** of the following conditions are met:

- It is carried in a compartment separated from the passengers.
- Additional security controls have been applied to it.

### 25.14 Access

To keep unauthorized persons and vehicles from entering protected airport areas, each State must introduce suitable access procedures and means of identification. The controls must apply to the airside of aerodromes serving international civil aviation and to other facilities important to airport security, including air traffic control facilities.

## Management of Response to Acts of Unlawful Interference

### 25.15 Safety of Passengers and Crew

Where an aircraft has been subjected to unlawful interference, the Contracting State must provide appropriate safeguards for the people aboard. Protection of passengers and crew must continue until they are able to resume their journey.

### 25.16 Air Traffic Control

A Contracting State responsible for ATS provision to an aircraft affected by unlawful interference must assemble all relevant information about the flight and pass it to the other States and ATS units involved. This includes units at an aerodrome known or reasonably believed to be the aircraft's destination. The purpose is to enable timely protective measures both during the flight and at any confirmed, probable or possible landing location.

The State should also arrange for information obtained through these measures to reach the ATS units concerned, relevant airport administrations, the aircraft operator and any other appropriate parties locally as soon as practicable.

### 25.17 Provision of Assistance

An aircraft that has been unlawfully seized must receive the assistance required by the situation from the Contracting State concerned. Such assistance includes the provision of **air navigation aids**, **air traffic services**, and **permission to land**.

### 25.18 Detention on the Ground

When an unlawfully seized aircraft lands within a State's territory, that State must take practicable steps to keep it on the ground. Departure may be permitted where the paramount need to preserve human life makes it necessary.

Where circumstances permit, the State in which the aircraft has landed should consult the State of the aircraft operator. It should also notify the States identified, or believed, to be the aircraft's intended destinations.

During the period of detention, arrangements must be made to protect the passengers and crew and meet their comfort needs until onward travel becomes possible.

### 25.19 Operator Security Programme

Every Contracting State must require air transport operators to establish, apply and keep current a **written operator security programme** consistent with the State's national aviation security programme.

Where an operator enters a code-sharing arrangement, it must check and ensure that the partner airline meets the requirements of the operator's security programme.

## Further Security Information

### 25.20 Other Annexes and Documents

Aviation security provisions also appear in other ICAO Annexes and documents. The following requirements are part of the relevant security material.

### 25.21 Extracts from Annex 2 — Rules of the Air

**Unlawful interference.** The crew of an aircraft experiencing unlawful interference must endeavour to inform the appropriate ATS unit. The notification should cover the incident, relevant significant circumstances, and any resulting changes to the current flight plan. These details allow ATS to prioritize the aircraft and limit conflict with other traffic.

Where interference prevents the aircraft from making that notification, the following procedures provide guidance.

**Action by the pilot-in-command.** Except where the situation on board makes this inappropriate, the pilot-in-command should remain on the assigned route at the assigned cruising level until the crew can communicate with an ATS unit or until the aircraft is within radar coverage.

**Departure from the assigned route or level.** If an aircraft must change its assigned track or cruising level without being able to establish radiotelephony communication, the pilot-in-command should, wherever circumstances permit:

- Broadcast a warning using the VHF emergency frequency and any other suitable frequencies, unless doing so would be inappropriate because of conditions on board. Where useful and possible, other equipment, including the transponder and data-link systems, should also be employed.
- Follow any applicable and published special procedures governing in-flight contingencies.
- In the absence of an applicable regional procedure, use a level separated from the normal IFR cruising levels of that area by **300 m (1,000 ft) above FL290**, or **150 m (500 ft) below FL290**.

### 25.22 Extracts from Annex 6 — Operation of Aircraft (Part I — International Commercial Air Transport)

**Flight crew compartment security.** An aeroplane equipped with a door to the flight crew compartment must have a means of locking that door. The locking function must be operable **only from within the flight crew compartment**.

**Aircraft search checklist.** Operators must provide, on board the aircraft, a checklist setting out the search procedure to be used when sabotage involving a bomb is suspected. It must be accompanied by guidance for dealing with a discovered explosive or other suspicious object and must identify the aircraft-specific position at which a bomb would pose the least risk.

**Training programmes.** Operators must maintain training for crew members on responding to unlawful interference in a manner intended to minimize its consequences. They must also train appropriate staff in preventive methods relating to passengers, baggage, cargo, mail, equipment, stores and supplies destined for aircraft carriage. This training is intended to support the prevention of sabotage and other unlawful acts.

**Reporting.** After unlawful interference has occurred, the pilot-in-command must report the incident to the designated local authority without delay.

**Least-risk bomb location.** The aircraft should have specialized equipment or arrangements to reduce the effects of a blast and direct its force at the designated least-risk bomb location.

**Stowage of weapons.** If an operator agrees to carry weapons taken from passengers, provision should be made for storing those weapons where no person can reach them during flight.

### 25.23 Extracts from Annex 9 — Facilitation

**Transit and transfer of passengers and crew.** Where traffic volume and type justify it, Contracting States should provide airport arrangements that allow passengers and crew continuing on the same aircraft, or transferring to another flight, to remain temporarily in a transit area without routine inspection formalities. Aviation security controls and inspections required by exceptional circumstances remain applicable. This provision does not prevent appropriate narcotics-control procedures.

**Inadmissible persons.** A person refused admission to a destination State may be returned to the operator for transport to the State of departure or to another State willing to admit that person. The operator is not prevented from seeking reimbursement of the associated transport costs from the passenger in the State of departure.

**Deportees.** A Contracting State that removes deportees from its own territory is responsible for all duties and costs resulting from that removal.

**Unruly passengers.** Contracting States should ensure that the relevant employees of airport operators, aircraft operators and public authorities receive training to identify and manage disruptive passengers. Such training must encompass recognition of deteriorating situations, methods of preventing their escalation, and management of crises.

### 25.24 Extracts from the Procedures for Air Navigation Services — Rules of the Air and Air Traffic Services (Doc 4444)

**Emergency procedures.** The circumstances of aviation emergencies vary too widely for one exhaustive set of detailed instructions to cover every situation. The procedures provide general direction for ATS personnel. Controllers must exercise sound judgement and ATS units must maintain thorough coordination throughout the response.

An aircraft fitted with an SSR transponder may indicate an emergency using either of these Mode A settings:

- **Code 7700** — general emergency indication.
- **Code 7500** — a specific indication of unlawful interference.

**Priority.** Any aircraft experiencing, or believed to be experiencing, an emergency, including unlawful interference, is to receive priority over other aircraft.

**Recognition of unlawful interference.** ATS personnel must be ready to recognize indications that an aircraft may be experiencing an act of unlawful interference.

**Checking SSR indications.** When unlawful interference is suspected and SSR equipment does not automatically present distinct indications for Mode A Codes 7500 and 7700, the radar controller should check the suspicion by selecting the SSR decoder first to **7500** and then to **7700**. An aircraft under unlawful interference is expected to use Mode A **7500**. It may instead use **7700** where it faces serious and immediate danger and requires urgent assistance.

**ATC action.** As soon as unlawful interference is known or suspected, ATS units must address the aircraft's requests and likely requirements without delay. They should supply relevant details of navigation facilities, procedures and services on the proposed route and at intended landing aerodromes, and facilitate each stage of the flight to the extent necessary.

Further action required from ATS units comprises:

- Continue providing operationally important information to assist the safe conduct of the flight, even where no reply from the crew can be expected.
- Observe, record and plot the aircraft's progress using available surveillance and other facilities. Arrange transfer of control with neighbouring ATS units without requiring the crew to transmit or acknowledge messages unless aircraft communication remains normal.
- Advise the ATS units likely to be affected by the flight, including those in adjacent flight information regions, and keep those units updated.

  In passing advance information, ATS must consider fuel endurance, possible changes of destination and the potential for abrupt alterations of course. The intention is to provide each affected ATS unit with as much warning as practicable before the aircraft might enter its area of responsibility.

- Notify the aircraft operator or its nominated representative, the appropriate **rescue coordination centre (RCC)** using the applicable alerting procedures, and the designated aviation security authority. Subsequent notifications to other involved parties are expected to be handled by the operator and/or security authority under established procedures.
- Pass appropriate communications concerning the circumstances of the interference between the aircraft and the designated authorities.

### 25.25 Extracts from Annex 14 — Aerodromes

**Isolated aircraft parking position.** An aerodrome must identify a separate parking location for aircraft affected by unlawful interference. This location must be **at least 100 m from all other aircraft parking positions**. It must not be positioned above underground gas or aviation-fuel installations. Where practicable, electrical and communications cables should also be avoided.

### 25.26 Taxi Instruction Considerations

In issuing taxi instructions to an aircraft affected by unlawful interference, ATC must observe three principles:

1. Keep the aircraft as distant as possible from other aircraft and airport installations.
2. Give the pilot a clearly specified taxi route.
3. Choose the route so that security risks to the public, other aircraft and installations are minimized.
