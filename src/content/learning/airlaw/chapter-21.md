# Chapter 21 — Aerodrome Lighting

## Aerodrome Lights

### 21.1 Introduction

An aerodrome may display numerous different lights, making the overall scene difficult to interpret until the function of each installation is understood. Most aerodrome lighting is intended to assist safe aircraft operations, and the various systems have distinct purposes. A pilot needs to recognise the systems and understand how they are used. Except for approach lighting, however, the spacing of individual lights and groups of lights is not a required element of this course; detailed design requirements are also excluded.

Lighting installations are not identical at every aerodrome. ICAO provides Standards and Recommended Practices in **Annex 14**, but that framework has not resulted in one universally applied arrangement. Pilots must therefore be familiar with the different patterns they may encounter.

### 21.2 Aircraft Safety

Lighting unrelated to aviation can create a hazard if it is visible from an aerodrome or its vicinity and interferes with aircraft operations. Such a light must be removed from view or rendered harmless by **extinguishing it, screening it, or altering it** sufficiently to eliminate the danger.

### 21.3 Elevated Lights

Runway, stopway and taxiway lights mounted above the surface must be **frangible** so that an aircraft strike will not expose the aircraft to unnecessary structural hazards. Installation heights must also allow adequate clearance beneath propellers and jet-engine pods. Any elevated lights that are otherwise difficult to notice require suitable marking.

### 21.4 Light Intensity

As visibility deteriorates, especially in daylight, a lighting system may remain distinguishable when surface markings are no longer effective. Its output must therefore be sufficient for the poor-visibility conditions in which the aerodrome is expected to operate, whether by day or at night. Meeting the necessary intensity frequently requires a directional beam; where directional fittings are selected, their illuminated sectors must be wide enough and correctly aimed for the intended operations.

The intensities of the various runway lights cannot be considered independently. Runway lighting must perform as a coordinated system, with enough intensity for the lowest intended visibility in the ambient illumination conditions. Where approach lighting is fitted, the intensity of the first runway-lighting section must also be compatible with that of the nearby approach-lighting section. Approach lights may legitimately be brighter than runway lights, but a sudden difference in brightness should be avoided: during an approach, it could cause an erroneous impression that the prevailing visibility has changed.

### 21.5 Intensity Control

A high-intensity lighting installation requires a means of adjusting its output for the conditions at the time. Individual controls, or equivalent arrangements, must allow installed lighting groups to be matched in apparent intensity. This applies to the following systems:

- Approach lighting;
- Runway edge lighting;
- Runway threshold lighting;
- Runway end lighting;
- Runway centre-line lighting;
- Runway touchdown-zone lighting; and
- Taxiway centre-line lighting.

### 21.6 Availability

Aerodrome lights may be extinguished when not needed, subject to the requirement that they can be brought back into operation **within 1 hour**.

### 21.7 Emergency Lights

Aerodromes ordinarily use a standby electrical supply to maintain lighting during a failure of the normal power source. If no alternative power supply is installed, **emergency lighting must at least be available for the primary runway**.

### 21.8 Aeronautical Beacons

For an aerodrome used at night, an **aerodrome beacon** or **identification beacon** is installed when operational circumstances make it necessary. The decision depends on the aircraft using the aerodrome, how clearly the aerodrome stands out from the surrounding terrain and lighting, and whether other visual or non-visual navigation aids already make its location sufficiently apparent.

### 21.9 Aerodrome Beacon

An aerodrome beacon uses **flashing** light to help a pilot find an aerodrome. A **land aerodrome** beacon displays either **white flashes** or **alternating white and green flashes**; a **water aerodrome** beacon displays **white** or **alternating white and yellow** flashes.

Where night operations are intended, an aerodrome beacon is required if one or more of these circumstances applies:

- Navigation by the aerodrome's users is predominantly visual;
- Reduced visibility occurs frequently; or
- Terrain features or surrounding lights make identification from the air difficult.

### 21.10 Identification Beacon

An aerodrome that operates at night and cannot readily be recognised from the air by other methods is provided with an **identification beacon**. Instead of the general locating flashes of an aerodrome beacon, it transmits the aerodrome identification in **Morse code**. The flashes are **green** at a civilian land aerodrome, **red** at a UK military aerodrome and **yellow** at a water aerodrome.

## Approach Lighting Systems

### 21.11 General

Approach lighting gives the pilot a visual path toward the runway threshold or aiming point during darkness or reduced visibility. It consists of an arrangement of **fixed white lights with adjustable intensity**. The light pattern may communicate distance, assist attitude assessment and, in some installations, provide an indication of the aircraft's height relative to the approach plane.

The simplest designs use a lit centre line and one crossbar; systems for precision approaches, particularly **Category III**, can be considerably more elaborate. Because visual acquisition of the lighting may contribute to the required visual references for landing, the design must accommodate the most demanding applicable **decision height (DH)** or **minimum descent height (MDH)**. ICAO uses a **300 m** segment length as the fundamental design unit for the approach-light pattern.

Any **ILS or MLS azimuth antenna** that projects into the plane established by the approach lights must be treated as an obstacle, including appropriate marking and lighting.

### 21.12 Calvert Systems

The Calvert approach-light arrangement, associated particularly with UK aerodromes but also encountered elsewhere, contains **five crossbars** together with a **distance-coded centre line**. The pattern uses the centre-line light arrangement as an additional cue to distance along the approach.

A comparable **NATO** system omits the centre-line distance coding.

### 21.13 Barrettes

The individual light sources in an approach-light pattern can be presented as separate points, as in a Calvert-style installation, or grouped into short transverse assemblies in the manner used by ICAO. Thus, a centre-line position may be represented by one point light or by **five closely grouped lights forming a short bar**. A close group of **three or more lights** of this kind is a **barrette**—a term derived from a word meaning a small bar. The distinction in terminology avoids confusing the short grouping of lamps with one of the major crossbars belonging to the approach-lighting layout.

### 21.14 Simple Approach Lighting System

In a simple approach-light arrangement, one line of lights follows the extended runway centre line for at least **420 m** from the threshold, wherever the available site permits. A single transverse crossbar, either **18 m** or **30 m** long, lies **300 m** before the threshold. That crossbar is placed as close as practicable to a horizontal straight line, at right angles to the centre-line lights, with an equal extent on each side of the centre line.

This form of installation is suitable for a **non-instrument runway** and may also be used on a **non-precision instrument runway**.

### 21.15 Precision Approach CAT I Lighting System

A **Category I precision approach** uses approach centre-line lights extending **900 m** outward from the threshold wherever possible. The nominal length is important: on a **$3^\circ$ glide path**, it corresponds approximately to the position of an aircraft at the **Category I minimum of 200 ft**. If the installed pattern does not reach 900 m, the aircraft may not yet be above any approach lights at its decision height.

The **five crossbars**, set **150 m apart**, provide a structure comprising three approach segments:

| Segment | Distance from threshold |
|---|---:|
| Inner | **0–300 m** |
| Middle | **300–600 m** |
| Outer | **600–900 m** |

Where an **ILS or MLS azimuth antenna** extends above the plane of the approach lights, it must be considered an obstacle and marked and illuminated as such.

### 21.16 ICAO Precision Approach CAT I Lighting System

The ICAO Category I configuration also aims to provide an approach-light centre line over **900 m**, but it uses a succession of **barrettes** rather than the Calvert distance-coded pattern. Its transverse arrangement contains **one crossbar at 300 m** from the threshold.

A line of strobes may supplement the centre line, flashing in sequence from the outer end of the installation **towards the runway threshold**. Rotating wing strobes may also be installed to improve recognition of the threshold.

### 21.17 Precision Approach Category II/III Lighting System

For **Category II and III** operations, additional approach-light detail makes the necessary visual references easier to acquire at decision height. Although installations differ, their foundations are either the **five-bar Calvert centre-line design** or the **ICAO barrette design**. Both are intended to extend **900 m** ahead of the threshold and to convey some information about aircraft attitude.

The principal enhancement is within the **inner 300 m** of the approach. Both types of system gain **supplementary approach lighting**, including **red wing barrettes**. In the Calvert version, the ordinary centre-line sources in that inner section are replaced by **barrettes**. These additions make the area immediately before the threshold more conspicuous and easier to identify.

A **Category II DH** is **not less than 100 ft**. On the example approach geometry used here, with the aircraft crossing the runway threshold at **50 ft**, this is associated with a position approximately **300 m** before the threshold.

### 21.18 Precision Approach Path Indicators (PAPI)

Once the visual part of a precision approach begins below decision height, **precision approach path indicators (PAPIs)** provide a reference for maintaining the required descent angle. The installation is a **wing bar of four equally spaced light units**. Each unit has a precisely established colour-change angle, with **white light visible above** that angle and **red light visible below** it. The units are individually adjusted to different transition angles, so the combined red/white indication identifies the aircraft's relationship to the required approach path, commonly **$3^\circ$**.

### 21.19 PAPI Indications

Because the indication depends on the sightline from the cockpit, it is the position of the **pilot's eye**—not the aircraft wheels—that determines the PAPI colours. The four lights provide **five** possible indications:

| Indicated position | PAPI lights, left to right |
|---|---|
| Well above the glide path | **White – White – White – White** |
| Slightly above the glide path | **White – White – White – Red** |
| On or close to the glide path | **White – White – Red – Red** |
| Slightly below the glide path | **White – Red – Red – Red** |
| Well below the glide path | **Red – Red – Red – Red** |

### 21.20 Circling Approach

A normal PAPI installation is positioned on the **left** of the runway. For a runway used for **circling approaches**, a PAPI wing bar is installed on **each side**. These two sets also give the pilot a horizontal visual reference for assessing aircraft attitude while completing the visual manoeuvre.

### 21.21 Minimum Eye Height (MEHT)

A PAPI indication applies to the height of the pilot's eye above the approach path, whereas safe runway clearance must account for the distance between that eye position and the lowest part of the undercarriage. If a PAPI were placed precisely at the runway threshold, following the indicated path could therefore cause the wheels to meet the ground **before** reaching the threshold. The point where this would occur depends on the aircraft's **eye-to-undercarriage distance**.

To provide suitable clearance, the **visual aiming point**, coinciding with the PAPI location in this description, is set some distance along the runway. For a **Code 4 instrument runway of at least 2400 m**, the aiming point is **400 m** beyond the threshold. At a normal **$3^\circ$** approach angle, this example gives a pilot's eye height of approximately **65 ft** when passing over the threshold.

However, the PAPI's **on-path** presentation occurs throughout a range of viewing angles, not at one exact median angle. The minimum relevant clearance is the pilot's eye height at the threshold when the PAPI still just shows the **lowest possible on-path indication**. That value is the **Minimum Eye Height (MEHT)**. It is shown beside the PAPI symbol on the **ICAO aerodrome chart**. A pilot can compare the published MEHT with the **eye-to-undercarriage requirement** for the aircraft type, which is covered in type-rating instruction, to determine whether the particular installation is suitable.

### 21.22 VASI

A standard **Visual Approach Slope Indicator (VASI)** comprises **two wing bars**, each containing **three lights**. The colour from each group changes between white and red according to the angle at which it is observed.

When the aircraft is correctly following the approach slope, the **nearer/first** bar appears **white** and the **farther/second** bar appears **red**. **Both white** indicates an approach above the intended path; **both red** indicates a path below it.

## Runway Lighting

### 21.23 Runway Edge Lights

Edge lighting is required for runways used **at night** and for **precision approach runways** used during either day or night. It should also be installed for daytime departures where the take-off minimum involves **RVR below approximately 800 m**.

Two parallel lines of lights extend down the **entire runway length**, each the same distance from the centre line. They are normally spaced uniformly, but some may be omitted or repositioned at runway intersections if the remaining pattern still gives pilots sufficient directional guidance.

Viewed along the approach direction, ordinary runway edge lights are **fixed white lights of adjustable intensity**. Special colours distinguish two areas:

- **Before a displaced threshold:** from the runway's physical start up to the displaced threshold, the edge lights appear **red** to an approaching aircraft.
- **Near the runway end:** a caution section may use **yellow** edge lights over the final **600 m** or the final **one-third of runway length**, whichever is shorter.

For installations also serving as **circling guidance**, runway edge lights must be visible in every direction (**omnidirectional**).

### 21.24 Runway Threshold and Wing Bar Lights

A runway with edge lighting also requires threshold lights, except where a **non-instrument or non-precision approach runway has a displaced threshold** and **wing-bar lights** are provided instead. For a threshold located at the physical runway end, the threshold lights form a straight transverse row **perpendicular to the runway centre line**, as close to that end as practicable. At a displaced threshold, light groups forming **wing bars (barrettes)** are placed at both sides of the threshold position.

Both threshold lights and wing-bar lights are **fixed, unidirectional GREEN lights**, directed toward approaching traffic.

### 21.25 Runway Threshold Identification Lights

Additional threshold emphasis may be achieved with **runway threshold identification lights**, especially on a **non-precision runway** when the threshold is insufficiently conspicuous or when it has been **permanently displaced**. They show **flashing WHITE** light, visible **only in the direction of approach**.

### 21.26 Runway End Lights

A runway fitted with edge lighting also has **runway end lights**. These display a **fixed RED unidirectional** indication towards an aircraft travelling along the runway. The row is aligned perpendicular to the runway’s longitudinal axis and is installed as close to the physical runway end as conditions permit. There should be **at least six** lights, either uniformly distributed between the two runway-edge rows or arranged in a symmetrical grouping about the centre line.

### 21.27 Runway Centre Line Lights

Centre-line lighting is installed on **Category II and III precision approach runways**, and should be included on a **Category I precision approach runway** if the distance between its two rows of edge lights is **greater than 50 m**. Centre-line lights are also required for a runway used for take-off at an operating minimum involving **RVR below approximately 400 m**.

These lights are **fixed** and have **variable intensity**. Their colour normally appears **white**, changing in the final part of the runway as follows:

| Distance remaining to runway end | Centre-line light colours |
|---|---|
| More than **900 m** | **White** |
| From **900 m** to **300 m** | **Alternating red and white** |
| Final **300 m** to runway end | **Red** |

### 21.28 Runway Touchdown Zone Lights

A **Category II/III precision approach runway** includes lighted touchdown-zone strips on both sides of its centre line. Where the runway is **1800 m or longer**, the installation runs for **900 m** from the threshold. Each strip occupies the same lateral width as the touchdown-zone pavement markings. The lights are **fixed**, **unidirectional**, **WHITE** and **variable in intensity**.

### 21.29 Stopway Lights

A stopway used at night is provided with lights over its **full length**. The stopway lighting presents **fixed, unidirectional RED light** towards the runway, with **variable intensity**.

### 21.30 Runway Lead-in Lights

A lead-in lighting route directs an aircraft along a selected approach track until the runway approach-light pattern can be seen. The route can consist of straight portions, curves or both. It is marked by groups containing **at least three flashing WHITE lights**, with the groups flashing progressively **towards the runway** wherever that sequence is practicable.

### 21.31 Circling Guidance Lights

Where approach and runway lights by themselves do not allow a pilot to locate the intended runway during a **circling-to-land manoeuvre**, dedicated circling guidance lights are provided. Their indication is **WHITE**, either **steady** or **flashing**.

## Taxiway Lighting

### 21.32 Application

Taxiway lighting helps a flight crew identify and follow ground routes between runway and parking areas. The systems include **centre-line lights**, **edge lights**, **runway guard lights** and **stop bars** at holding positions.

### 21.33 Taxiway Edge Lighting

Taxiway edge lights delineate taxiways and associated surfaces such as **holding bays**, **de-icing/anti-icing areas** and **aprons**. They are intended for nighttime use on taxiways without illuminated centre lines. Separate edge lights are not essential where alternative lighting, for example **floodlighting of sufficient strength**, already defines the route adequately.

If an active runway is also part of a **standard night taxi route** and that route has no taxiway centre-line lighting, it must have suitable edge lighting. The taxiway edge light is **fixed**, **BLUE**, **omnidirectional** and of **variable intensity**.

### 21.34 Taxiway Centre Line Lights

Taxiway centre-line lighting is used on **exit taxiways, taxiways and aprons** operated at **RVR below 350 m**, so that an aircraft can follow an uninterrupted lit route from the runway centre line to the apron location where parking manoeuvring begins. At an aerodrome with little traffic, centre-line lighting need not be provided if **edge lights and a painted centre-line marking** already offer sufficient guidance.

Where part of a **runway** also serves as a standard taxi route in **RVR below 350 m**, taxiway centre-line lighting is required. Ordinarily the lamps are **fixed, variable-intensity GREEN** and arranged so that they can be seen by aircraft on or close to the taxiway. **Alternating GREEN and YELLOW** indications are used within an **ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area**, and also on a **high-speed runway exit**. If the same taxiway centre line can be followed in either direction, an aircraft proceeding **towards the runway** must see **GREEN** from all the centre-line lights.

### 21.35 Stop Bars

A **stop bar** is a transverse line of **RED** lights directed at an aircraft approaching while taxiing. When lit, it means the aircraft must **stop** and remain stopped until **ATC clearance** permits further movement. Stop bars may supplement pavement markings and enable visually controlled traffic management at **taxiway intersections** and **taxi-holding positions**.

Every taxi-holding position giving access to a runway intended for operations at **RVR below 350 m** must have a stop bar. Two **elevated lights at each end** of the bar should be added where ground-mounted lights could disappear from view—for example, behind snow or heavy rain, or because the aircraft's own structure obscures them when it stops very close to the lights.

### 21.36 Intermediate Holding Position Lights

An intermediate taxiway holding position is indicated by **three fixed YELLOW lights**, arranged **at right angles to the taxiway**. The lights are **unidirectional**, facing the approaching aircraft.

### 21.37 Road-holding Position Lights

Lights controlling a road-holding position take one of two forms: a controllable **RED stop / GREEN go** signal, or a **flashing RED** signal.

### 21.38 Runway Guard Lights

Runway guard lights warn aircraft pilots and vehicle drivers that their route is about to lead onto an **active runway**. They are fitted at runway entries used with **RVR below 550 m** when there is **no stop bar**; they are also used when RVR is **550–1200 m** and **traffic density is high**.

Both permitted arrangements show **flashing YELLOW light towards approaching taxi traffic**. In **Configuration A**, there is a pair of warning lights on each side of the taxiway entrance. In **Configuration B**, the lights are arranged across the entrance at **3 m intervals**.
