# Chapter 15: Air Traffic Services

## 1. Introduction

### 15.1 Air Traffic Services (ATS)

**ICAO Annex 11** sets out the Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) under which contracting States establish air traffic services. Each State appoints an authority to regulate its ATS arrangements and ensures that suitable services are provided within the airspace for which it is responsible. In the United Kingdom, the **Civil Aviation Authority (CAA)** is the regulatory authority, while **National Air Traffic Services (NATS)** is the operating organization identified in this framework.

### 15.2 ICAO Document 4444

Annex 11 establishes the general requirements but does not contain every operational detail needed by ATS personnel. **ICAO Doc 4444, Procedures for Air Navigation Services — Air Traffic Management (PANS-ATM)**, provides the more detailed procedures for managing air traffic. Although primarily intended for ATS personnel, flight crews need familiarity with provisions affecting **air traffic management and services, separation, automatic dependent surveillance (ADS), controller–pilot data link communications (CPDLC), air traffic incident reports (ATIRs)** and **repetitive flight plans (RPLs)**.

### 15.3 Objectives of ATS

The combined ATS system has five purposes:

1. Reduce the risk of collision between aircraft.
2. Prevent aircraft from colliding with obstacles on the **manoeuvring area**.
3. Maintain an orderly traffic flow and facilitate the progress of flights.
4. Make available information and advice needed for safe, efficient operations.
5. Inform the appropriate organizations when an aircraft may need **search and rescue (SAR)** support and help those organizations respond.

### 15.4 Main Divisions of ATS

ATS comprises three distinct services:

| Division | Principal function |
|---|---|
| **Air Traffic Control Service (ATC)** | Controls designated traffic and provides the applicable separation and movement instructions. |
| **Flight Information Service (FIS)** | Supplies safety-related operational information to aircraft. |
| **Alerting Service** | Coordinates notification and assistance when an aircraft requires, or may require, emergency support. |

### 15.5 Divisions of Air Traffic Control

ATC itself is divided according to the stage of operation:

- **Area Control Service:** ATC for controlled flights in **control areas (CTAs)** and en-route controlled traffic within **FIRs** and **UIRs**.
- **Approach Control Service:** Control of arriving and departing controlled flights, generally in and around **control zones (CTRs)**.
- **Aerodrome Control Service:** ATC for aerodrome traffic at a controlled aerodrome.

### 15.6 Establishing the Required ATS

The appropriate service level depends on the operational environment. The factors used when assessing the need for ATS include **the nature of air traffic, traffic density, prevailing weather**, and any other relevant local constraints. Examples of such constraints include **mountainous ground, substantial expanses of water and limited navigation facilities**.

### 15.7 Effect of ACAS Carriage

Whether aircraft operating in a region carry an **Airborne Collision Avoidance System (ACAS)** does **not** determine the standard or level of ATS that the region must provide.

## 2. Air Traffic Control

### 15.8 Flights Receiving ATC

ATC applies to controlled flights operating in controlled airspace and to aerodrome traffic at controlled aerodromes. Under the airspace arrangements described here:

- **IFR flights** receive ATC when operating in controlled airspace.
- **VFR flights** receive ATC in **Classes B, C and D**, but the provision of service and separation depends on the class.
- **Special VFR (SVFR) flights** receive an ATC service.

### 15.9 ATS Units and Their Functions

ATS operations are supported by **Air Traffic Control Units (ATCUs)** and **Flight Information Centres (FICs)**. The unit's title generally identifies the service it provides:

| Unit | Typical task |
|---|---|
| **Area Control Centre (ACC)** | Provides Area Control to aircraft on en-route portions of controlled flights. |
| **Approach Control Office** | Manages arriving and departing controlled flights within its assigned terminal area. |
| **Aerodrome Control Tower** | Controls aerodrome traffic. |
| **Flight Information Centre (FIC)** | Provides flight information and, where applicable, alerting functions. |

ACC names commonly include a geographical designation, such as **London ACC**. Approach control may be based in an aerodrome control tower or consolidated at a facility responsible for several aerodromes. The **London Terminal Control Centre (LTCC)** is an example of an approach-control organization serving Heathrow, Gatwick, Stansted, Luton, London City, Biggin Hill and Northolt within the historical arrangement described here. **FIS and Alerting Service** within an FIR may be supplied by the same ATS unit.

### 15.10 Control of Surface Movement

The **aerodrome controller** is responsible for controlling aircraft and vehicle movement on the aerodrome as required. At larger locations, ground-movement functions may be delegated to a separate **ground controller**, keeping the aerodrome control radio frequency available principally for take-off, landing and associated safety traffic.

An **Apron Management Service** may manage aircraft and vehicles on the apron. In such cases, ATC responsibility commonly begins when an aircraft enters the taxiway system and ends when it passes to the directions of an appropriate marshaller or apron service.

### 15.11 Common Time Reference

ATS units use **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)** rather than local time. Time is expressed using the **24-hour clock**, with hours, minutes and, where needed, seconds. UTC is historically associated with **Zulu time** and Greenwich-based timekeeping. At **1200 UTC**, the mean Sun is approximately above the Greenwich meridian. A shared time reference permits consistent flight-plan interpretation and coordination between controlling units.

### 15.12 Accuracy of ATS Clocks

ATSU clocks and timing equipment are to be checked so that their indication remains within **±30 seconds of UTC**. When supplying a time check in whole minutes, the time is rounded to the **nearest minute**.

## 3. ATC Clearances

### 15.13 Meaning of an ATC Clearance

An **ATC clearance** is permission from an air traffic control unit for an aircraft to proceed subject to the conditions specified by that unit.

### 15.14 Why Clearances Are Issued

Clearances organize and separate air traffic using the traffic information available to the controller. They can apply to aircraft on the ground as well as aircraft in flight. A clearance must be delivered sufficiently early for the flight crew to understand and comply with it.

A pilot unable to comply with a clearance, or who considers it unsuitable, may request another. ATC may offer an alternative when practical, although a change may introduce a delay.

### 15.15 Responsibility for Safe Flight

Receiving a clearance does **not** transfer the pilot's fundamental responsibility for the safety of the aircraft, for avoiding terrain or for following the **Rules of the Air**. When a controller is **radar-vectoring** an aircraft, the controller is required to issue vectors that maintain the necessary obstacle clearance.

### 15.16 Separation Provided Through Clearances

The required separation service depends on the airspace class:

- **IFR–IFR separation** is normally supplied within controlled airspace.
- In **Classes B and C**, IFR traffic is separated from VFR traffic.
- In **Class B**, VFR aircraft are also separated from other VFR aircraft.
- **SVFR traffic** operating within a CTR is normally separated according to the applicable special-VFR arrangements.

### 15.17 Information in an ATC Clearance

Clearances should be short, unambiguous and expressed using recognized terminology. The information included, as applicable, is:

- **Aircraft identification** corresponding to the flight plan.
- **Clearance limit**, if one applies.
- **Cleared route** or an identifiable published route or procedure.
- **Assigned level**, including an initial or current level where relevant.
- Other instructions needed for the flight, such as **SID**, **STAR**, communications instructions or clearance expiry.
- **Transition level** in an approach clearance when prescribed or requested.
- **QNH**, unless already known to have been received, in the relevant circumstances: the first clearance to an altitude below the transition level, an approach clearance, a clearance to join an aerodrome traffic circuit or a taxi clearance for departure.

Standardized route references can simplify a clearance: for example, ATC may clear a flight via its **flight-planned route**, a named **Midhurst departure**, or an **Ockham arrival**. The pilot must then use the relevant published procedure to determine the detailed routing.

### 15.18 Coordinating Clearances Between FIRs

The **ACC responsible for the FIR of departure** has responsibility for the initial en-route clearance. Ideally, the route would be coordinated with each subsequent ACC before it was issued. For long routes, obtaining prior agreement from every ACC is often impractical. An initial clearance may therefore extend only through the originating FIR, or into the next FIR when early transfer of responsibility is expected.

Thereafter, clearances may be issued successively by downstream ACCs. Occasionally, the pilot may be instructed to communicate directly with the next FIR to obtain permission **before crossing its boundary**.

### 15.19 Mandatory Read-back Items

Pilots must read back safety-critical instructions transmitted by voice. The items specifically requiring read-back include:

1. **ATC route clearances**.
2. Runway-related clearances: **landing, take-off, crossing, entering, backtracking and holding short**.
3. **Runway in use**.
4. **Altimeter settings**.
5. **SSR/transponder codes**.
6. **Levels, headings and speeds**.
7. **Transition levels**.

### 15.20 Air Traffic Flow Management (ATFM)

**ATFM** uses traffic predictions based on filed plans, including **repetitive flight plans (RPLs)**, to anticipate demand in individual airspace sectors. When predicted demand exceeds capacity, the relevant unit advises operators and crews about restrictions or likely delays. Publishing such limitations makes the allocation of disruption more transparent.

Where delays can be predicted, it is preferable to **hold a flight on the ground** rather than let it reach the busy terminal area and wait airborne. Ground-based delay management supports economy, environmental protection and operational safety.

## 4. Persons and Vehicles on Aerodromes

### 15.21 Movement Control on the Manoeuvring Area

The aerodrome control tower regulates, as necessary, the movement of people, road vehicles and **aircraft being towed** on the manoeuvring area to prevent interference with aircraft landing, taking off or taxiing.

### 15.22 Low-Visibility Operations

When low-visibility procedures are operating, with **ground visibility below 800 m**, the number of people and vehicles allowed onto the manoeuvring area is kept as small as possible. Further precautions protect **ILS/MLS sensitive areas** during **CAT II and CAT III** precision operations, identified here in connection with **ground visibility below 550 m**.

### 15.23 Emergency Vehicle Priority

A vehicle responding to an accident or incident has priority over other **surface vehicles and movement traffic**, subject to the requirements for safe control of aerodrome operations.

### 15.24 Vehicle Right-of-Way Rules

Vehicles moving on the manoeuvring area follow this hierarchy:

- Vehicles, including vehicles **towing aircraft**, yield to aircraft that are landing, taxiing or taking off.
- Ordinary vehicles yield to a **vehicle towing an aircraft**.
- Between ordinary vehicles, priority is determined by **ATS unit instructions**.
- All such movements remain subject to **aerodrome control tower instructions**, irrespective of other right-of-way rules.

## 5. Flight Information Service

### 15.25 FIS Within a Flight Information Region

A **Flight Information Region (FIR)** is the basic airspace unit in which **Flight Information Service** and **Alerting Service** are available. An FIR does not necessarily contain controlled airspace; where controlled airspace exists, ATC may also operate within it. **ATC tasks take priority over FIS tasks** when provided by the same system.

FIS depends on timely aviation information. Its operation is supported by the State's **Aeronautical Information Service (AIS)**, established under **ICAO Annex 15**.

### 15.26 Flight Information Service Officers

Outside controlled airspace and at aerodromes without aerodrome ATC, FIS may be provided by **Flight Information Service Officers (FISOs)** working from a **Flight Information Centre** or an appropriate aerodrome unit. The radio callsign of a unit providing this service normally ends in **“Information.”**

A FISO is licensed for the flight information function, not for general air traffic control. The limited exception described here concerns instructions to **aircraft taxiing before entering the runway for take-off at a controlled aerodrome**. In the historical UK example, **London Information** supplies FIS for three geographical sectors of the London FIR using dedicated VHF channels.

### 15.27 Who Receives FIS and What It Contains

Information is supplied to aircraft likely to benefit from it, notably aircraft already receiving ATC and aircraft otherwise known to the ATS unit.

FIS may transmit information about:

- **SIGMET and AIRMET** weather warnings.
- **Volcanic activity**.
- **Radioactive or toxic releases** into the atmosphere.
- Changes in the availability of **radio-navigation facilities**.
- Significant changes affecting aerodrome conditions.
- **Unmanned free balloons**.
- Other matters affecting the safety of flight.

Other information provided as relevant includes:

- Observed or forecast weather at **departure, destination and alternate aerodromes**.
- **Collision hazards** for aircraft in **Classes C, D, E, F and G**.
- Details of **surface vessels** for flights over water, when requested.

In addition to FIS provided through two-way VHF communications, scheduled routine information is disseminated through the **Operational Flight Information Service (OFIS)**.

### 15.28 AIRMET

An **AIRMET** advises of specified en-route meteorological phenomena **affecting low-level aircraft** that have occurred or are expected, when those phenomena were **not included in the low-level forecast** for the relevant FIR or its sub-area.

### 15.29 SIGMET

A **SIGMET** is issued by a **meteorological watch office** to advise about specified significant en-route weather phenomena that are occurring or expected and could endanger aircraft operations.

OFIS has three broadcast forms:

- **HF OFIS**.

### 15.31 Automatic Terminal Information Service

**ATIS** may be supplied as **Voice ATIS** or **Data ATIS**. The relevant **ATS organization** is responsible for preparing and disseminating the information.

### 15.32 Arriving and Departing Traffic

Voice ATIS is used where repeating routine aerodrome information on an ATC frequency would otherwise cause unnecessary congestion. An aerodrome may provide:

- One ATIS broadcast for **arrivals**.
- One ATIS broadcast for **departures**.
- One combined broadcast serving **arrivals and departures**.
- Separate arrival and departure broadcasts when a combined message would be exceptionally long.

### 15.33 Voice ATIS Frequencies

Voice ATIS normally uses a **dedicated VHF frequency** published on the aerodrome chart. It may also be sent over the voice facility of a suitable **VOR**, such as a navaid associated with a departure or an approach fix. **ATIS must not be transmitted on the ILS voice channel.** Voice broadcasts repeat continuously.

### 15.34 Currency and Identification of ATIS

An ATIS message normally concerns **one aerodrome** and, in this framework, should be **no longer than 30 seconds**. A change of operational significance requires an immediate update. Consecutive versions are identified by sequential **letter designators**.

On the first call to ATC, the crew reports the identifier of the ATIS information received. One example of the required communication content is:

> “Oxford Tower, G-ABCD, bay 27, information Charlie, QNH 1003, request start.”

### 15.35 Cloud Information in ATIS

The cloud cover is included in ATIS when the base is **below 5,000 ft** or **below the highest minimum sector altitude (MSA)**, **whichever of those thresholds is higher**. Information about **cumulonimbus** is included regardless of that criterion.

### 15.36 ATIS No Longer Current

If the aircraft reports receiving an earlier ATIS version, **ATC must pass the changed information without delay** so the pilot is operating with current data.

## 6. Alerting Service

### 15.37 Purpose and Priority

The ATS **Alerting Service** links aircraft that need, or may soon need, assistance with the organizations able to respond. Any ATS unit—including an **ACC**, **ATCU**, **FIC** or relevant aerodrome unit—may perform alerting duties even if that is not its normal main function. When necessary, emergency-related work takes precedence over routine ATS activity.

### 15.38 Aircraft Covered and Notification Chain

Alerting Service is provided for:

- **All aircraft receiving ATC**.
- As far as practicable, aircraft with filed **flight plans** or aircraft otherwise known to ATC.
- Aircraft **known or believed to be affected by unlawful interference**.

The **FIC or ACC** collects information about a suspected emergency and forwards it to the appropriate **Rescue Coordination Centre (RCC)**. An approach or aerodrome controller who becomes aware of an emergency normally informs the ACC/FIC so that the RCC can be notified. Where speed is essential, the local controller can also activate emergency services in the vicinity immediately.

### 15.39 Information to Other Aircraft

When an ATS unit establishes that an aircraft is in an emergency close to other traffic, it should advise nearby aircraft of the relevant danger as soon as practicable. **Unlawful interference** requires special care: controllers should not mention it in their transmissions unless the affected aircraft has already introduced the matter and doing so will **not aggravate the situation**.

### 15.40 Three Emergency Phases

The general term **emergency phase** embraces three progressively more serious states:

**Uncertainty phase — INCERFA**

INCERFA is applicable when there is uncertainty about an aircraft's safety. The examples specified here are:

- No expected communication has been received **within 30 minutes** after it should have occurred.
- The aircraft has not arrived **within 30 minutes** of its estimated arrival time.

**Alert phase — ALERFA**

ALERFA follows an uncertainty phase if concern remains, or is established through another relevant event. Circumstances include:

- Continued unsuccessful attempts to contact the aircraft, with no additional information about its whereabouts or condition.
- Failure to land **within 5 minutes after a landing clearance has been issued**.
- Information that aircraft operating efficiency has deteriorated but **a forced landing is not yet considered likely**.
- Known **unlawful interference** with an aircraft.

**Distress phase — DETRESFA**

DETRESFA follows the alert phase when the available evidence indicates grave danger or the need for immediate assistance. It may be triggered by:

- Continued communication failure and broader enquiries pointing to probable distress.
- Evidence that **the aircraft’s fuel is exhausted**.
- Impaired performance such that a **forced landing is likely**.
- Information that the aircraft **is about to make, or has made, a forced landing**.

An escalation to the next phase is not required when reliable information indicates that the aircraft and occupants are not in the danger that would warrant that phase.

## 7. Additional ATS Procedures

### 15.41 Speed Control for Separation

ATC may assign speeds to establish or maintain spacing between aircraft. Speed instructions should be issued with enough advance notice to permit smooth compliance. Prolonged speed control is discouraged because it can affect fuel consumption. Controllers should ask only for changes needed to meet separation or spacing requirements.

In the system described here:

| Flight phase or level | Speed-control increment or restriction |
|---|---|
| At or above **FL250** | Adjustments in multiples of **Mach 0.01** |
| Below **FL250** | Adjustments in multiples of **10 kt IAS** |
| Intermediate and final approach | Only small adjustments of approximately **20 kt IAS** |
| After a point **4 NM from landing threshold** | **ATC speed control is not to be imposed** |

### 15.42 Automatic Dependent Surveillance (ADS)

**ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast)** periodically sends an aircraft's position and other operational details over a dedicated data link. Information may include its **position, altitude, type/category, airspeed, identity and movement trend**, such as a turn, climb or descent. The system described here relies on **GPS** or another navigation source offering at least equivalent performance and developed during the **1990s**.

Reception is generally **line of sight**, with a typical maximum range described as **less than 200 NM (approximately 370 km)**. The messages may be received by the appropriate ATS unit and by other suitably equipped aircraft within range.

The basic ADS report contains:

- **Latitude**.
- **Longitude**.
- **Altitude**.
- **Time**.
- A **figure of merit**, indicating positional accuracy.

### 15.43 Routine AIREPs

An **AIREP** is an air report conveying aircraft operational and/or meteorological information. A **routine AIREP** has three sections:

| Section | Information |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Position report** |
| **2** | **Operational details**, including estimated time of arrival (ETA) and endurance |
| **3** | **Meteorological observations** |

**Section 1 is mandatory.** Section 2 is supplied when the operator asks for it or the pilot judges it necessary. Section 3 is transmitted when routine en-route meteorological reports have been requested at designated points.

### 15.44 AIREP SPECIAL

All aircraft encountering the specified significant hazards are required to make **special air-reports**. Conditions listed include:

- **Severe turbulence**.
- **Severe icing**.
- **Severe mountain waves**.
- **Obscured, embedded, widespread or line-squall thunderstorms without hail**.
- **Heavy dust or sandstorms**.
- **Volcanic ash clouds**.

### 15.45 Air Traffic Incident Reports (ATIRs)

The **ATIR** system provides a formal means to record, examine and provide feedback on incidents arising during the provision of ATC. The report categories are:

- **Aircraft proximity incidents (AIRPROX)**.
- **ATC procedure incidents**.
- **ATC equipment incidents**.

An AIRPROX can originate from a **pilot — AIRPROX(P)** or **controller — AIRPROX(C)**. A pilot normally submits an initial AIRPROX by **radiotelephony (RTF)** and completes the detailed report on the ground.

### 15.46 Priority for Aircraft in Emergencies

An aircraft **known or reasonably believed to be in an emergency** receives priority over other aircraft. This also applies to a flight affected by **unlawful interference**.

### 15.47 Temporary Callsign Changes

Where two aircraft have sufficiently similar callsigns that they could be confused on one radio frequency, **ATC can direct one aircraft to use a different callsign temporarily**.
