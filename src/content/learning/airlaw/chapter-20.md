# Chapter 20 — Aerodromes - Visual Aids, Markings and Signs

## Requirements

### 20.1 Background

A pilot navigating the ground environment of an aerodrome must be able to identify routes, restrictions and usable runway surfaces without ambiguity. Because aerodromes differ in their geographical setting, traffic patterns and physical configuration, the visual information available during taxi, take-off preparation, approach and landing requires consistent presentation. **ICAO Annex 14** establishes the relevant Standards and Recommended Practices (**SARPs**). Knowledge of these aids therefore includes recognising how they appear in operational use, not merely knowing their definitions.

The material covers visual aids for navigation, pavement markings and aerodrome signs. Although contracting States implement the ICAO system, national differences remain possible. For this reason, an item encountered at an aerodrome outside the United Kingdom may not necessarily be found at a UK aerodrome, and the reverse can also apply.

## Visual Aids for Navigation

### 20.2 Indicators and Signalling Devices

Pilots operating without radio communication must still be able to establish the surface wind and the direction intended for landing. The requirement to take off and land into wind makes a visible wind indicator important. Aerodromes accepting non-radio operations also need a landing-direction indication and a facility in the visual control room through which appropriate visual signals can be transmitted to aircraft.

### 20.3 Wind Direction Indicators

At least **one wind direction indicator** is required at an aerodrome. This is normally a fabric windsock shaped as a truncated cone. Its dimensions must be at least **3.6 m in length**, with a diameter of at least **0.9 m at the wider mouth**. The windsock must show the direction of the surface wind unambiguously and provide an approximate indication of its strength.

Against its local background, the chosen colour scheme must allow a pilot at **300 m altitude or above** to see and interpret the indication. A single contrasting colour is preferred when practicable, particularly **white or orange**. At least one indicator, usually the principal or *master* windsock, has its position identified by a circular **white** surrounding band **15 m in diameter and 1.2 m wide**. An aerodrome operating at night must make provision for illumination of at least one wind indicator.

### 20.4 Landing Direction Indicator

Where a landing-direction indicator is installed, it occupies a prominent aerodrome location. A landing **“T”** is always included when a signal square is provided. The indicator itself is T-shaped and coloured **white or orange**, whichever is most distinguishable against its background. A T that must be used during darkness is illuminated or delineated with **white lights**.

### 20.5 Signalling Lamp

The control tower of a controlled aerodrome must have a lamp for transmitting the light signals used for aircraft on the ground and in flight, as specified in Chapter 6. A purpose-built **Aldis signalling lamp** is a usual means of meeting this requirement. It must permit manual aiming at a chosen aircraft or other target; selection of **red, green and white** light; a change from a signal of one colour to either of the others; and transmission of Morse messages in any of the three colours at **at least four words per minute**.

### 20.6 Signal Panels and Signal Area

Providing a **signal square**, or signals area, signifies that an aerodrome permits ordinary non-radio operations. The aerodrome authority need not provide one if routine non-radio traffic is prohibited. That prohibition does not remove the duty to assist an aircraft which has lost communications and has indicated that it intends to land.

The square must be observable from **300 m** height at all azimuths for viewing angles of more than **10° above the horizontal**. It requires an even, horizontal area measuring at least **9 m × 9 m**. Its surface colour must contrast with the panels displayed inside, and its perimeter must have a white border at least **0.3 m** wide. It is usually situated on the aerodrome-facing side of the tower or **visual control room (VCR)**. The individual ground signals displayed there are addressed in Chapter 6.

## Runway Markings

### 20.7 General

Painted pavement markings consist of numerals, letters, lines and shapes on runways, taxiways and aprons. Depending on their form and location, they establish where an aircraft is, indicate the route it should take, or communicate a compulsory restriction such as a stopping point. Lettering and symbols must be large and clear enough to be recognised from the flight deck. Under ICAO colour conventions, **runway markings are white** and **taxiway markings are yellow**.

### 20.8 Runway Markings

White paint is used for runway identification and guidance. On a pale-coloured runway, black outlines may be added so that the white markings remain visible. Since extensive painted surfaces can reduce pavement friction, appropriate low-slip paint should be selected wherever practical. Symbols may comprise alphanumeric characters, solid shapes or multiple longitudinal stripes that visually achieve the same effect as a filled area.

Runway markings identify the landing threshold and runway designation, show the centreline, and locate the intended aiming point. Instrument runways additionally use **touchdown zone (TDZ) markings** to describe the touchdown area.

### 20.9 Runway Designation Marking

The landing threshold of every paved runway must carry a runway designation. An unpaved or grass runway should also be designated at its threshold whenever practicable. A runway with a displaced threshold may display an additional designation sign for departing aircraft where the take-off starting position lies before that threshold.

### 20.10 Characteristics

A runway designation consists of a **two-digit number**, with an additional letter when parallel runways have to be distinguished. The number is based on the landing-direction **magnetic QDM divided by 10**, rounded to the nearest whole number and displayed as two digits. Thus a QDM of **094°** gives **09** because \(094/10 = 9.4\). Numbers below 10 are normally prefixed with a **0**. Some States omit the leading zero; the south-to-north runways at New York JFK, for example, have been marked **4L** and **4R**.

For a single runway and for a group of two or three parallel runways, the same magnetic-direction numbering method is used. At aerodromes with **four or more** parallel runways, one adjacent group is assigned the nearest tenth of the QDM, while the next adjacent group uses the next-nearest tenth to provide distinct designations.

Viewed from the approach direction, the letters are assigned in geographical left-to-right order:

- **Two parallels:** `09L`, `09R`.
- **Three parallels:** `09L`, `09C`, `09R`.
- **Four parallels:** `09L`, `09R`, `10L`, `10R`; the numeric part changes for the second pair even when the physical directions are substantially identical.

### 20.11 Runway Centre Line Marking

A **paved runway** must have a white centreline marking extending between its runway designation markings and coinciding with the geometric runway centreline.

### 20.12 Characteristics

The runway centreline is formed by regularly repeating **white stripes and intervening gaps**. One stripe together with its following gap must extend for **50–75 m**. Each stripe must be no shorter than **30 m** and must also be at least as long as the corresponding gap; the greater of those two requirements governs.

### 20.13 Threshold Marking

On grass, the threshold is at the beginning of the designated landing area; on a paved runway, it corresponds to the start of the available landing pavement. **Threshold markings** are required for **paved instrument runways**, and for **paved non-instrument code 3 and code 4 runways** intended for international commercial air transport. The threshold of an unpaved runway should be marked when practicable.

### 20.14 Location

The longitudinal threshold stripes—widely referred to as **piano keys**—normally commence **6 m beyond the threshold**.

### 20.15 Characteristics

Threshold markings are symmetrical groups of equal-sized longitudinal white stripes on either side of the centreline. On non-precision or non-instrument runways **45 m or more in width**, the runway designation may be embedded between the two stripe groups. In either arrangement, the outer limit of the stripes is no farther sideways than **3 m from the pavement edge** or **27 m from the centreline**, whichever provides the lesser distance from the centreline.

When the runway designation is integrated within the threshold marking, each side of the centreline must contain **at least three stripes**. Where the numeral is positioned before the stripe group, the threshold stripes extend across the runway. Each stripe must be at least **30 m** long, with approximately **1.80 m** between adjoining stripes. Where stripe groups extend across the whole runway, the pair nearest the centreline has twice the normal space; when the designation falls between the groups, the stated separation at the centre is **22.5 m**.

The specified number of threshold stripes depends on runway width:

| Runway width | Number of stripes |
|---|---:|
| 18 m | 4 |
| 23 m | 6 |
| 30 m | 8 |
| 45 m | 12 |
| 60 m or more | 16 |

### 20.16 Transverse Stripe

An additional **white transverse stripe** identifies a threshold where the landing threshold is displaced from the physical runway end, or where the physical end is not perpendicular to the centreline. If the displacement is permanent, the section of runway before the threshold is marked with **arrows**. Reasons for moving a landing threshold were considered in Chapter 19.

### 20.17 Aiming Point Marking

Two large, conspicuous white rectangles form the standard **aiming point marking**. The marking locates the reference point for the visual approach slope indicated by **PAPI** and the **ILS glide-path (GP)** transmission system. It is mandatory at both approach ends of paved instrument runways with **code numbers 2, 3 or 4**. Its use is recommended on **code 1 paved instrument runways** and on **code 3 or 4 paved non-instrument runways** when better identification of the aiming point is needed.

### 20.18 Location

The beginning of the aiming point marking is determined by the **landing distance available (LDA)** according to the distances below. Where **PAPI or VASI** is installed, its start is aligned instead with the origin of the visual approach slope.

| Landing distance available (LDA) | Threshold to start of aiming point marking |
|---|---:|
| Less than 800 m | 150 m |
| 800 m to less than 1200 m | 250 m |
| 1200 m to less than 2400 m | 300 m |
| 2400 m or more | 400 m |

For the relevant **code 4 runway below 2400 m**, the stated aiming-point distance is **300 m**; at **2400 m or more**, it becomes **400 m**. With a nominal **3° approach slope**, approximately **300 ft/NM**, the shorter positioning corresponds to about **50 ft** threshold-crossing height and the longer positioning to about **67 ft**, offering additional undercarriage clearance for larger aircraft.

### 20.19 Touchdown Zone Markings

**TDZ markings** define the area in which an aircraft is intended to touch down, rather than treating the threshold stripe as the expected landing contact point. Alongside the need to respect the available landing distance, they enable pilots to judge the amount of touchdown area ahead; when distance-coded, the groupings also communicate how much touchdown-zone distance remains.

TDZ markings are mandatory on **paved precision approach runways of codes 2, 3 and 4**. They are recommended on **code 3 or 4 non-precision or non-instrument paved runways** when increased conspicuity is desirable.

### 20.20 Location

Paired white rectangles are positioned symmetrically about the centreline. The quantity of pairs is selected with reference to the **LDA**. A **code 4 runway 2400 m or longer** has **six pairs** in its touchdown-zone pattern.

### 20.21 Characteristics

The pattern may be **Pattern A**, with basic rectangular pairs, or **Pattern B**, in which the bars convey distance information. The choice between them does **not** depend on runway length. Successive pairs begin at intervals of **150 m**, measured from the threshold. If a scheduled pair would coincide with the aiming point rectangles or fall within **50 m** of them, that pair is omitted so that the symbols remain distinct.

**Apron safety lines** assist safe aircraft movement into and out of stands, although they do not constitute taxiway guidance markings themselves. Ground support equipment and vehicles kept behind those boundaries need not be treated as immediate stand-entry obstacles by the pilot or marshaller. ### 20.25 Taxiway Centre Line Markings

A paved taxiway, de-icing or anti-icing installation, and associated apron serving **code 3 or 4 aircraft** must carry taxiway centreline markings; provision for **codes 1 and 2** is recommended. Such a line guides aircraft continuously from the runway centreline toward the position on the apron where stand markings take over.

A taxiway centreline may also be marked along a paved runway when that runway is part of a standard taxi route and either has **no runway centreline marking**, or the required taxiway route is **not coincident with the runway centreline**. The taxiway line is **continuous yellow**. Where yellow edge/side stripes are provided, they can designate **non-load-bearing** adjacent pavement rather than a usable aircraft route.

### 20.26 Runway Holding Position Marking

Every runway entrance has a designated **holding position**, and more than one may be established along a single entrance taxiway. The holding position itself is marked across the **full taxiway width**, with a mandatory holding-point sign placed **at least on the left** as aircraft approach; signs on both sides are preferable where practicable. The boundary may also be emphasised by **stop bars** or **runway guard lights** (paragraph 21.38).

The distance from the holding position to the associated runway centreline follows the dimensions introduced after paragraph **19.53**. For precision approach runways, it must also protect radio aids—especially the **ILS**—from interference caused by waiting aircraft or vehicles. A separate holding point may be required when the approach path to one runway passes above the taxiway leading to another; the associated sign then identifies the relevant approach/runway.

### 20.27 Patterns

Two prescribed forms of runway holding-position surface marking are distinguished: **Pattern A** and **Pattern B**.

### 20.28 Pattern A

The holding position **nearest the runway** always uses **Pattern A**. This includes a taxiway intersection with a **visual/non-instrument runway**, a **non-precision approach runway**, or a **take-off runway**. If a taxiway serving a **Category I, II or III precision approach runway** has just one holding position, Pattern A is also used. For a **code 4 runway**, the Pattern A position is at least **75 m from the runway centreline**; it also serves as the **visual holding point**.

Where taxiways intersect, a holding position can be placed at a safe distance from the crossing route. One taxiway may have movement priority; in that case the holding indication may receive a complementary mandatory pavement marking. If a **stop bar** or **clearance bar** is fitted, the intermediate holding line should coincide with it. ### 20.31 Aircraft Stand Markings

At established parking positions on **paved aprons** and at **de-icing/anti-icing facilities**, stand markings should provide the guidance appropriate to the parking geometry. Depending on the configuration and other installed aids, these may include a **stand identification**, **lead-in line**, **turn bar**, **turning line**, **alignment bar**, **stop line** and **lead-out line**.

Stand identification, consisting of letters and/or numbers, should appear shortly after the lead-in line begins, sized so that it can be read from the aircraft cockpit. The principal lead-in, turning and exit lines are normally **continuous**. If several aircraft types share one stand and their markings overlap, the guidance for the most demanding aircraft is continuous, while guidance for the remaining aircraft is broken. Curves must suit the turning radii of the most demanding intended aircraft.

Where movement is authorised only in one direction, directional arrows form part of the lead-in and lead-out lines. The **turn bar** is perpendicular to the lead-in line, abreast the **left-seat pilot** at the point where the intended turn begins. Its offset from the lead-in line depends on aircraft type and the pilot's sight lines. The **alignment bar** follows the extended aircraft centreline at the final parking position and remains visible to the pilot as the manoeuvre concludes. A **stop line**, perpendicular to that alignment bar, is located abreast the left pilot's position where the aircraft should stop.

### 20.32 Road Holding Position Markings

A service road that leads onto a runway must have a **road holding-position marking** across the roadway at its designated stopping point. The marking follows the local **road traffic regulations**.

### 20.33 Mandatory Information Marking

If a mandatory instruction sign cannot practicably be installed, the equivalent instruction must be **painted on the taxiway pavement**. The required surface instructions include **runway holding-point designations**, shown as **white designation text against red**, and **NO ENTRY** messages. Aircraft may cross these markings only with specific ATC authorisation.

### 20.34 Information Markings

When a location, direction or destination sign would be appropriate but cannot physically be installed, pavement information markings supply that information. They can also supplement a sign where operational needs justify additional guidance. Position and orientation must make the wording legible to pilots approaching along the taxiway or apron.

A marking replacing or reinforcing a **location sign** uses **yellow characters**; one replacing or supporting a **direction/destination sign** uses **black characters**. Where natural pavement contrast is inadequate, provide a **black panel behind yellow text**, or a **yellow panel behind black text**, respectively. Numbers, letters and other symbols may be combined, but **numerals without letters** on the manoeuvring area are reserved for **runway designations**.

## Signs

### 20.35 General Specification

Aerodrome signs communicate **mandatory instructions**, identify a particular position on the movement area, show the route or destination available from that position, or provide other information required for operations.

### 20.36 Characteristics

A sign installed on an aircraft movement area is **frangible** so that it will break away if struck. Signs next to runways and taxiways must be low enough to avoid interference with **propellers or jet-engine nacelles**. The sign face is **rectangular and wider than it is high**. Among signs on the movement area, a **red background is restricted to mandatory instructions**, while signs containing numbers without accompanying letters identify runways.

For night use, a sign must be **reflective and/or illuminated** as specified. Illumination is expressly required for operations under any of the following conditions:

- **Runway visual range (RVR) below 800 m**.
- **At night**, when used with an **instrument runway**.
- **At night**, when used with a **code 3 or code 4 non-instrument runway**.

### 20.37 Mandatory Instruction Signs

A mandatory sign identifies the position **past which an aircraft or vehicle must not move without clearance from aerodrome control**. This group comprises runway designation signs, **Category I, II and III** holding-point signs, taxi-holding-position signs, road-holding-position signs and **NO ENTRY** signs.

At an intersection of a taxiway and a runway, **Pattern A** holding-position marking is supported by a **runway designation sign**. A **Pattern B** marking is accompanied by an appropriate **Category I, II or III** holding-position sign. When a runway designation sign is installed at a taxiway/runway junction, an associated **location sign**, where appropriate, goes on the **outboard side**, farther from the taxiway. An entry prohibited to aircraft is identified by a **NO ENTRY** sign.

### 20.38 Locations of Signs

Mandatory signs must be conspicuous to approaching pilots and vehicle drivers without themselves becoming a hazard. Their positioning follows these arrangements:

- **Runway designation sign at a taxiway/runway junction:** at least on the **left side** facing aircraft approaching the runway; on **both sides** wherever practicable.
- **NO ENTRY sign:** at the start of the prohibited route, at least on the **left as seen by an approaching pilot**; duplicate on the other side when practicable.
- **Category I, II or III holding-position sign:** on **both sides** of the relevant line, facing traffic approaching the protected critical area.
- **Taxi-holding-position sign:** on the **left side** facing the approach to the runway or to the **ILS/MLS critical or sensitive area**, as applicable; provide one on each side when practicable.

### 20.39 Characteristics

Mandatory instruction signs display **white characters on a red field**. A runway sign shows the runway designator. Instrument holding-point signs append the appropriate suffix **CAT I**, **CAT II**, **CAT III** or combined **CAT II/III** to the runway designation. A taxi-holding-position sign bears the taxiway designation and the relevant holding-position number.

### 20.40 Information Signs

An information sign identifies a **place on the aerodrome** or points to a **route, exit or destination**. This class includes **direction signs**, **location signs**, **destination signs**, **runway exit signs** and **runway vacated signs**.

Where possible, information signs appear on the **left of the taxiway**. Before a taxiway junction, they are aligned with the **taxiway intersection marking**. A runway-exit sign stands on the side of the runway containing that exit. A **runway-vacated sign** is placed on at least one side of the exit taxiway at the position where aircraft have cleared the relevant protected/sensitive area.

If a runway-vacated sign is combined with a taxiway location sign, the **taxiway location sign is outboard**. The same outboard positioning applies to a location sign combined with a runway-designation sign. A sign giving information **other than location** must **not** be placed immediately together with a mandatory instruction sign.

### 20.41 Characteristics

The appearance of information signs and their inscriptions is standardised:

- Except for **location signs**, information signs show **black text or symbols on yellow**.
- **Location signs** use **yellow letters or numbers on black**; when the location sign is on its own, a **yellow border** surrounds it.
- A **runway-exit sign** displays the name of the exit taxiway and an arrow showing the way to leave the runway.
- A **runway-vacated sign** reproduces the **Pattern A taxiway holding-position marking**.

### 20.43 Taxiway Designators

Taxiways use one or more **letters**, optionally followed by a **number**. The letters **I**, **O** and **X** should be avoided where possible because they can be confused with **1**, **0** and the **closed-runway marking**, respectively. The terms **inner** and **outer** are also discouraged in taxiway names. A designation composed **only of numerals** is reserved for a runway on the manoeuvring area.

### 20.44 Aircraft Stand Identification Signs

Where feasible, an aircraft parking-stand identification marking should be supported by a sign positioned so that the crew can read it **before entering the stand**. Normally the face displays **black characters on yellow**. An alternative used in **Europe** is **white lettering on a blue background**.

### 20.45 Road-Holding Position Signs

Every road entry to a runway must have a **road holding-position sign**. It is installed at the holding position, **1.5 m from the roadway edge**, on the left or right as dictated by local traffic rules. The sign has **white lettering on red** and is reflective or illuminated where used at night.

Its wording follows the **national language** and local road-traffic requirements. It must clearly instruct road users to **stop**, to **obtain ATC clearance**, and must state the applicable **location designator**.

### 20.46 Illumination of Signs

Aerodrome signs requiring use in the circumstances below must be illuminated:

**a.** when **RVR is less than 800 m**;

**b.** during darkness in association with an **instrument runway**; and

**c.** during darkness at a **non-instrument runway of code number 3 or 4**.

## Markers

### 20.47 Definition

Unlike a pavement marking, a **marker** is a visible object raised above the surface. It may identify an obstacle or define the edge of a protected or operational area when painted markings or lights are absent or inadequate. Examples include identifying taxiway edges after snowfall and marking unsuitable patches of ground on a grass aerodrome. Its height must preserve clearance for aircraft propellers and the engine pods of jet aircraft. **Reflective green markers**, for example, are used to identify the refuelling area at Oxford.

### 20.48 Unpaved Runway Edge Markers

If an **unpaved runway** cannot readily be distinguished from adjacent ground, markers should delineate its limits. When runway lights are present, integrate the markers into the light fittings. Without lights, use **flat rectangular** or **conical** markers placed so that the runway boundary is clear. A rectangular marker must measure at least **1 m × 3 m**, with its long dimension **parallel to the runway centreline**. A conical marker must not exceed **50 cm in height**.

### 20.49 Taxiway Edge Markers

Where a taxiway has neither appropriate **centreline or edge lighting** nor **taxiway centreline markers**, provide taxiway edge markers. They should occupy at least the positions that taxiway edge lights would occupy if lights had been fitted. These markers are **reflective blue** and must be **frangible**.

### 20.50 Taxiway Centre Line Markers

Where **edge lights or taxiway edge markers are absent**, a taxiway should instead have centreline markers. Their distribution should correspond at least to the locations that taxiway centreline lights would use. Normally they sit directly on the painted taxiway centreline, but may be displaced **no more than 30 cm** sideways where installation on the line is impracticable. Taxiway centreline markers are **reflective green**. Their construction must allow aircraft wheels to pass over them without harming either the aircraft or the marker.

### 20.51 Unpaved Taxiway Edge Markers

The edges of a grass or other unpaved taxiway should be identified with markers when its surface is insufficiently distinct from adjoining terrain. Fit markers into existing taxiway light fixtures if lighting is installed; otherwise use **conical edge markers** to make the taxiway width and route apparent.

### 20.52 Stopway Edge Markers

Markers identifying a **stopway** must have an appearance sufficiently unlike a **runway marker** to prevent misidentification. Small upright boards whose reverse face is camouflaged for a viewer looking from the runway have been shown to be operationally satisfactory.

### 20.53 Boundary Markers

An aerodrome with a **landing area but no runway** must mark the outer landing-area boundary. For boundary-marker shapes of the type illustrated, the maximum spacing is **200 m**. When **conical markers** are used, the spacing is approximately **90 m**, and there is a marker at **each corner**.

The markers may have the depicted form or be cones not less than **50 cm high** and **75 cm across at the base**. They must stand out from the background: suitable schemes include **orange**, **red**, **orange with white**, or **red with white**, unless those colours would blend into the surroundings.

### 20.54 Fixed Distance Markers

A runway may have **fixed distance-to-go boards** positioned beside it to tell pilots how much runway remains. The panels contain **white numbers on black** and are spaced in steps of **1000 ft**, reducing toward the far runway end. The first board is located **1000 ft (300 m) from the far threshold**. This system is particularly associated with **military aerodromes**.

### 20.55 Parking Stand Markers

Identification of a parking position may be presented as a surface marking at the stand entrance or by a sign fixed to a building at the far end of the stand. In this marker arrangement, the identification appears as **white text on blue**.

## Visual Docking Guidance Systems

### 20.56 Overview

**Visual Docking Guidance Systems (VDGS)**—also termed **Nose-in Docking Guidance Systems** or **Stand Entry Guidance Systems**—provide pilots with alignment and stopping cues needed to position an aircraft accurately on its stand. Their use is particularly relevant to stands with **passenger airbridges**, where precise final positioning is necessary.

ICAO calls for **aircraft stand manoeuvring guidance lights** on paved aprons or on de-icing/anti-icing facilities intended for poor-visibility use where another means of guidance does not provide sufficiently accurate positioning. VDGS are not assigned specific learning objectives in this chapter; **Chapter 4 of CAP 637** provides further reading should the subject arise in the examination.
