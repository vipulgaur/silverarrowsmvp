# Chapter 14: Airspace

## 1. Introduction

### 14.1 Division of Airspace

A State's airspace is organized into one or more **Flight Information Regions (FIRs)**. An FIR forms the basic territorial unit for the provision of **Flight Information Service (FIS)** and **Alerting Service**. Its name normally identifies the geographical region it covers, such as the London FIR. Each FIR is associated with an **Area Control Centre (ACC)** containing the relevant flight information function.

The boundaries of an FIR need not follow the State's national boundary exactly. Where strict national control is applied, FIR limits may coincide with sovereign borders; elsewhere, particularly where complex borders make air traffic administration difficult, operationally convenient boundaries may be drawn along geographical coordinates or agreed dividing lines.

Several other forms of airspace may be established inside an FIR:

- **Control Areas (CTAs)** for controlled flights, including the corridor-like CTAs used as airways.
- **Control Zones (CTRs)** in the vicinity of aerodromes.
- **Aerodrome Traffic Zones (ATZs)** protecting traffic operating close to an aerodrome.
- **Prohibited, restricted and danger areas**, where activities or flight restrictions require special attention.

These designations may overlap geographically at different levels. A single geographical location can therefore lie within an FIR and also beneath, inside or adjacent to a particular control area or zone.

### 14.2 Upper Information Regions (UIRs)

A State may divide an FIR vertically. The upper part is then designated an **Upper Information Region (UIR)**. This arrangement permits upper-airspace traffic to operate under procedures and separation arrangements that differ from those used at lower levels.

The underlying operational distinction is that aircraft in a UIR are predominantly crossing the region during the en-route phase, whereas lower-level traffic is more likely to be climbing, descending, approaching or departing.

In the framework described here, the vertical division is:

| Area | FIR/UIR division |
|---|---|
| Europe | **FL195** |
| United States | **FL180** |

The lower boundary of a UIR is specified as a **VFR flight level**.

### 14.3 Open FIR

The term **open FIR** refers to FIR airspace that is not assigned to a CTA, CTR or another specially restricted classification. In this part of the FIR, the air traffic services available are **Flight Information Service** and **Alerting Service**. An ATC separation service is not automatically supplied merely because the aircraft is inside an FIR.

### 14.4 Oceanic Control Areas (OCAs)

Over extensive oceanic regions, conventional traffic control is more difficult because surveillance and navigation coverage may be limited and long-range communication has historically relied on **HF radio** and radio operators. Oceanic airspace therefore makes use of particular navigation and procedural arrangements.

In the system covered here, airspace **above FL55** over the major oceanic regions may be designated an **Oceanic Control Area (OCA)**. Flights operating there must meet the applicable oceanic procedures and operating rules. The North Atlantic is an example of an area where such arrangements are necessary.

### 14.5 Prohibited, Restricted and Danger Areas

Every prohibited, restricted or danger area within a State forms part of that State's FIR structure. Changes in an area's operating status or periods of activity are promulgated through **NOTAMs**.

Each area receives an identifier with three elements:

1. The **ICAO State/country identifier**.
2. One letter indicating its type: **P** (Prohibited), **R** (Restricted) or **D** (Danger).
3. A unique numerical designation from **001 to 999**.

For example, **EG D001** identifies a danger area associated with the United Kingdom: `EG` identifies the UK, `D` specifies a danger area, and `001` is the area's assigned number. The example concerns an area off the north coast of Cornwall.

## 2. Control Areas and Control Zones

### 14.6 Controlled Airspace: CTAs and CTRs

An **air traffic control service** is provided inside **controlled airspace (CAS)**. In the classification used here, controlled airspace consists of **Control Areas (CTAs)** and **Control Zones (CTRs)**. Its generally accepted upper limit is **FL660**. The primary purpose of CTAs and CTRs is to make controlled IFR operations possible under an ATC service.

### 14.7 Control Areas (CTAs)

A **CTA** is controlled airspace principally used by aircraft in the **en-route** phase, as distinct from the immediate departure and approach phases. The ATC service provided is called **Area Control**.

CTAs tend to be established around major aerodromes and at points where several airways converge. An **airway** is itself a corridor-shaped CTA joining controlled airspace regions.

Unlike a control zone, a CTA normally begins **above** the surface. In this chapter's specified minima, its base must be at least **200 m (700 ft) above ground level (AGL)**. The upper boundary may be defined by an altitude or a flight level; when a flight level is used, the relevant boundary is a **VFR flight level**.

A CTA may be split internally to serve different traffic patterns. At higher levels, its traffic is mainly transiting the region. Lower portions deal with aircraft entering or leaving the terminal operating area, including flights routing to **initial approach fixes (IAFs)**. Their design also helps separate departures from one aerodrome from arrivals at another.

### 14.8 Control Zones (CTRs)

A **CTR** is controlled airspace associated with an aerodrome or a group of aerodromes. It supports the arrival and departure phases of controlled flight, with the ATC service provided by **Approach Control**.

A CTR begins at **the surface** (ground level or mean sea level datum, as defined). If its upper limit is stated as a flight level, the chapter specifies a **VFR flight level**. Its lateral dimensions must cover the airspace needed to conduct approaches and departures. In particular, it normally extends **at least 5 NM** from the aerodrome reference point in the directions from which aircraft make approaches.

Where a CTR lies inside the lateral limits of a CTA, the CTR extends vertically to **meet the CTA's lower boundary**, preventing a gap between the controlled terminal and en-route structures.

### 14.9 Upper Control Areas

A CTA may extend above **FL195**. In that case, the rules applied in the higher portion of that CTA need not automatically be different from those applicable in lower controlled airspace; the lower-airspace rules may continue to apply.

### 14.10 Air Traffic Control Units

Responsibility for ATC within controlled airspace is assigned to designated **Air Traffic Control Units (ATCUs)**. The principal division is:

| Unit | Airspace/service normally associated with it |
|---|---|
| **Area Control Centre (ACC)** | Area Control within CTAs and along controlled en-route routes |
| **Approach Control Unit** | Approach/departure control in terminal airspace and CTRs |
| **Aerodrome Control Tower** | Aerodrome control functions associated with aerodrome traffic and CTR operations |

## 3. Classes of Airspace

### 14.11 The A–G Classification System

ICAO introduced a harmonized method of classifying airspace in **1980**. Seven classes, **A through G**, distinguish the rules under which aircraft may fly, the services supplied, which traffic is separated, the need for clearances and communications, and the permitted type of flight.

### 14.12 Controlled Airspace: Classes A–E

**Classes A, B, C, D and E** are classified as controlled airspace. IFR flights operating in these classes are controlled flights.

- **Class A:** IFR flights only; ATC separates all flights.
- **Class B:** IFR and VFR are permitted; all flights are controlled and separated from one another.
- **Class C:** IFR and VFR are permitted. IFR is separated from both IFR and VFR; VFR is separated from IFR and receives information about other VFR flights.
- **Class D:** IFR and VFR are permitted. IFR is separated from IFR; information is passed concerning conflicting IFR/VFR and VFR/VFR traffic rather than providing separation for every pairing.
- **Class E:** IFR receives ATC and separation from IFR; VFR is permitted without an ATC clearance and receives FIS as applicable. Under ICAO's classification, **Class E is not used for CTRs**.

A CTR can be established in the controlled classes appropriate for providing control-zone operations. The designated category determines the practical separation, communications and entry requirements.

### 14.13 Advisory Airspace: Class F

**Class F** is advisory airspace. IFR traffic intending to make use of the advisory service is **participating traffic**. The advisory service supplies information and advice aimed at providing separation between participating IFR flights as far as practicable, but **does not issue an ATC clearance**.

All IFR flights operating in Class F must file a **flight plan**. Class F is regarded as a temporary arrangement that may be used while the suitability of a full controlled-airspace service is assessed. Flight Information Service is available to relevant traffic.

### 14.14 Special VFR (SVFR)

A control zone of an eligible airspace class may accommodate **Special VFR (SVFR)** operations. SVFR allows a flight to operate in a CTR under the relevant special clearance and conditions rather than under the ordinary VFR minima.

### 14.15 VFR Operations Without Radio Communication

Within **Classes E, F and G**, VFR aircraft may operate without continuous **two-way VHF radiotelephony communication**, subject to applicable local provisions. Where the availability of alerting is particularly important—for example, across remote terrain or extensive sea areas—designated routes and operating levels may be recommended so aircraft can maintain contact with an ATS unit.

### 14.16 Airspeed Restrictions

The chapter specifies **250 kt indicated airspeed (IAS)** below **10,000 ft / FL100** for:

- **VFR flights in Class C**.
- **All flights in Classes D, E, F and G**.

It does **not** apply a general 250 kt restriction to Class B traffic or to IFR flights in Class C under this classification.

### 14.17 Flight Information Service and Traffic Information

Flight Information Service is available in **Classes C through G** for flights requesting it or otherwise known to the ATS unit. Where relevant, information about nearby traffic and possible collision hazards is passed to the pilot, who remains responsible for taking the necessary avoiding action under the Rules of the Air.

Such notices are termed **traffic information**. In Classes **A and B**, FIS is also available, but the provision of ATC has priority. Traffic considered to warrant equal attention because of a potential conflict is referred to as **essential traffic**, with the corresponding report called **essential traffic information**.

## 4. Required Navigation Performance (RNP)

### 14.19 Meaning of RNP

**Required Navigation Performance (RNP)** expresses the navigation accuracy demanded of aircraft flying in a particular part of ATS airspace. It is stated numerically and established by a State on the basis of **Regional Air Navigation Agreements (RANs)**.

The RNP figure represents a containment requirement applying for **at least 95%** of the relevant operating time or population. In practical terms, an aircraft is expected to remain within the prescribed lateral tolerance for not less than 95% of the time spent in that airspace. The State concerned specifies which RNP value applies.

### 14.20 RNP Values and Navigation Accuracy

An **RNP4** requirement indicates a lateral accuracy of **4 NM** relative to the intended position or track for the stipulated **95%** containment standard.

The RNP values identified in the chapter are **RNP1, RNP4, RNP10, RNP12.6 and RNP20**. The following examples are used to distinguish their application:

| Reference navigation technique or context | Value discussed |
|---|---|
| Radar-supported operations | RNP1 |
| Satellite-navigation capability (theoretical value cited) | RNP0.3 |
| VOR/DME-based RNAV airways | RNP5 |
| Earlier long-range multiple-IRS transatlantic navigation | RNP12.6 |
| North Atlantic MNPS airspace example | RNP20 |

**RNP5** appears in the discussion of VOR/DME operations even though it is not one of the listed RNP classes. The continued use of VOR/DME navigation is therefore acknowledged in this framework despite earlier expectations that it would have been displaced by more accurate systems.

### 14.21 Application to North Atlantic Track Spacing

North Atlantic track design provides a numerical example of how containment and an additional safety margin influence lateral route spacing.

In **RNP20** airspace, the protected portion extends **20 NM either side of the specified track**. An additional allowance of **20 NM** is then used between adjacent protected portions. The resulting nominal spacing between two route centre lines is:

$$
20~\mathrm{NM}+20~\mathrm{NM}+20~\mathrm{NM}=60~\mathrm{NM}
$$

## 5. Airways and ATS Routes

### 14.22 Establishment and Function of Airways

**Airways** are defined corridors connecting controlled airspace areas. Their original purpose was to organize en-route traffic along routes where aircraft could be separated by direction, flight level and agreed procedures. The key issue is maintaining separation between aircraft travelling in opposite directions and assigning suitable levels to traffic following broadly the same direction.

The top of an airway may be linked to the lower limit of a UIR. Its base is influenced by the need to clear terrain and obstacles, existing airspace restrictions, and the interests of other users, including military aviation.

The development of route networks initially helped connect widely spaced controlled areas. Increased traffic density has also exposed their disadvantages: fixed corridors can concentrate demand at choke points, reduce routing flexibility, cause delays, increase travel distance and raise fuel consumption. The chapter also describes the growing use of routes not confined to conventional airways, including traffic using military radar assistance and the European **open-skies** and **gate-to-gate** operating concepts associated with EUROCONTROL. It envisages a continuing decline in conventional airways and the eventual treatment of much of the airspace **above approximately 6,000 ft** as controlled airspace.

### 14.23 Regional and Non-regional Routes; RNAV and Conventional Navigation

Traditional airways were often based on **VOR-to-VOR navigation**, sometimes described as *beacon hopping*. Developments in **area navigation (RNAV)** allowed route segments to be defined by waypoints, including those established from **VOR/DME** information, rather than requiring continuous flight directly from one beacon to the next.

A route is **regional** when it operates between States within an ICAO region. A **non-regional** route does not extend beyond one State. Combining this distinction with the navigation method produces four categories:

- Regional, non-RNAV route.
- Regional, RNAV route.
- Non-regional, non-RNAV route.
- Non-regional, RNAV route.

### 14.24 Other Air Traffic Services Routes

Not all ATS routes are conventional airways. Published **Standard Instrument Departures (SIDs)**, **Standard Terminal Arrival Routes (STARs)** and low-level **helicopter routes** also use route designators. A standardized designator allows a route to be identified concisely in flight plans and in exchanges with air traffic control.

### 14.25 ATS Route Designators

An ATS route designation contains an identifying letter sequence and a **unique number from 1 to 999**. The letters identify the type of route and may add operational details about its use.

**Optional prefixes** are:

| Prefix | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **U** | Upper air route |
| **S** | Supersonic transport route |
| **K** | Low-level helicopter route |

**Optional suffixes** are:

| Suffix | Application |
|---|---|
| **F** | Advisory route in Class F airspace |
| **G** | FIS route in Class G airspace |
| **Y** | **RNP1** route **at and above FL200**; turns of **30°–90°** must remain within the specified RNP tolerance of a **22.5 NM-radius tangential arc** joining the straight route legs |
| **Z** | **RNP1** route **at and below FL190**; turns of **30°–90°** must remain within the specified RNP tolerance of a **15 NM-radius tangential arc** joining the straight route legs |
