## Introduction

### 19.1 Aerodromes

An **aerodrome** is a location on the Earth's surface provided for aircraft arrivals and departures. Its form can range from a field supporting occasional private flying or a designated area of water to an extensive airport complex such as Heathrow, Gatwick, Chicago O'Hare or JFK. Regardless of size or construction, its distinguishing feature is its provision for aircraft operations. The physical standards considered here concern land aerodromes, particularly those serving international commercial aviation.

Several everyday labels are used imprecisely. An *airfield* is, strictly, a field—often lacking substantial preparation—used on occasion for aircraft landings. An *airport* functions as an international entry or exit point for airborne passengers and cargo, comparable to a seaport for maritime movements. For the physical aviation facility itself, **aerodrome** is the appropriate formal designation.

### 19.2 Annex 14

**Annex 14** to the Convention on International Civil Aviation establishes the Standards and Recommended Practices (**SARPs**) concerning aerodromes. Article 38 permits contracting States to notify differences from those provisions. Article 15 establishes the context of public-use aerodromes: States must ensure that aerodromes open for public use satisfy the applicable Annex 14 requirements. In the United Kingdom, this oversight is exercised by the **Civil Aviation Authority (CAA)**.

Four UK aerodrome categories are distinguished:

- **Public-use licensed:** available for general use on equal terms.
- **Ordinary/private licensed:** operated by the licence holder and those whom the holder permits to use it.
- **Unlicensed:** limited-use aerodrome.
- **Government-owned:** for example, military or DERA facilities.

### 19.3 Use by Commercial Air Transport

For **commercial air transport (CAT)** operations, the distinction between an ordinary licensed aerodrome and one licensed for public use is not the deciding issue. The aerodrome must provide aircraft-handling capability, markings, lighting, facilities and services meeting the State's conditions for licensing the intended CAT operation.

CAT use of an unlicensed aerodrome is not expressly excluded; however, associated instrument approach procedures at an unlicensed site have not been certified as safe for CAT purposes. Consequently, within this framework, CAT operations at such a site are limited to visual operations.

### 19.4 International Airports

A State may apply immigration, health, customs and excise controls to incoming people and goods. An aerodrome with operational arrangements and facilities for those formalities is an **international aerodrome**. Names such as *Los Angeles International* identify that function. UK terminology has traditionally used **customs aerodrome**, although names such as *Birmingham International* illustrate the increasing use of the international designation.

The Montreal Convention concerns the relevant acts of unlawful interference committed at an **international aerodrome**.

### 19.5 Basic Layout

Annex 14-compliant aerodromes have a **movement area**, **manoeuvring area** and **apron**. The licensing framework normally controls the physical aerodrome, while some off-site safeguards—particularly the protection of approaches—may fall to local planning authorities. A proposed thirty-storey apartment block half a mile beyond Heathrow runway **27R**, for example, would be restricted through planning controls even though it is outside the immediate aerodrome property.

A **controlled aerodrome** requires an ATC tower with a **visual control room (VCR)**. Where aircraft without radio equipment are accepted, a ground **signals area/signal square** must be positioned so that pilots can see it from the specified distance and height. If there is no signal square, non-radio aircraft are not accepted. The ground signals themselves form part of the Rules of the Air. Aircraft types, required operational availability and the facilities that must be supported determine how the aerodrome is built.

### 19.6 Movement Area

The **movement area** encompasses the aerodrome surfaces provided for aircraft ground movement: the manoeuvring area, aprons and areas allocated to aircraft maintenance. Designated grass surfaces can therefore form part of the movement area, whereas buildings and other constructed installations do not. It covers the operational aircraft-movement land rather than merely the runway system.

### 19.7 Manoeuvring Area

The **manoeuvring area** comprises the surfaces used for aircraft take-off, landing and ground manoeuvring, but excludes aprons and aircraft-maintenance areas. Its components include paved or grass runways and taxiways, designated stopways and any defined pre-take-off surface ahead of a runway threshold.

### 19.8 Apron

The **apron** is the aerodrome area used for aircraft passenger, mail and freight handling; **ramp** is the common US expression. It is also the location at which a pilot should check altimeter accuracy before departure. At major commercial aerodromes, an **Apron Management Service** may take responsibility for safe apron movements following delegation by ATC. Such services are frequently supplied by a specialist organisation under contract to the aerodrome operator, providing or coordinating baggage handling, refuelling, marshalling and associated transport.

### 19.9 Aeronautical Part

The **aeronautical part**, commonly called **airside**, covers the security-controlled portion of an aerodrome, including its buildings and facilities. Access is restricted by aviation-security controls.

### 19.10 Failure or Irregularity of Systems and Equipment

An air traffic control unit (**ATCU**) must, without delay and in accordance with its local procedures, report any defect or abnormal operation of **communication, navigation or surveillance equipment** that could impair flight safety, flight-operation efficiency or provision of ATC services.

## Aerodrome Reference Code

### 19.11 Use

The **aerodrome reference code** connects aircraft-related design characteristics to aerodrome planning and construction specifications. Designers can use it to match facilities to the aircraft expected to operate there. It is **not** a method of prescribing runway length, setting the permissible minimum or maximum runway length, or assessing pavement strength. The code elements are applied to individual facility requirements when those characteristics are relevant.

### 19.12 Elements

The reference code combines **a number and a letter**, each derived from aircraft characteristics. The **code number (Element 1)** depends on aeroplane reference field length. The **code letter (Element 2)** reflects aircraft wingspan and outer main-landing-gear wheel span. An individual facility requirement may depend on one element or the combination, selecting the dimension representing the critical design aircraft. The intended fleet is therefore established first, followed by the appropriate number and letter.

### 19.13 Aeroplane Reference Field Length

**Aeroplane reference field length** represents the minimum field distance required for take-off by the aircraft at its maximum certificated take-off mass, using sea-level **standard-atmosphere** conditions, **still air** and the specified standard runway-slope condition. The figure is obtained from the approved **Aeroplane Flight Manual (AFM)** or equivalent manufacturer information and is used to select the aerodrome code number.

## Aerodrome Data

### 19.15 Aerodrome Reference Point (ARP)

Every aerodrome is assigned an **ARP**. Its latitude and longitude identify the published geographical reference position and must be supplied to the **aeronautical information services (AIS)** authority in degrees, minutes and seconds. The point is selected near the existing or proposed geometrical centre and normally remains unchanged after designation. In practice it is frequently at about the centre of the longest runway.

### 19.16 Pre-flight Altimeter Check Location

At least one position suitable for checking an aircraft altimeter before flight must be made available. Locating it on the **apron** allows the check to be completed before taxi clearance, avoiding a subsequent stop. A whole apron may be designated as the check area. Its published elevation is the **mean elevation** across the position, rounded to the nearest metre or foot, and the elevation at any point within it must not differ by more than **3 m (10 ft)** from that mean.

### 19.17 Aerodrome and Runway Elevations

The aerodrome elevation and the **geoid undulation** at the aerodrome's highest landing-area point are measured with a precision of **0.5 m or 0.5 ft**. For a precision runway the stated survey precision is **0.25 m or 0.25 ft**. The **geoid** is the gravitational surface corresponding to mean sea level extended beneath land masses; local variations in gravity make that surface irregular and account for geoid undulation.

### 19.18 Pavement Strengths

For paved runways, taxiways and aprons intended to accommodate aircraft with a maximum take-off mass **above 5700 kg**, pavement-bearing capability is published using the **Aircraft Classification Number–Pavement Classification Number (ACN–PCN)** method. Under this system, an aircraft is acceptable on the pavement when its **ACN does not exceed the stated PCN**.

### 19.19 PCN

The **Pavement Classification Number** expresses the published strength of a **paved** runway, taxiway or apron. It is especially important at aircraft parking positions because the aircraft's mass is ordinarily greatest there. The system does not apply to unpaved ground.

### 19.20 ACN

The **Aircraft Classification Number** indicates an aircraft's relative loading effect upon a paved surface, taking account of pavement construction and thickness. Its numerical scale begins at **0** and has **no upper bound**; an ACN is assigned to each aircraft.

### 19.21 Aircraft of 5700 kg or Less

For an aircraft with a maximum mass **not exceeding 5700 kg**, pavement suitability is expressed through the **maximum authorised aircraft mass** or **maximum permissible tyre pressure** rather than through the ACN–PCN method described above.

### 19.22 Declared Distances

Runways intended for international CAT must have four published **declared distances**, calculated and reported to the nearest **metre or foot**:

- **Take-off Run Available (TORA)**
- **Take-off Distance Available (TODA)**
- **Accelerate-Stop Distance Available (ASDA)**
- **Landing Distance Available (LDA)**

### 19.23 TORA

**TORA** begins where an aircraft is permitted to start the ground roll for departure and ends at the first point ahead where the surface can no longer carry the aircraft's mass under normal operating conditions. It is therefore the physically usable take-off ground-run distance.

### 19.24 TODA

**TODA** begins at the start of TORA and extends along the departure direction to the limiting obstacle that protrudes above the aerodrome surface and could affect aircraft safety in flight, subject to the specified cap of **1.5 × TORA**. Where a suitable **clearway** is provided, the usual relationship is

\[
\mathrm{TODA}=\mathrm{TORA}+\mathrm{clearway},\qquad \mathrm{clearway}\le 0.5\,\mathrm{TORA}.
\]

### 19.25 ASDA

**ASDA** represents the ground distance from the take-off start to the last point at which the aircraft could continue to roll and stop safely after an emergency or rejected take-off. Former terminology used **Emergency Distance Available (EDA)**. Where a stopway exists, the normal relationship is

\[
\mathrm{ASDA}=\mathrm{TORA}+\mathrm{stopway}.
\]

### 19.26 LDA

**LDA** extends from the approved landing start to the point beyond which the surface will not normally support the aeroplane's weight. It generally corresponds to the runway length available for landing. Where a threshold is displaced, the applicable arrangement can also include an available paved stopway.

### 19.27 Condition of the Movement Area and Related Facilities

The **AIS** and **ATS** units must receive information on movement-area conditions and on whether related facilities are operating correctly. AIS receives the condition and status information for aeronautical publication; ATS receives significant information for briefing arriving and departing traffic. Continuous monitoring is necessary, with changes communicated promptly and with particular attention to matters capable of changing aircraft performance or operational safety:

- Aerodrome construction or maintenance activities.
- Surface break-up, unevenness or roughness on runways, taxiways or aprons.
- Accumulations of **snow, slush or ice** on those areas.
- Standing water on runways, taxiways or aprons.
- Snow piles, banks or drifting snow beside those areas.
- De-icing or anti-icing liquid chemicals present on a runway or taxiway.
- Temporary dangers such as parked aircraft obstructing movement.
- Malfunction or absence of any of the aerodrome's visual aids.
- Loss of either primary or secondary electrical supply.

The three specified frozen-water conditions on a runway are **snow, slush and ice**.

## Runways

### 19.28 General

A **runway** is the portion of the manoeuvring area designated for aircraft take-off and landing. Away from the most remote operating sites it is generally prepared, frequently using concrete or bituminous pavement, designed to be straight and without excessive gradients. One paved strip commonly serves two reciprocal landing and take-off directions; those are operationally regarded as two runways. Painted markings help pilots determine how the runway may be used and guide landing alignment and touchdown.

Runways vary substantially from one aerodrome to another, so pilots must become familiar with the layout and procedures of the aerodromes they operate to. At a controlled aerodrome, the **aerodrome controller** controls entry onto and use of the runway. Taxiing onto it, crossing, backtracking, taking off and landing all require the appropriate ATC permission. The controller states the runway designator explicitly, and the pilot reads it back during radiotelephony (**RTF**) exchanges. For example, an instruction to take off from **runway 01** must be acknowledged with that runway number. After the authorised movement is complete, control of the runway remains with ATC.

### 19.29 Usability

Runway **location, orientation and number** depend on local operating constraints, among them the prevailing wind. Because aircraft ordinarily land and take off into wind, alignment strongly affects the aerodrome's **usability factor**. For the aircraft population the facility is intended to accommodate, runway arrangements should achieve a wind-related usability of **at least 95%**.

### 19.30 Types of Runway

The classification of a runway principally reflects the **operations authorised on it**, not the material from which it is constructed. A grass airfield can have clearly established landing directions or none, while a customs or international airport may offer multiple paved runways. Whether operations are visual, instrument or take-off-only determines the associated services, visual markings, guidance signs and physical provision.

### 19.31 Visual Runway

A **non-instrument runway**, also described as a **visual runway**, supports take-offs and landings subject to visual conditions. Typical operating limits concern observed ground visibility or **RVR**, cloud ceiling and whether operations are by day or at night. Its markings identify its visual-operating status.

### 19.32 Instrument Runway

An **instrument runway** has published procedures for instrument departures, approaches or both. Instrument departures are typically specified by a **Standard Instrument Departure (SID)**. Arriving aircraft may use either:

- **Precision approaches**, employing **ILS**, **MLS** or **PAR**, which provide the guidance appropriate to precision procedures.
- **Non-precision approaches**, employing **VOR**, **NDB**, **SRA** or an **ILS used for azimuth guidance alone**.

### 19.33 Take-off Runway

A **take-off-only runway** is designated for departure without a corresponding authorised landing operation. This is commonly a consequence of terrain or other restrictions that prevent a satisfactory instrument arrival or missed approach. Often one runway direction is usable for this purpose and the reciprocal direction is not.

### 19.34 Location of Runway Threshold

The normal threshold location is at the beginning of the available runway surface. **Operational reasons** may justify moving it farther down the paved strip. A **displaced threshold** is indicated with a white transverse stripe across the runway and arrows pointing along the section preceding the new threshold. Possible reasons include pavement unserviceability, runway end safety provision, radio-altimeter operating area requirements, approach glide-path geometry and the need for adequate obstacle clearance.

### 19.35 Length of Runways

Runway length must satisfy the needs of the aircraft planned to use the aerodrome. The chosen length must at least equal the greatest length obtained after accounting for the local conditions relevant to performance: **elevation, temperature, runway gradient, humidity and surface condition/characteristics**. There is no universal requirement to accommodate the most demanding aircraft at its maximum critical mass. When a second runway is planned, its length and provision must contribute to achieving the **95% usability** objective.

Published runway length is measured in **metres**. Fixed distance-to-go signs placed along runway edges indicate remaining distance in **thousands of feet**; the markings are positioned in terms of **300 m** increments, beginning **300 m** beyond the threshold. For a single physical strip to support **instrument approaches at both ends**, the threshold-to-threshold separation must be **at least 1800 m**.

### 19.36 Width of Runways

Runway width has to accommodate the aircraft fleet, particularly its **wingspan** and **outer main gear wheel span**. A runway suitable for previous-generation aircraft may not meet the needs of a larger type such as the **A380**. The runway-width requirements associated with the code system are:

### 19.37 Runway Strips

The **runway strip** is the protective area incorporating the runway and any stopway. Its design minimises obstacles and reduces the consequences of an aircraft departing the pavement, while also offering protection to aircraft passing over it on approach or departure. Essential objects that remain should present the least possible impact hazard: they are made **frangible**, so that they break away when struck.

### 19.38 Length of Runway Strips

Beyond the runway end or stopway, and before the threshold, the required strip extensions are at least:

- **60 m** for runway code numbers **2, 3 and 4**.
- **60 m** for a **code 1 instrument runway**.
- **30 m** for a **code 1 non-instrument runway**.

### 19.39 Width of Runway Strips

Where feasible, the strip extends symmetrically outwards from the runway centreline and its extensions for the full strip length. The following distances apply **to each side** of that centreline:

### 19.40 Grading of Runway Strips

For an **instrument runway**, a graded strip surface is required next to the runway centreline and its extensions so that an aircraft accidentally departing the runway is less likely to be damaged. The graded zone reaches at least **75 m** on each side for codes **3 and 4** and at least **40 m** for codes **1 and 2**. Wherever a strip meets the runway, runway shoulder or stopway, the adjoining surfaces must be at the **same level**.

### 19.41 Objects on a Runway Strip

The protected inner portion of a runway strip must be free of fixed installations other than permitted **visual aids**, such as **PAPI** units. Its stated limits from the runway centreline are:

- **77.5 m** for a **code 4F precision CAT I, II or III** runway.
- **60 m** for **code 3 or 4 precision CAT I, II or III** runways.
- **45 m** for **code 1 or 2 CAT I** runways.

No movable objects may occupy these protected areas during take-off or landing. A non-exempt object within the stated boundaries constitutes an obstacle and should be removed wherever practicable.

### 19.42 Runway End Safety Area (RESA)

A **RESA** is an obstruction-controlled area beyond a runway strip, generally provided at each end about the extended runway centreline. It is not included as operational runway length for performance or flight planning. Instead, it limits the consequences of aircraft **undershooting or overrunning** the runway.

RESAs should be present for **all code 3 and 4 runways** and **code 1 and 2 instrument runways**. Width must be **at least twice the runway width**; a RESA should extend **at least 90 m** outward from the end of the strip. Recommended distances are **240 m** for code **3/4** and **120 m** for code **1/2**.

### 19.43 Clearways

A **clearway** is a defined rectangular surface under the responsible aerodrome authority's control, selected or prepared to allow an aircraft to climb through an initial portion of its departure to a prescribed height. It starts at the **end of TORA**. Its length must not exceed **one-half of TORA**, and its lateral extent must reach **at least 75 m either side** of the extended runway centreline.

### 19.44 Stopways

A **stopway** is a prepared rectangular area immediately beyond TORA on which an aircraft can stop during a rejected take-off. The stopway is **as wide as its associated runway** and is considered when establishing accelerate-stop distance.

### 19.45 Radio Altimeter Operating Area

Precision-approach runways require suitable terrain conditions **before the threshold** for reliable radio-altimeter measurements. For **CAT II/III**, radio-altimeter information is required for determining **decision height (DH)**. For example, the CAT II system-minimum DH of **100 ft** occurs approximately **600 m (one-third NM)** before the aiming point on a glide path descending **300 ft/NM**. With the aiming point usually **300 m beyond the threshold**, the example positions the DH point roughly **300 m before the threshold**. CAT III has a DH of **less than 50 ft**, so an area meeting the CAT II requirement can also serve CAT III.

The operating area must extend **at least 300 m before the threshold** and, normally, **60 m to each side** of the extended runway centreline. Special circumstances may permit reducing that lateral dimension to **not less than 30 m**, provided an aeronautical study demonstrates no adverse effect on aircraft safety. The ground should be broadly level; undulations are not to exceed **7%**.

## Taxiways

### 19.46 Introduction

Aerodrome capacity may be limited by whether taxiways can accept the **dimensions, mass and turning geometry** of the fleet. Taxiway width alone is insufficient: pavement strength needs to match runway strength, while taxiway pavements are exposed to repeated concentrated loads during starting, stopping and turning and can consequently be particularly susceptible to surface damage.

Taxiways may merge, intersect, turn or cross and must provide appropriate direction signs and surface markings. Parallel taxiways must be clearly distinguished from runways; at **Gatwick**, a parallel taxiway has also been used as a secondary runway. Safe taxi routing must consider aircraft waiting before runway entry, clearance to runways and the possibility that the metallic mass of an aircraft close to an approach facility could disturb **ILS or MLS** signals or become an obstacle. The basic **Annex 14** requirement is to provide taxiways enabling **safe, efficient and prompt aircraft ground movement**.

### 19.47 General Layout

Runways should be supported by enough **entry and exit taxiways** to move traffic efficiently. Where the volume of landings makes them beneficial, **rapid-exit taxiways** permit faster runway vacation. A runway end without a taxiway may require extra pavement so aircraft can turn around; intermediate turning provisions can also be appropriate where they reduce taxi time.

### 19.48 Taxiing of Aeroplanes

A taxiway has a **painted centreline**, with lighting where night use requires it. Particularly in turns, the pilot should keep the **nose wheel** on this centreline to achieve the intended main-wheel clearances. Pilots ordinarily develop the required judgement through experience. Where cockpit geometry makes alignment unusually difficult, special visual techniques may be adopted: **Concorde** crews, for example, used windscreen reference marks against the taxiway edge because the nose gear lay far behind the nose of the aircraft.

### 19.49 Rapid Exit Taxiways

**Rapid-exit taxiways**, or **fast turn-offs**, allow landing aircraft to leave the active runway without slowing to the speed required for a conventional right-angle exit. They are valuable on aerodromes with high traffic volumes. Their design wet-surface turn-off speeds are **93 km/h (50 kt)** for **code 3 or 4** and **65 km/h (35 kt)** for **code 1 or 2**. The operator determines the maximum permitted exit speed under dry conditions.

A straight section after the curved exit allows the aircraft to stop completely while clear of any intersecting taxiway. The exit should join the runway at an angle **between 25° and 45° inclusive**, with **30°** preferred.

### 19.50 Minimum Requirements

Taxiway dimensions are selected so the **outermost main wheels** have adequate clearance from the pavement boundary when the **nose wheel follows the centreline**. For these provisions, **wheelbase** means the distance between the foremost nose-wheel point and the trailing edge of the main landing gear. **Wheel-to-edge clearance** is measured from the outside of the main-wheel tyres to the designated taxiway edge.

Turbine-aircraft taxiways need treated shoulders to prevent both **jet-blast erosion** and ingestion of loose ground material by engines. Associated **taxiway strips** identify the surfaces that are to be kept free of obstacles.

### 19.52 Taxiway Curves

Taxi routes should avoid unnecessary changes in heading; bends should be as gradual as the layout permits. Radius is selected to suit the **turning characteristics and usual taxi speeds** of the design aircraft. Since the main wheels track inside the nose-wheel path in a turn, **pavement widening** at bends may be needed to preserve the required outer-main-wheel edge clearance.

### 19.53 Holding Bays and Runway Holding Positions

At a busy aerodrome, departing aircraft must often wait before entering the runway. Demand may exceed the length or capacity of the available access taxiway. **Holding bays** adjacent to runway entrances can contain these queues without blocking through traffic, including where noise or other environmental restrictions influence ground routing. Airports experiencing concentrated traffic peaks may also provide **“sin bins”**, allowing an aircraft with a problem to leave the queue while other movements continue.

Each runway entrance is protected by a clearly defined **mandatory runway holding position**, offset far enough from the runway to suit the operating aircraft. A runway holding position is also required wherever a taxiway's position or alignment could cause an aircraft or vehicle to penetrate an **obstacle limitation surface** or interfere with **ILS/MLS** navigation facilities. The indication of these positions is addressed under aerodrome signs and markings.

Mandatory holding points are established both where a **taxiway meets a runway** and where a **runway meets another runway** while the former is used as a standard taxi route. Minimum offsets are measured from the runway centreline and are given below.

### 19.54 Road Holding Position

A **road holding position** is a designated stopping location for vehicles where an aerodrome road also serves as, or connects with, aircraft movement routes. Traffic lights are usually fitted. ## Aprons

### 19.55 Requirement

Aprons accommodate aircraft **passenger boarding and disembarkation**, **cargo and mail loading/unloading**, and **aircraft servicing**, without obstructing other aerodrome traffic. Overall apron capacity should allow smooth handling of the **maximum anticipated traffic density**. Because aircraft move slowly, stop and concentrate their weight at stands, apron pavement must tolerate **greater local loading stresses than runway pavement**.

Aircraft stands are marked, and adequate separation is maintained between parked aircraft. Minimum stand clearances specified here are **3 m for code A** and **7.5 m for code D and higher**.

### 19.56 Isolated Aircraft Parking Position

An aerodrome must designate a suitable **isolated aircraft stand** or identify appropriate isolated locations to the control tower. It is intended for an aircraft known or suspected to be subject to **unlawful interference**, or requiring segregation for another reason. The location must be at least **100 m** from other stands, buildings and public areas. It must also avoid positions above underground services, including **gas**, **aviation fuel**, **electricity** and **communications** lines.
