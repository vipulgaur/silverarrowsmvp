# Chapter 22: Gas Turbines — Gearboxes and Accessory Drives

## 1. Auxiliary Gearbox

A gas turbine must supply mechanical power to equipment that supports both engine operation and aircraft systems. This power is distributed through an **auxiliary gearbox**, also called an **accessory gearbox** or **accessory drive**.

The gearbox drives equipment such as:

- **Fuel pumps**, which provide fuel for engine operation.
- **Oil pumps**, which circulate and recover lubricating oil.
- **Tacho-generators**, which provide a rotational-speed signal.
- **Hydraulic, pneumatic and electrical equipment**, which supplies services to the engine or aircraft.
- Other engine accessories required for efficient operation.

The usual arrangement takes the drive from the **high-pressure (HP) compressor shaft**. Mechanical power is passed through an **internal gearbox** and then delivered to an **external gearbox**, which provides mounting positions and drive connections for the accessories. In most installations, the **starter motor** is also mounted on this external gearbox.

### Accessory Drive Arrangement on a Modern Turbofan

A large turbofan provides sufficient room within its intake-fairing cowling to accommodate sizeable accessories. As a result, the physical space needed for the gearbox is less of a concern than the route taken by the drive shaft from the HP compressor to the externally mounted equipment.

Taking the drive from the **hot end of the engine** would create substantial mechanical losses. It would also require shaft materials capable of coping with the much higher temperatures in that region. Consequently, the arrangement must allow power to be transmitted from the compressor shaft through the engine to the external accessory gearbox.

## 2. Allowing for Expansion of the Compressor Shaft

The compressor shaft changes length as its temperature varies during engine operation. This produces **axial movement**: the shaft moves slightly along its own longitudinal axis as it expands or contracts.

A conventional bevel-gear drive could be damaged if that movement separated the meshing gear teeth. The loss of contact would briefly interrupt the transfer of power, and the teeth could then strike one another again as the drive re-engaged. A drive transmitting roughly **400–500 horsepower** could suffer severe gear-tooth damage, potentially destroying the bevel gears.

The drive must therefore remain engaged while still permitting the compressor shaft to expand and contract. Two arrangements used to accommodate that movement are a **stub shaft drive** and an **idler gear drive**.

## 3. Stub Shaft Drive

In this method, the compressor shaft is **splined**: a series of longitudinal grooves is formed parallel to its axis. A separate, hollow **stub shaft** is fitted over the compressor shaft. The inside of the stub shaft carries matching teeth that engage with those grooves.

The splines transmit rotation from the compressor shaft to the stub shaft while allowing the compressor shaft to slide axially relative to it. A **location bearing** holds the stub shaft in its correct position, so the gear connection does not move apart as compressor-shaft length changes.

The essential feature is that **rotational drive is maintained without preventing axial expansion or contraction**.

## 4. Idler Gear Drive

An alternative uses an **idler gearshaft** supported and held in position by location bearings.

One end of the idler gearshaft carries a **wide-toothed spur gear**. It meshes with the spur gear attached to the compressor shaft. The extra tooth width allows the compressor-shaft gear to move along its axis while the gears remain engaged.

At the opposite end of the idler gearshaft is a **bevel gear**, which meshes with the gear on the radial drive shaft. The idler arrangement therefore transmits power to the radial drive while accommodating the axial movement of the compressor shaft.

## 5. Spreading the Load

On some engines, accessory demand is divided between **two external gearboxes** rather than being concentrated on one. A second radial drive shaft is taken from the **low-pressure (LP) compressor shaft**, which rotates more slowly than the HP shaft, and used to operate a second gearbox.

This arrangement has two practical benefits:

1. It **distributes the mechanical load** associated with driving the various accessories.
2. It allows the accessories to be **split into two smaller groups**, making them easier to position within the restricted space around the engine.

### High-Speed and Low-Speed Gearboxes

During engine starting, the starter motor turns the **HP compressor shaft first**. Equipment essential to the engine's own operation—including its **fuel pumps and oil pumps**—is therefore grouped on the gearbox driven from the HP shaft.

This gearbox is known as the **high-speed external gearbox**, because it takes its power from the fastest-rotating shaft. The gearbox operated from the LP shaft is correspondingly called the **low-speed external gearbox**.

### External Gearbox Layout and Accessories

Because the gearbox must fit around the curved engine casing, its overall form is frequently **banana-shaped**. The external gearbox is commonly installed on the **underside of the engine** to make maintenance and servicing more accessible.

An external gearbox carries several different accessories and mechanical connections. One example is the **engine hand-turn access**, which enables the engine to be turned for inspection of internal components.

The oil pumps may be built into an **oil-pump pack**. This assembly contains **one pressure pump** and, on some engines, **as many as six scavenge pumps**.

## 6. The Shear Neck

A seized or mechanically failed accessory can create an excessive load on its drive. Without suitable protection, the failure could damage the entire gearbox and cause the associated engine to fail.

For this reason, some accessory drive shafts contain a deliberately weakened section known as a **shear neck**. It performs a mechanical protective function comparable to that of an electrical fuse.

The reduced-diameter portion is designed to fracture when the transmitted load becomes excessive—typically at a load **around 25% above the normal maximum** for the accessory concerned. When it breaks, the failed accessory is disconnected from the drive, allowing the **rest of the gearbox and its other accessories to continue operating**.

Shear necks are **not used on the drives of certain primary engine accessories**, particularly the **oil pumps** and **HP fuel pumps**. Failure of either of these essential units would require the **engine to be shut down immediately**, so allowing the engine to keep operating after their disconnection would not be appropriate.
