# Chapter 9: Piston Engines — Icing

## 1. Engine Icing

Ice within an engine's induction system can restrict the air or fuel reaching its cylinders and reduce the power available. **Carburettor-equipped piston engines** are especially vulnerable, although induction icing is not confined to carburetted installations. Despite long-standing awareness of the problem and improvements in fuel-metering equipment, induction-system icing continues to cause accidents.

### 1.1 Atmospheric Conditions That Encourage Icing

The risk must be considered in air containing substantial moisture, particularly when **relative humidity exceeds 50%**. Induction-system icing can develop over a surprisingly broad outside-air-temperature range, from approximately **−7°C (20°F) to +33°C (90°F)**. Consequently, relatively warm weather does not rule out icing.

These combinations of temperature and humidity can occur throughout the year in the **United Kingdom and Europe**. Pilots must therefore anticipate icing and act before airflow is restricted enough to produce serious engine problems. Once the engine has stopped because its induction passage is blocked by ice, restarting it before an accident occurs may be extremely unlikely.

The likelihood and severity of icing also vary with **engine power setting**. Conditions associated with serious icing at descent power do not necessarily produce the same effect when higher power is being used.

### 1.2 Forms of Induction-System Icing

Three different mechanisms can obstruct an engine's intake or fuel path:

1. **Impact ice:** Atmospheric moisture freezes when it strikes components such as **air filters** or changes of direction in the **induction ducting**. The resulting deposit reduces the passage available for incoming air.
2. **Refrigeration ice (carburettor ice):** A **float-type carburettor** can become cold enough to freeze moisture in the induction air. Cooling comes from the **evaporation of fuel** and the **low-pressure region** inside the carburettor. Ice then accumulates in the induction passage.
3. **Fuel icing:** Moisture suspended in the fuel separates and freezes at the low temperatures encountered in the carburettor. The resulting deposits can collect particularly around **bends in the inlet manifold**, restricting the movement of air and fuel toward the engine.

Pilots need to understand which mechanisms may affect the specific engine installation they operate and how that engine responds when induction heating is selected.

## 2. Carburettor Icing

### 2.1 Recognition in Flight

The usual early symptom on an aircraft with a **fixed-pitch propeller** is an unexplained, gradual **decrease in engine rpm**. If icing progresses, **rough running and vibration** may follow.

With a **constant-speed propeller**, the propeller governor may maintain rpm despite an engine-power reduction. The more useful indications are therefore a **fall in manifold pressure** or a **reduction in airspeed during level flight**.

These symptoms may only become noticeable after icing has already started to interfere with normal induction airflow.

### 2.2 How Carburettor Ice Forms

Within the carburettor throat, two effects contribute to cooling:

- **Fuel vaporization** absorbs heat from the air passing through the carburettor.
- The **pressure reduction in the Venturi** lowers the local temperature further.

Together these processes can produce a carburettor temperature much lower than that of the air entering it. The chapter gives a possible reduction of **22°C (70°F)** relative to incoming-air temperature.

If the incoming air holds enough water vapour, that local cooling allows ice to deposit, especially near the **throttle butterfly**. As the deposit grows, it reduces the cross-sectional area through which air can pass. It may also stop the throttle plate from moving freely. Engine power falls as induction flow becomes restricted; if the blockage becomes complete, the engine may stop.

The description also notes that at outside-air temperatures of **−1°C (14°F) or below**, atmospheric moisture is already frozen and may pass through the carburettor without forming the same type of deposit; on that basis, it states that carburettor heat should not be used in those circumstances.

## 3. Action to Be Taken if Engine Icing Is Suspected

If signs of carburettor icing appear, select **FULL HOT** on the carburettor-heat control. Keep the setting hot long enough to remove the accumulated ice. Clearing may require **up to one minute or longer**, depending on the amount of ice present.

**Partially heated air must not be selected** unless the aeroplane has a **carburettor-air-temperature gauge** that allows its temperature to be monitored.

### 3.1 What Happens When Heat Is Applied

The heating system directs air warmed around the **engine exhaust pipe** into the induction system. This air melts the ice, and the resulting water passes onward through the engine. While the ice is being removed, the engine may temporarily run more roughly and lose still more power as water enters the cylinders.

This temporary worsening does **not** necessarily mean that the heat selection has failed. The control should not be returned prematurely to **OFF/COLD** simply because the engine initially sounds or runs worse after heat is applied.

### 3.2 Low-Power Flight and Long Descents

Extended operation at reduced power—particularly during **glide descents** or descents for an approach—makes induction icing more likely. Because carburettor heat depends on energy taken from the engine, the available heating effect decreases as the engine cools during a prolonged low-power descent.

When icing conditions are present, select **full carburettor heat before reducing engine power**, so the heat system initially benefits from the higher engine temperature. During a lengthy descent, increase the engine periodically to a **cruise-power setting every 500–1,000 ft**. This helps keep the engine warm, maintains sufficient heat for ice removal and reduces the risk of **lead fouling on the spark plugs**.

### 3.3 Taxiing and Preparation for Take-off

Ice may also develop while the aircraft is **taxiing with a small throttle opening** or the engine is idling. Where icing is suspected or present, heated air must be used to remove it before take-off. Then:

1. Return the carburettor-heat selector to **COLD/OFF**.
2. Advance the throttle for take-off only after restoring the cold-air setting.
3. Check that the engine develops the proper **take-off rpm or manifold pressure**.

**Carburettor heat must not be used during take-off.**

## 4. Engine Considerations When Using Carburettor Heat

Selecting warm induction air changes more than the amount of ice in the carburettor. Several associated engine effects have to be considered:

- **Power reduction:** Hot induction air can reduce power output by approximately **15%**.
- **Mixture enrichment:** Heating the intake charge produces a **richer mixture**, which may make the engine run roughly.
- **High-power restriction:** Carburettor heat should not be selected at settings above **80% engine power** because of the associated risk of **detonation and engine damage**. The relatively wide throttle opening used at such power settings should also make intake icing unlikely.
- **Prolonged heating:** Continuous use is undesirable because it changes mixture strength and increases engine temperatures. Heating is intended to be used for the period needed to clear the problem, rather than maintained unnecessarily after icing conditions have ended.

### 4.1 Confirming That the Ice Has Cleared

After the icing has been removed, a return to cold induction air should allow the **rpm or manifold-pressure indication** to recover. If the reading rises **above the value noted before applying heat**, this indicates that the earlier loss of performance was associated with ice accumulation.

Once the aircraft is clear of the relevant icing conditions, carburettor heat should not remain selected. Nevertheless, the pilot should check periodically that fresh ice has not formed.

## 5. Fuel-Injected Engines

**Fuel-injected piston engines** do not have the float-chamber Venturi icing mechanism described for conventional carburettors. They are not, however, immune to restricted induction airflow or icing elsewhere in the installation.

Possible locations include:

- **Bends in the induction passages**, where fuel icing may accumulate.
- **Impact-sensing tubes**, where moisture can freeze on exposed surfaces.
- **Air filters**, which are particularly susceptible to impact ice while operating in **cloud at low temperatures**.

If this type of icing is encountered, select the engine's **alternate-air supply** and carry out the applicable icing procedure in the **aircraft checklist**.

## 6. Diesel Engines

Diesel aircraft engines do not experience the normal float-chamber-carburettor icing mechanism because their induction systems do **not use a conventional carburettor or its Venturi**.

Two other low-temperature problems remain relevant:

- **Impact icing at the intake filter:** **Ice guards** provide a bypass arrangement that allows air to enter when the filter has become obstructed by ice.
- **Fuel waxing:** In sufficiently low temperatures, diesel fuel can become less fluid as its viscosity rises. This process, known as **waxing**, is addressed by adding suitable **fuel additives** or using **fuel heaters** in the fuel pipes or filters to warm the fuel before it reaches the engine.

## 7. Operational Procedures

### 7.1 Ground Operation

Keep the use of carburettor heat on the ground **to a minimum**. The warmed intake air is **unfiltered**, so prolonged operation may carry dust and dirt into the engine, increasing wear of the **pistons and cylinders**.

Before take-off, carry out the carburettor-heat functional check:

1. Note the existing engine rpm.
2. Select carburettor heat **HOT** and confirm an rpm drop of approximately **100 rpm**.
3. Return the control to **OFF/COLD** and confirm that the rpm recovers to its previously selected value.

### 7.2 Take-off

If ice has formed while the aircraft is on the ground, use carburettor heat to clear the deposit **before** taking off. Restore the selector to **OFF/COLD** and ensure that the engine achieves its **normal take-off power** before proceeding.

### 7.3 Climb

Do not use carburettor heat during the climb. The restriction also applies at power settings greater than **80%**, stated as approximately **2,500 rpm** in the chapter.

### 7.4 Flight Operations

Maintain awareness of icing risk on **damp, cloudy, foggy or hazy days** and during flight **near cloud, in rain or in drizzle**. An unexplained decrease in **rpm or manifold pressure** is a warning sign.

Where routine checks for carburettor icing are required:

1. Observe and remember the current engine **rpm**.
2. Select carburettor heat for approximately **15–30 seconds**.
3. Note the rpm reduction caused by selecting hot air, listen for changes in engine sound and consider the **outside-air temperature**.
4. Watch for an rpm **increase while heat is applied**, or for the engine to settle at a **higher rpm than the initial reading** after the control is returned to COLD.

Either type of rpm recovery can indicate that ice was present. If the aircraft remains in icing conditions, continue to use heat as needed while those conditions persist.

### 7.5 Descents

During **glide descents** or prolonged low-power flight below **1,800 rpm**, apply carburettor heat. Briefly raise power to warm and clear the engine at approximately **500–1,000 ft** intervals throughout the descent.

### 7.6 Approach and Landing

The normal position of the carburettor-heat selector during **approach and landing** is **COLD**, except during a **glide approach**. When icing is known or suspected, select **FULL HOT**.

Before applying engine power for a **roller landing** or an **overshoot**, move the carburettor-heat control back to **COLD**.

### 7.7 Hot and Dry Conditions

During **hot, dry weather**, introducing additional hot intake air can enrich the mixture sufficiently to produce a **rich cut** and stop combustion. Carburettor heat must therefore be selected with regard to the conditions and the engine's response, rather than applied automatically without considering whether it is appropriate.
