# Chapter 17: Gas Turbines — The Turbine Assembly

## The Task of the Turbine Assembly

The turbine performs the opposite energy-transfer function to an axial-flow compressor. A compressor receives mechanical energy and adds it to the passing air; a turbine takes energy from the hot gases leaving the combustion system and converts that energy into rotation of a shaft.

The gas first passes through a stationary set of **nozzle guide vanes (NGVs)**, which direct it onto a rotating row of turbine blades. Energy extracted from this flow supplies the mechanical power required by the **compressor** and engine **gearboxes**. The gearboxes can drive engine accessories. Where an engine is intended to deliver shaft power rather than produce most of its propulsion from a jet, the turbine can also supply power to a **propeller or rotor**.

Before reaching the turbine, the gas carries energy in three forms:

- **Heat energy:** associated with the high gas temperature.
- **Pressure (potential) energy:** associated with the gas pressure.
- **Kinetic energy:** associated with gas velocity.

As the turbine converts the gas energy into mechanical work, these energy components are reduced through the turbine assembly. This does not mean that gas velocity must continuously decrease everywhere in the engine: the gas travels more slowly within the **combustion chamber** than it does in the **exhaust unit**.

## The Stresses in the Turbine

The combination of **very high rotational speed** and **very high operating temperature** creates exceptionally severe working conditions for turbine components.

- Turbine blade tips may travel faster than **1,500 ft/s** during normal operation.
- In a modern engine, gas temperature at the turbine may approach **1,700°C**.
- Hot-gas velocity may reach **2,500 ft/s**, a value close to the speed of sound at the temperatures involved.
- A blade weighing only **2 oz** when stationary may impose a load equivalent to approximately **2 tons** at maximum rotational speed.

The rotating blade is pulled outward by a substantial centrifugal load. At the same time, the hot environment weakens the material's ability to resist continued deformation. Together, the **tensile load** and **temperature** produce **creep**: progressive stretching that goes beyond the metal's capacity to return to its original dimensions.

Creep accumulates as an engine operates. Even when the specified **temperature** and **rpm** limits have been respected, the blades can gradually become longer with operating hours and repeated engine cycles. Consequently, turbine blades have a **finite service life**; if allowed to deteriorate indefinitely, they will eventually fail.

Two forms of fatigue are distinguished:

- **Low-cycle fatigue:** failure after a comparatively small number of cycles when the operating demands are severe.
- **High-cycle fatigue:** failure after a larger number of cycles where the individual operating demands are less severe.

## Turbine Blade Materials

### Development of Heat-Resistant Alloys

The earliest gas-turbine blades were made from **high-temperature steels**. The thermal limits of those steels restricted the permissible temperature in the rear section of the engine. Because a gas turbine is fundamentally a **heat engine**, restricting its operating temperature also restricted its available power.

A major improvement came with **nickel-based alloys**, followed by more advanced **superalloys**. These alloys are blends of several elements, including **chromium, cobalt, nickel, titanium, tungsten and carbon**. Their purpose is to withstand the combined effects of high temperature and heavy mechanical loading.

Approximate operating-temperature capabilities given for these materials are:

| Blade material or arrangement | Approximate temperature limit |
|---|---:|
| Superalloy blade without internal cooling | **1,100°C** |
| Internally cooled superalloy blade | **1,425°C** |

### Powder Metallurgy and Single-Crystal Casting

**Powder metallurgy** produces components by hot-pressing powdered superalloy material until it forms a solid component. Another development, employed in particularly advanced turbine designs, is **single-crystal casting**.

Conventional metal-solidification methods produce many crystalline regions, or **grains**. The interfaces between those grains form weak areas in the structure and can provide starting points for damage or failure. In a **single-crystal blade**, the component is formed as a single grain, eliminating the internal grain boundaries associated with those weaknesses. The result is much greater resistance to **creep** and improved resistance to the deterioration described for conventional blades.

### Ceramic Protection

**Ceramic materials** are also used in turbine-blade manufacture and protection. An early technique was to apply ceramic as a **plasma-sprayed coating**.

This coating helps defend the underlying blade metal against corrosion arising from the interaction of:

- The blade's base metal.
- **Sodium** present in the air.
- **Sulphur** contained in the fuel.

## The Turbine Stage

A compressor increases the pressure energy of the airflow, whereas the turbine obtains useful work by allowing the pressure energy of the hot gas to decrease.

The conversion takes place in two related steps:

1. **Stationary nozzle guide vanes:** gas pressure is reduced as part of the available pressure energy is converted into **gas velocity**.
2. **Rotating turbine blades:** the moving blades convert energy from the gas flow into **mechanical shaft power**.

A single **turbine stage** therefore contains **one stationary row of nozzle guide vanes** followed by **one rotating row of turbine blades**. One shaft can support a turbine with several stages. When this turbine is mechanically linked to a compressor, the interconnected rotating assembly forms a **spool**.

A three-stage turbine fitted to a single shaft, similar to that of a **Rolls-Royce Dart turboprop**, illustrates several design features.

### Annulus Area and Blade Length

The passage carrying gas through successive turbine stages is made progressively **divergent**. As the hot gas expands, the available annular flow area becomes larger. Blades can therefore be longer in the stages farther downstream, helping to control gas velocity as the gas flows through the expanding passage.

### Blade Shrouds

A **turbine blade shroud** helps limit gas escaping over the outer blade tips without doing useful work. Shrouding also helps to **reduce blade vibration**.

### Blade-Tip Clearance Control

The distance between the rotating blade tips and the stationary casing does not remain fixed under all operating conditions. Turbine components heat and cool at different rates, so their differing rates of **thermal expansion and contraction** change this clearance.

One way to reduce leakage is to use an **abradable lining** in the casing. A more effective solution is **active clearance control**, which regulates the casing and its temperature to maintain a small blade-tip clearance throughout the operating cycle. Similar clearance-control principles are used in modern compressors.

## The Free (Power) Turbine

A turbine forming part of a compressor **spool** cannot rotate at an entirely independent speed: its speed must suit the compressor's operating requirements, including the rotational speed at which the compressor achieves its intended compression performance.

A **free power turbine** is different. It has **no mechanical connection to the compressor**. Instead, it drives only the reduction gearbox supplying a **propeller or rotor**, so the free turbine can operate at a speed selected to suit its own design and power-output requirements.

Several practical benefits follow:

- During **taxiing**, the propeller can be kept at a relatively low rpm, reducing **noise** and **brake wear**.
- The engine needs **less starting torque**.
- A **rotor parking brake** can be installed, avoiding the risks associated with propellers continuing to rotate in windy conditions while the aircraft is on the ground.

## Multi-spool Engines

One way to obtain more turbine power is to increase **turbine diameter**. However, a larger diameter increases the engine's frontal area and associated drag, while the increased radius also produces greater **centrifugal stresses**.

Using **additional turbine stages** provides another means of increasing power output without requiring a comparable increase in turbine diameter.

The design involves a trade-off between blade speed, efficiency and structural loading:

- Blade efficiency generally improves as **rotational speed increases**, with losses decreasing in relation to the **square of mean blade speed**.
- At the same time, blade stresses rise in proportion to the **square of blade speed**.
- Raising the speed can therefore call for stronger turbine blades; if strength is obtained by adding material, blade weight and the associated stresses may increase further.

**High-bypass-ratio engines** help address this compromise because their superior **propulsive efficiency** permits a smaller turbine for a given thrust requirement.

### Triple-spool Arrangement

A **three-spool** engine uses three sets of rotating turbine/compressor components, each able to operate at the speed appropriate to its role:

1. **High-pressure (HP) spool:** the **HP turbine** turns the **HP compressor** at relatively high speed.
2. **Intermediate-pressure (IP) spool:** the **IP turbine**, situated behind the HP turbine, turns the **IP compressor** through a separate shaft running inside the HP turbine shaft.
3. **Low-pressure (LP) spool:** the rearmost **LP turbine**, illustrated with **two stages**, drives the **fan**, which also acts as the **LP compressor**. It rotates more slowly than the other two spools.

In this high-bypass arrangement, the LP turbine supplies the mechanical energy responsible for **almost all the thrust**, by powering the fan that accelerates a **large mass of bypass air** to a comparatively **low velocity** relative to the exhaust of a pure turbojet.

The LP drive shaft runs **concentrically inside the shafts** joining the HP and IP compressors to their corresponding turbines.

## Blade Shape

The stationary **nozzle guide vanes** have an **aerofoil cross-section**. The spaces between them act as **convergent ducts**, changing part of the gas's pressure energy into velocity energy before the flow reaches the moving turbine blades.

Three blade-action arrangements are distinguished:

- **Impulse blades:** the moving gas produces a turning effect on the blades in a manner compared to a **water wheel**.
- **Reaction blades:** blade rotation results from the **reaction to aerodynamic lift** produced by the blade.
- **Impulse–reaction blades:** blade designs incorporate **both** effects.

A combined **impulse–reaction** blade is the normal solution for many gas-turbine applications. Pure impulse or pure reaction designs are relatively uncommon in main engine turbines.

### Blade Twist and Work Distribution

The proportion of impulse and reaction action varies along a combination blade. Its cross-sectional shape changes from the **root** to the **tip**, with a **greater blade angle at the tip than at the base**. The resulting **twist** distributes the work extracted from the moving gas more evenly along the blade's length.

This blade geometry also allows the gas to leave the turbine and enter the exhaust system with a **uniform axial velocity**.

The proportions of impulse and reaction used depend on the **engine's design requirements**. Combination blades are widespread in gas-turbine engines, while **impulse-type blades** are also employed in **air-starter motors**.

When a **pure reaction** blading arrangement is used, the nozzle guide vanes are arranged to **turn the gas flow without changing its pressure**. Such pure reaction systems are uncommon.

## Turbine Blade Fixing

The fastening between each turbine blade and its rotor disc must withstand extremely large loads when the engine reaches operating speed. A commonly used attachment on modern gas turbines is the **fir-tree root**.

The blade root has a series of precisely machined **serrations** that fit the matching slots in the turbine disc. The purpose of this profile is to **spread the centrifugal loading across several contact surfaces** rather than concentrating it at a single point.

When the engine is stopped, the blade can remain relatively **free within the fir-tree slot**. As the turbine accelerates, **centrifugal force** presses the root firmly into its seating, producing the tight fit needed during operation.

## Losses in the Turbine

Although the turbine converts gas energy into mechanical energy very efficiently, some losses remain. The approximate **total loss is 8%**, distributed as follows:

| Source of loss | Approximate proportion |
|---|---:|
| Aerodynamic losses associated with the **turbine blades** | **3.5%** |
| Aerodynamic losses associated with the **nozzle guide vanes** | **1.5%** |
| **Leakage across blade tips** | Approximately **1.5%** |
| Losses in the **exhaust system** | Approximately **1.5%** |
| **Total** | **8%** |

The last two items account, in roughly equal shares, for the **3%** remaining after the principal blade and nozzle-guide-vane aerodynamic losses have been considered.

## Temperature Measurement

The maximum permissible gas temperature is a major limit on how much **thrust** or **shaft horsepower (SHP)** the engine can produce. Exceeding the temperature restriction can cause **irreparable turbine damage**. Monitoring turbine temperature is therefore essential.

### Thermocouple Location and Connection

Temperature is detected using **thermocouples** positioned in the hot gas stream at a chosen location in or after the turbine assembly. A common arrangement places the sensing point **after the high-pressure or low-pressure turbine** and displays the value as **Exhaust Gas Temperature (EGT)**.

The thermocouples are electrically connected **in parallel**. One benefit of this arrangement is that failure of an individual probe does not substantially change the gauge indication; the measured temperature may fall slightly, but the reading remains almost unaffected.

### Other Temperature Terminology

Older engines may use different names for the gas-temperature reading, depending on where the sensors are positioned:

- **Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT)**.
- **Turbine Gas Temperature (TGT)**.
- **Jet Pipe Temperature (JPT)**.

### Protection of the Sensing Probes

In more recent engine designs, temperature probes may be housed **inside selected stationary nozzle guide vanes**. This protects the probes from direct impact by the very high-speed gas flow while permitting the temperature to be detected.

An increase in commanded engine output, whether expressed as **thrust** or **SHP**, requires increased **fuel flow** and is accompanied by a rise in **EGT**. When fuel flow and engine output are reduced, EGT falls correspondingly.
