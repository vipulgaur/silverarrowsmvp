# Chapter 3: Piston Engines — Lubrication

## 1. Function of the Lubrication System

### 1.1 Why engine components require lubrication

The internal parts of a piston engine operate under substantial mechanical loads, at high temperatures and often at considerable rotational or reciprocating speeds. Many of these parts are metallic, and their surfaces move against one another. **Friction** is the resistance that occurs where these surfaces interact. Its magnitude generally grows as loading, temperature and speed increase.

Repeated contact between moving components also leads to **wear**, meaning the gradual removal or deterioration of material from their surfaces. A lubricant places a substance with relatively low frictional characteristics between those surfaces, reducing direct metal-to-metal contact and limiting both friction and wear.

Lubrication can be provided by different substances, including grease, powders and some solid materials. In piston-aircraft engines, however, the principal lubricant is **oil**. Two ways in which it reaches moving parts are:

- **Pressure lubrication:** A pump delivers oil under pressure to the parts requiring lubrication.
- **Splash lubrication:** Movement of engine components distributes oil by splashing it onto surfaces.

### 1.2 Primary and secondary functions

Limiting **friction and wear** is the lubrication system's primary purpose. The circulating oil also performs several other important jobs:

- **Cooling:** Oil removes heat from internal engine parts and carries it away from the areas where it is generated. Of the secondary duties, this is especially important.
- **Cleaning:** As oil moves through the engine, it picks up residues and by-products associated with combustion.
- **Corrosion protection:** Oil provides a protective coating on internal metal surfaces, helping resist corrosion.
- **Hydraulic medium:** The oil film helps cushion loading between the crankshaft and its bearings, reducing shocks and vibration. Engine oil may also supply hydraulic power for a variable-pitch propeller.
- **Indication of engine condition:** Conditions within the oil system provide information about how the engine is operating. Abnormal indications can warn the pilot of developing mechanical problems or a possible loss of power.

Increased internal friction requires more power to overcome mechanical resistance. This raises **friction horsepower**, leaving less **brake horsepower** available as useful output from the engine. For this reason, lubrication is significant both for mechanical protection and for engine performance.

## 2. Wet-Sump and Dry-Sump Lubricating Systems

The two principal oil-system arrangements are **wet sump** and **dry sump**. Both circulate lubricating oil through the moving engine parts; the essential difference is **where the main oil supply is stored**. Selection of an arrangement is influenced by the engine's power output and the aircraft's intended operation.

### 2.1 Wet-sump system

A wet-sump installation keeps its oil supply in the lower portion, or **sump**, of the engine. This arrangement simplifies the engine's construction and is commonly found on light aircraft not designed for aerobatics. It nevertheless has limitations:

1. **Problems during manoeuvring:** Engine attitude and movement may displace the oil, disturbing its supply to the lubrication points. Rotating shafts can throw sump oil around the crankcase and produce excess oiling. Inverted flight is particularly troublesome.
2. **More difficult oil-temperature control:** The oil supply sits within the hot engine structure.
3. **Greater susceptibility to deterioration:** Continuous exposure to hot engine components encourages contamination and oxidation.
4. **Restricted stored quantity:** The amount of oil available is constrained by the physical size of the sump.

### 2.2 Dry-sump system

In a dry-sump installation, most of the oil is kept in a **separately mounted tank**, away from the engine sump. This arrangement avoids the wet-sump storage disadvantages and allows a different approach to oil capacity and temperature management.

A **pressure pump** sends oil through the engine to lubricate its internal parts. A separate **scavenge pump**, or group of scavenge pumps, removes returning oil from the engine sump and sends it back to the tank. The scavenge function prevents an excessive quantity of oil from accumulating within the engine.

Oil-system layouts vary between engines, but the functional components have much in common. A typical dry-sump system comprises an oil tank, suction and pressure filters, pressure and scavenge pumps, an oil cooler, oil-temperature and oil-pressure indications, and connecting pipes. The wet-sump arrangement described here omits the separate storage tank and uses a pressure pump rather than a separate pressure/scavenge pair.

## 3. The Oil Tank

### 3.1 Tank construction and location

The separate tank in a dry-sump system is usually manufactured from sheet metal. Internal strengthening and **baffles** help it withstand the forces associated with oil moving inside the tank during aircraft manoeuvres.

Where the installation allows it, the tank is positioned **above the engine** so gravity can assist the feed to the pressure pump. It must contain sufficient oil for engine requirements while also leaving an **air space** above the oil. That space is needed for several operational reasons:

- **Oil returning after start:** Oil left on the crankcase surfaces at the end of an earlier run gradually drains into the sump while the engine is stopped. When the engine starts again, the scavenge pump returns that accumulated oil to the tank; the tank needs room to receive it.
- **Thermal expansion:** Oil expands as it absorbs heat, especially from the bearings and other hot components.
- **Aeration and frothing:** The oil may contain entrained air and produce foam, which occupies additional space.
- **Oil returned from other equipment:** Feathering propellers and other automatic control devices can displace oil back into the tank.

### 3.2 Hot well or hot pot

A **hot pot**, also called a **hot well**, is a small reservoir within the main oil tank. Its purpose is to let the engine warm a limited circulating quantity of oil more quickly following a cold start, before the full tank contents are introduced into circulation.

The hot pot is a metal cylinder around the outlet supplying the pressure pump. Consequently, oil travelling to the pump must first enter this inner compartment. During a cold start, oil is drawn from the hot pot and its level falls, exposing a ring of small inlet holes. Because cold oil is thick, those holes present substantial resistance to its movement, so relatively little cold oil can enter from the surrounding tank. Oil coming back from the engine returns into the hot pot and circulates again.

As the returned oil warms the walls of the hot pot, oil immediately outside its small holes also becomes warmer and less viscous. It can then pass more easily through the openings. An increasing quantity of oil from the main tank therefore joins the circulation as temperatures rise. Before reaching the pressure pump, the oil passes through the **suction filter**.

### 3.3 Oil reserve for feathering

On aircraft with **feathering propellers**, the lower set of ports feeding the hot pot is positioned above the tank bottom. Oil below that level provides a **feathering reserve**. If the normal feed line leaks badly or fails and the tank drains through its ordinary outlet, oil below the port level is retained for the feathering requirement.

### 3.4 De-aeration and venting

The scavenge return line carries oil into the hot pot by routing it over an internal **de-aerator plate**. As the oil crosses this plate, air is separated out, limiting the amount of froth reaching the reservoir. The tank is connected to the **crankcase breather**, allowing venting and helping prevent oil losses when frothing becomes excessive.

## 4. The Suction Filter

The **suction filter** sits in the oil path between the storage tank and the pressure pump. It normally uses comparatively coarse wire mesh, capable of capturing larger particles before they can enter and damage the pump. Finer filtration is provided later in the system by the pressure filter.

## 5. The Pressure Pump

### 5.1 Spur-gear operation

A typical pressure pump contains **two closely meshing, deep-toothed spur gears** within a precisely fitting housing. The gears are driven from the engine's accessory housing. As they rotate, oil is carried around the outside of the gear pair in the spaces between the gear teeth and housing, from inlet toward outlet.

Oil emerging from the pump encounters the resistance of the components and passages that it must lubricate. This resistance to flow produces **oil pressure**. The pressure achieved depends principally on:

- **Pump speed**;
- **Oil temperature**, which affects viscosity; and
- **Resistance within the lubrication circuit**.

The pump is sized to supply the required minimum pressure even under an unfavourable combination of **low rotational speed** and **high inlet-oil temperature**. Because the pump must work under those conditions, its output under more favourable operating conditions can otherwise generate pressure that is too high.

### 5.2 Pressure relief valve

A **pressure relief valve (PRV)** prevents excessive system pressure. It bridges the pump outlet and inlet: when the system reaches a preset pressure, the valve opens and redirects enough oil to the inlet side to hold pressure within the permitted maximum. Across the engine's operating range—from idle to maximum revolutions per minute—normal indicated oil pressure will fall within a specified range.

## 6. The Check Valve

**Check valves** are also known as **non-return** or **one-way valves**. They are especially relevant when a dry-sump tank stands above the engine and normally supplies the pressure pump by gravity.

Following shutdown, hot oil becomes relatively thin. Gravity can then force it through the small internal clearances of a stationary pressure pump. If there were no device to stop this flow, the tank could gradually empty into the crankcase, leaving the engine flooded with oil. This shutdown condition is sometimes described as **over-oiling**.

A check valve blocks the unwanted flow until the engine is started again. The device may take the form of a **lightly spring-loaded valve** or an **electrically operated shut-off valve (SOV)**.

## 7. The Pressure Filter

Oil from the pressure pump passes through the **pressure filter** before it reaches the engine bearings and other lubricated components. Unlike the suction filter, which captures comparatively large debris, this filter is intended to remove **very small solid particles**.

A **spring-loaded bypass or relief valve** is fitted around the filter element. It permits oil to continue reaching the engine when cold, thick oil cannot pass readily through the element, or when the element becomes obstructed. The bypass arrangement also provides protection if debris from a failed pressure pump would otherwise block the normal flow path.

## 8. The Scavenge Pump

In a dry-sump engine, the **scavenge pump** collects oil from the sump and returns it to the separate tank. It must deal with oil that has drained down into the crankcase while the engine was stopped, as well as oil returning during normal operation.

If the scavenge pump had only the same delivery capacity as the pressure pump, it might not clear the extra oil accumulated before startup. The sump could remain excessively full. To maintain dry-sump operation, the scavenge pump is therefore designed with **greater capacity than the pressure pump**. A typical scavenge-pump capacity is **25–50% higher**.

## 9. Oil Cooler

### 9.1 Why oil cooling is necessary

Oil removes heat from engine components, but the lubricant itself must not be allowed to become excessively hot. Oil that overheats can lose the properties needed to provide effective lubrication. An **oil cooler** is installed to reject heat from the circulating oil to the outside airflow.

### 9.2 Cooler construction

The cooler includes a **matrix or tube bundle** that divides the oil into relatively thin passages, exposing a large oil-contact area to cooling air. The reference construction uses circular tubes whose ends are expanded into hexagonal shapes, allowing neighbouring tubes to be soldered together at their contacting faces. Their flats are also soldered to the inside of the surrounding cooler jacket, securing the matrix in place.

### 9.3 Cold-oil protection

When an engine is started in cold conditions, the cooler may contain highly viscous oil. Pushing that oil through the narrow matrix passages can require excessive pressure and risk cooler damage. An **anti-surge valve** allows oil to bypass the cooler matrix while it is too cold and resistant to flow.

### 9.4 Factors governing oil temperature

The oil temperature depends on three principal factors:

1. **Engine power and heat generation:** Greater heat production places more demand on the oil's cooling function.
2. **Cooling-air temperature:** Cooler ambient air can carry away heat more effectively.
3. **Cooling-air flow rate:** The amount of air passing through the cooler influences the heat that can be removed.

On some light aircraft, cooling-air movement depends mainly on the aircraft's **forward airspeed** in flight and the **propeller slipstream** on the ground. A prolonged climb may combine **high engine power with relatively low forward speed**, increasing the danger of high oil temperature. The flight manual therefore provides recommended climb speeds consistent with adequate cooling.

More powerful aircraft may use **adjustable shutters** behind the cooler to regulate airflow. Closing the shutters during startup helps the oil warm rapidly and reduces the internal friction associated with cold oil. As oil temperature rises, the shutters open to admit enough cooling air. They may close again when the aircraft climbs into colder air at altitude. Shutter operation may be **manual or automatic**.

Aviation diesel-engine lubrication arrangements are commonly **wet-sump**, and the textbook identifies an oil cooler as necessary for removing their additional engine heat.

## 10. Lubrication Monitoring Instruments

The main parameters used to monitor engine lubrication are **oil temperature**, **oil pressure**, and **oil quantity**.

### 10.1 Temperature indication

Oil temperature is measured **at the inlet of the engine-driven pressure pump**. On most aircraft, an electrical sensor transmits this measurement to a cockpit indication. A temperature around **85°C** is described as normal in the source.

### 10.2 Pressure indication

Oil pressure is measured **at the outlet of the engine-driven pressure pump**. The pressure depends on engine size and loading; a representative range is **50–100 psi**. A pressure indication may be obtained electrically or through a direct-reading mechanical arrangement. These measurement methods are addressed in the engine-instrumentation material.

### 10.3 Quantity and operating limits

The cockpit must provide indications of **oil pressure and oil temperature**. Oil quantity may also be displayed; if it is not, an accessible means of checking the level before flight must be available, such as a **dipstick** or **sight glass**.

Interpreting pressure and temperature **together** provides particularly valuable information about engine health. Values outside the permitted operating limits can be an early indication of a developing failure.

## 11. Viscosity

**Viscosity** describes a fluid's internal resistance to flowing. A liquid that moves easily is considered **low-viscosity**, or *thin*; a liquid that resists movement is **high-viscosity**, or *thick*.

Viscosity is important because aircraft engines encounter different loads, power settings and surrounding air temperatures. The correct oil must maintain suitable flowing and lubricating characteristics despite these changes.

For typical oils, **higher temperature lowers viscosity**, while lower temperature raises it. As an engine moves from a cold start to prolonged high-power operation, its oil temperature changes substantially. The oil must remain within an acceptable viscosity range throughout the conditions for which it is specified. The textbook refers to temperature-related viscosity behaviour in terms of **viscosity index**.

## 12. Viscosity Grade Numbering

Viscosity-grading systems provide a standard basis for comparing oils. A common testing principle is to measure how long a specified amount of oil takes to pass through an opening of known dimensions at a controlled temperature.

Two numbering schemes mentioned for aviation oils are:

- **SAE:** Society of Automotive Engineers viscosity numbering.
- **Saybolt Universal:** A separate viscosity numbering system.

In either scheme as presented here, **a lower grade number indicates a thinner oil**. The textbook gives the following approximate comparison:

| Commercial SAE number | Saybolt Universal number |
|---:|---:|
| 30 | 60 |
| 40 | 80 |
| 50 | 100 |
| 60 | 120 |

For these listed grades, the commercial SAE number is half the corresponding Saybolt number.

A lightly loaded engine may use a comparatively **low-viscosity oil**. More highly loaded or powerful engines tend to require **higher-viscosity oil**. The climatic conditions in which the engine operates also affect grade selection. As the textbook's example, a light aircraft operating in the United Kingdom might use **grade 80 in winter** and **grade 100 in summer**, depending on the usual ambient temperature.

A very thick oil in extremely cold conditions can make starting difficult. **Multigrade oils** are formulated to function over a wider temperature range; an example is **SAE 15W/50**, providing suitable lower-temperature flow characteristics while retaining an appropriate higher-temperature viscosity grade.

## 13. Types of Oil

### 13.1 Straight oil

Aircraft piston-engine oils are commonly **mineral-based**. When an oil does not contain added performance compounds, it is described as **straight oil**. The container is generally identified by a viscosity number alone—for example, **80** for a straight grade-80 oil.

Straight oils are commonly used when **running in a new engine**, or in installations for which a specific straight oil is prescribed.

### 13.2 Compound oil

A mineral oil containing ingredients added to alter or improve particular characteristics is a **compound oil**. Additives cited in the textbook include:

- **Antioxidants**, used to resist deterioration through oxidation;
- **Detergents**, associated with cleaning; and
- **Oiliness agents**, added for lubricating properties.

A compound oil is identified both by its viscosity grade and by additional letters, which depend on the manufacturer. A grade-80 compound oil might be marked **AD 80** or **W 80**. **AD** stands for **Ashless Dispersant**, describing an oil with particular cleaning and dispersing characteristics.

Although mineral-based formulations are typical, the textbook also notes that some engine manufacturers have evaluated and approved **semi-synthetic oils** for particular piston-engine applications.

## 14. Operational Considerations

### 14.1 Interpreting oil indications

Engine-oil **temperature and pressure** are major indicators of mechanical condition, but their meaning depends on the operating circumstances. The pilot must interpret them as related measurements rather than treating each reading in isolation.

Following engine start, **positive oil pressure** should appear within the time specified for the aircraft. The textbook gives **30 seconds for the Piper Warrior** as an example.

An engine started cold can initially show unusually **high oil pressure**, because cold oil is more viscous. This is not necessarily abnormal if the pressure reduces into the specified range as the oil warms. Conversely, **high oil temperature** can accompany **low pressure** because hotter oil flows more easily. Pressure should be assessed against its limits at the appropriate normal operating temperature.

Oil-pressure fluctuations can indicate **low oil quantity** or a malfunction in the lubrication system. A **low-pressure indication at normal operating temperature** may point to an imminent engine failure; in that situation the textbook calls for a landing **as soon as possible**.

### 14.2 Hydraulicing in radial and inverted engines

Radial and inverted piston engines can accumulate oil in their **lower cylinders** while stopped. This can produce a condition called **hydraulicing**, or **hydraulic lock**.

Oil does not compress significantly. If enough oil collects above a piston, movement on the compression stroke can trap that oil between the piston and cylinder head, imposing loads capable of seriously damaging the engine. The procedure described for such engines is to **pull the engine through its cycle using the propeller before starting**, checking for a possible hydraulic lock. The textbook explicitly requires confirmation that the **magnetos are OFF** before turning the propeller. Any handling and starting actions must be consistent with the applicable aircraft procedure.

### 14.3 Coring in very cold weather

**Coring** can occur during startup in very cold conditions. Thick, cold oil does not circulate readily through the engine, reducing the flow required to carry away heat. Oil around the temperature-sensor location may heat up quickly, resulting in a rapid indicated temperature rise even though **most of the system's oil remains cold**.

The textbook describes **closing the oil-cooler flaps** as a way to address this situation. Although the local temperature may rise initially, warming the oil improves its circulation—particularly through the cooler—and can subsequently bring the temperature back down.

### 14.4 Normal oil consumption

Engine oil is exposed to the combustion environment. Because sealing at pistons and valves is not perfect, a portion of the oil can enter the combustion process and be burned. Engines therefore have an **oil-consumption rate** even where there are no external leaks.

Oil consumption varies by engine. The textbook uses a figure of approximately **1 pint per hour** for a light aircraft as an illustration and notes that consumption above the expected rate can be evidence of engine wear.

### 14.5 When to check the oil quantity

The oil level must be checked **before flight**, with the timing of the check determined by the sump arrangement.

- **Dry-sump engine:** The tank level should be checked **soon after shutdown**, ideally within a few minutes, before oil has time to migrate by gravity from the tank into the engine sump. On larger engines a valve beneath the tank may close at shutdown through spring force or an electrical actuator to prevent this migration.
- **Wet-sump engine:** The opposite timing principle applies. Allow approximately **15–20 minutes or longer** after shutdown for oil to settle back into the sump before checking the level, broadly comparable to the procedure used for motor vehicles.

The correct checking interval therefore depends on where the engine stores its oil and how oil moves through the system after stopping.
