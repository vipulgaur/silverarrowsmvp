# Chapter 2: Piston Engines — General

## 1\. Engine Layout

### 1.1 Why aircraft engines use multiple cylinders

A piston engine can produce more power by using several cylinders instead of relying on one very large cylinder. This arrangement normally gives a smoother power delivery because combustion events take place at intervals throughout the engine's operating cycle. An engine's cylinder layout is selected to suit its required output, the aircraft's role and the cooling method.

The main arrangements considered here are **in-line**, **V**, **radial**, and **flat or horizontally opposed** configurations.

### 1.2 In-line engines

In an in-line engine, the cylinders form one row along the crankshaft. This layout was widely used in early aircraft and can be cooled by either air or liquid. Air-cooled in-line designs are generally limited to about **six cylinders**, since supplying cooling air evenly becomes more difficult as the row gets longer.

Some in-line engines have an **inverted installation**: the crankshaft sits above the cylinders and the pistons operate underneath. With the propeller attached to the crankshaft, this places the propeller shaft higher and increases its clearance from the ground.

### 1.3 V engines

A V engine has two banks of cylinders arranged at an angle. The configuration is especially associated with powerful **eight- to twelve-cylinder** aircraft engines, including Second World War fighters. Liquid cooling allowed the engine to fit within a comparatively streamlined fuselage, reducing external drag, although the cooling equipment added weight and mechanical complexity. V engines could also be installed inverted.

### 1.4 Radial engines

Radial cylinders are distributed around the crankshaft and act on a common crank throw. This makes the engine relatively short, strong, lightweight for its output, and capable of producing substantial power. Its drawback is the **large frontal area**, which increases drag.

The cylinders in an individual radial row are arranged in an **odd number**. Placing further rows behind the first gives **double-bank** or **triple-bank** radials, allowing much higher outputs. These multi-row, air-cooled engines nevertheless tend to become heavy and retain a large frontal profile.

### 1.5 Flat / horizontally opposed engines

Most modern light aircraft piston engines use **four or six cylinders** positioned in opposing banks. Their compact length, structural rigidity and low profile make them easier to streamline within the aircraft cowling.

## 2\. The Theoretical Otto Cycle

### 2.1 Stroke, bore and crank geometry

The **stroke** is the straight-line distance travelled by a piston between its upper and lower limits. At the upper limit the piston is at **Top Dead Centre (TDC)**, and at the lower limit it is at **Bottom Dead Centre (BDC)**. The cylinder's internal diameter is called the **bore**.

Because the piston is attached to the crankshaft through a connecting rod, one stroke between TDC and BDC corresponds to **180° of crankshaft rotation**. In a conventional four-stroke engine the complete sequence needs four piston strokes, so the crankshaft turns through **720°**, or **two revolutions**.

The crankshaft's radial offset is its **crank throw**. The stroke length equals twice the crank throw:

$$
\\text{Stroke}=2\\times\\text{Crank throw}
$$

The textbook describes the bore-equals-stroke arrangement using the term *over-square*; this terminology is retained here as presented in the source.

### 2.2 Four strokes and the changing charge

The four stages of piston travel are **induction**, **compression**, **power**, and **exhaust**. Throughout these stages, the cylinder's volume, gas pressure, temperature and valve positions change.

1. **Induction:** The inlet valve opens and the descending piston increases cylinder volume. Pressure in the cylinder falls below ambient, which causes an air-fuel charge to enter. The charge becomes cooler and its mass in the cylinder increases.
2. **Compression:** Both valves close while the piston moves upward. The trapped charge occupies less volume; its pressure and temperature rise, but the mass of charge remains essentially constant.
3. **Power:** The charge is ignited near TDC. As combustion takes place, temperature and pressure rise rapidly, and the expanding gas forces the piston down. The piston and crankshaft convert this gas energy into mechanical work. During expansion the volume increases and cylinder pressure eventually falls.
4. **Exhaust:** The exhaust valve is opened and the upward-moving piston pushes the products of combustion out of the cylinder. The gas is released to atmospheric pressure through the exhaust system.

### 2.3 The four strokes and the five events

The **theoretical Otto cycle** distinguishes the four piston strokes from the five events taking place in the cylinder. The five events are:

1. Induction
2. Compression
3. **Combustion**
4. Power
5. Exhaust

In the idealized description, an electrical spark fires at **TDC at the end of compression**. It initiates a controlled flame through the mixture rather than an instantaneous explosion. The idealized combustion event occurs while cylinder volume is unchanged, hence **constant-volume combustion**.

A valve-timing diagram relates these events to crankshaft angles. Each segment of crank movement between TDC and BDC represents **180°**.

## 3\. The Practical Otto Cycle

Actual engines cannot obtain the best cylinder filling, burning, expansion or exhaust scavenging if their valves open and close only at the exact theoretical dead-centre positions. **Practical timing** therefore advances or delays several valve and ignition events to account for inertia, finite burning time and the behaviour of moving gases.

### 3.1 Induction stroke: early inlet opening and late inlet closing

The inlet valve begins opening **before TDC**, so it is well open when the descending piston starts its induction movement. This avoids a delay between piston motion and incoming mixture flow, which would otherwise arise from the charge's inertia.

As the inlet flow accelerates it develops momentum. Near BDC it is moving quickly enough to keep entering the cylinder briefly even after the piston has started back upward. Leaving the valve open **after BDC** allows this flow to continue until cylinder and induction-manifold pressures have become approximately equal. The result is better cylinder filling.

### 3.2 Compression stroke and heating

When the inlet valve finally closes, upward piston travel compresses the enclosed charge. Its volume decreases while pressure and temperature increase. The gas is heated both by compression and by contact with surrounding hot metal surfaces; accordingly, actual pressure is higher than a calculation based on volume reduction alone would suggest.

An **adiabatic** process involves **no heat transfer into or out of the gas**. It is a useful idealization, although the textbook notes that real compression and expansion do not take place with perfectly zero heat exchange.

### 3.3 Power stroke: ignition advance and exhaust lead

The spark occurs **before compression TDC**. Combustion takes a finite time to spread through the charge, while the rotating assembly carries the piston beyond TDC. As combustion finishes, cylinder pressure reaches its maximum at approximately **10° after TDC**.

Following this peak, expanding gases give up energy to the piston. The source states that pressure has fallen to approximately **half its peak by 45° after TDC**, while by about **90° after TDC** much of the useful energy has already been transferred into mechanical work.

The exhaust valve is opened **before BDC**. Residual gas pressure then starts pushing the burned charge out, reducing the resistance that would otherwise oppose the subsequent upward exhaust stroke. Although releasing pressurized gas early might appear to waste some energy, the loss is small because little piston travel remains and the expanded gases are already producing comparatively low force.

### 3.4 Exhaust stroke and late closing

During the upward exhaust movement the piston displaces spent gases into the exhaust pipe. The exhaust valve stays open beyond TDC so the **momentum of the outgoing gas** can continue scavenging the cylinder and help improve the charge available for the next cycle.

### 3.5 Ineffective crank angles

At positions very near TDC and BDC, quite a large movement of the crankshaft produces only a **small change in piston position**. These regions are termed **ineffective crank angles**. Because cylinder volume changes little near dead centre, it is advantageous to begin opening valves early and close them later, improving induction and exhaust gas exchange.

### 3.6 Valve lead, lag and overlap

* **Valve lead** occurs when opening starts earlier than the ideal opening point. The inlet opens before TDC, and the exhaust opens before BDC.
* **Valve lag** occurs when closing is delayed beyond the ideal point. The inlet remains open after BDC, and the exhaust remains open after TDC.
* **Valve overlap** is the interval around TDC during which **both valves are partly open together**. Outflowing exhaust gas can reduce pressure in the cylinder below induction-manifold pressure; fresh charge then enters and helps remove the remaining burned gas. This improves **volumetric efficiency** and therefore cylinder filling.

For a particular engine design, these valve events occur at **fixed crankshaft angles**. Their angular timing does **not change with engine RPM**. The pilot varies the amount of charge, and thus combustion pressure and engine power, primarily by changing **throttle opening** to control incoming air.

## 4\. Engine Indicator Diagrams and Power Measurement

### 4.1 Pressure–volume (PV) indicator diagram

A research or test engine may be equipped with an **indicator system** that records cylinder pressure throughout piston travel. A pressure pick-up in the combustion chamber sends the pressure signal to a tracing mechanism; the resulting graph of cylinder pressure against cylinder volume or piston position is an **indicator diagram**, also called a **PV diagram**.

The diagram records the maximum cylinder pressures and the pressure changes during the engine cycle. The area enclosed by the plotted cycle indicates the **net work done** and can therefore be used when assessing the engine's developed power.

### 4.2 Indicated Mean Effective Pressure (IMEP)

The pressure variation can also be plotted as a sequence across **compression, power, exhaust and induction strokes**. The area associated with expansion represents the work delivered to the piston. Work absorbed during charge compression and gas expulsion must be deducted to determine the net effective work.

The uniform, notional pressure that would generate the same net indicated work over a working cycle is **Indicated Mean Effective Pressure (IMEP)**. It is an **average effective pressure**, not the maximum pressure reached during combustion.

### 4.3 Manifold Absolute Pressure (MAP)

IMEP is not ordinarily shown directly to the pilot. The **manifold pressure gauge** gives a practical indication of induction-manifold pressure and thus provides useful information about the pressure conditions affecting cylinder charge and engine output. Opening the throttle increases manifold pressure; closing it reduces manifold pressure.

A **Manifold Absolute Pressure (MAP)** gauge is normally calibrated in **inches of mercury (inHg)**.

### 4.5 Indicated Horsepower (IHP)

The engine's theoretical power developed **inside its cylinders** is its **Indicated Horsepower**. The textbook uses the mnemonic **PLANE** for the following calculation in Imperial units:

$$
\\mathrm{IHP}=\\frac{P\\times L\\times A\\times N\\times E}{33{,}000}
$$

|Symbol|Meaning|Unit in this expression|
|-|-|-|
|**P**|Indicated mean effective pressure|lb/in²|
|**L**|Piston stroke|ft|
|**A**|Cylinder bore cross-sectional area|in²|
|**N**|Number of cylinders|—|
|**E**|Number of effective power strokes per minute|strokes/min|

The multiplication gives **foot-pounds of work per minute**; dividing by **33,000** converts that rate of work to horsepower. The historical horsepower definition derives from the work-rate standard used by James Watt: **33,000 ft·lb/min**, equivalent to **550 ft·lb/s**. The chapter approximates **1 hp as 750 W**.

In a four-stroke engine, an individual cylinder delivers one power stroke every **two crankshaft revolutions**, so its effective strokes per minute equal **RPM ÷ 2**.

### 4.6 Indicated, friction and brake horsepower

**Indicated Horsepower (IHP)** represents power produced by cylinder pressure. Some is required to overcome internal friction and drive the engine's moving parts; this is **Friction Horsepower (FHP)**. What remains available at the output shaft to drive the propeller is **Brake Horsepower (BHP)**:

$$
\\mathrm{BHP}=\\mathrm{IHP}-\\mathrm{FHP}
$$

## 5\. Performance Measures

### 5.1 Power-to-weight ratio (specific power output)

Power-to-weight ratio compares engine power output with engine mass or weight. It can be expressed in **kW/kg** or **hp/lb**. A higher value means greater power from a given engine weight.

The source example is an engine weighing **1,000 lb (approximately 450 kg)** and producing **250 hp (approximately 190 kW)**:

$$
\\frac{190\~\\mathrm{kW}}{450\~\\mathrm{kg}}\\approx0.42\~\\mathrm{kW/kg}
$$

$$
\\frac{250\~\\mathrm{hp}}{1{,}000\~\\mathrm{lb}}=0.25\~\\mathrm{hp/lb}
$$

### 5.2 Specific Fuel Consumption (SFC)

**Specific Fuel Consumption** measures the **weight of fuel burned for each unit of useful power produced during a defined time interval**. In the Imperial system used here, it is commonly expressed as **lb of fuel per brake horsepower-hour**.

$$
\\mathrm{SFC}=\\frac{\\text{Fuel consumed per hour}}{\\mathrm{BHP}}
$$

Burning more fuel without increasing power **raises SFC** (a less economical result). Producing the same or more power from less fuel **lowers SFC** (a more economical result). SFC reflects both engine design and the way the pilot operates the engine. The pilot cannot redesign it, but appropriate operation is essential to obtaining published performance.

### 5.3 What efficiency means

Efficiency compares useful **output** with the **input** needed to produce it. Because energy is lost in practical engines, efficiency is less than 100%.

$$
\\eta=\\frac{\\text{Useful output}}{\\text{Input}}\\times100%
$$

### 5.4 Mechanical efficiency

Mechanical efficiency expresses the proportion of indicated cylinder power left after mechanical losses:

$$
\\eta\_{\\mathrm{mechanical}}=\\frac{\\mathrm{BHP}}{\\mathrm{IHP}}\\times100%
$$

A typical figure given for a piston engine is **80–85%**.

### 5.5 Thermal efficiency

A combustion engine changes chemical energy in fuel into heat and then useful mechanical work. **Thermal efficiency** describes how successfully the released heat is converted into work:

$$
\\eta\_{\\mathrm{thermal}}=\\frac{\\text{Heat energy converted into work}}{\\text{Heat energy available in fuel}}\\times100%
$$

Correct fuel and efficient engine design help improve the result. The textbook gives approximately **25–28%** as a good thermal-efficiency value for an internal-combustion engine.

### 5.6 Volumetric efficiency

The mass of mixture available for combustion determines how much power the engine can develop. In practice, the cylinder cannot always be filled with the amount of charge that would occupy its swept volume under standard reference temperature and pressure; charge inertia, inlet resistance, temperature, exhaust back pressure and other restrictions reduce filling.

**Volumetric efficiency** compares the **actual mass of charge inducted** with the **mass that could fill the cylinder under normal reference temperature and pressure**:

$$
\\eta\_{\\mathrm{volumetric}}=\\frac{\\text{Actual charge mass inducted}}{\\text{Reference charge mass filling the cylinder}}\\times100%
$$

The textbook quotes about **75–85% maximum** for a **normally aspirated** engine, where incoming charge flow is produced by the pressure difference between ambient air and the cylinder during induction. Valve lead, lag and overlap can improve breathing; high exhaust back pressure and induction resistance can reduce it.

A **supercharger** improves filling by increasing the pressure that drives air into the cylinders; higher charge mass makes more power possible. Supercharging is developed in a later chapter.

## 6\. Compression Ratio

### 6.1 Cylinder volume definitions

The three relevant cylinder volumes are:

* **Total volume:** Space above the piston when it is at **BDC**.
* **Swept volume:** Space displaced by the piston during one complete stroke; numerically **cylinder cross-sectional area × stroke length**.
* **Clearance volume:** Small space remaining above the piston at **TDC**, constituting the combustion chamber.

Thus:

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{total}}=V\_{\\mathrm{swept}}+V\_{\\mathrm{clearance}}
$$

### 6.2 Definition and calculation

**Compression ratio** compares the total gas space at BDC with the remaining gas space at TDC:

$$
\\text{Compression ratio}=\\frac{V\_{\\mathrm{total}}}{V\_{\\mathrm{clearance}}}:1
$$

If swept volume is **1,300 cm³** and clearance volume **200 cm³**:

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{total}}=1{,}300+200=1{,}500\~\\mathrm{cm}^{3}
$$

$$
\\text{Compression ratio}=\\frac{1{,}500}{200}:1=\\boxed{7.5:1}
$$

Raising the compression ratio generally improves how effectively the fuel's energy is used and increases mean effective pressure, provided a suitable fuel is used. It also increases combustion pressures and therefore the loads imposed on the moving parts.

## 7\. Engine Construction

## 7.1 Crankcase

The **crankcase** is the main structural casing that supports the cylinders and the crankshaft's main bearings. It is commonly manufactured in **two sections** so the shaft can be installed and removed without unnecessary difficulty. It also provides mounting faces, locating features for adjoining casings and the attachment points required to secure the engine to the airframe.

A light-alloy crankcase forms a sealed space containing lubricating oil and engine moving parts. It incorporates a **vent or breather to atmosphere** so pressure cannot build up excessively inside the casing.

## 7.2 Crankshaft (cranked-shaft)

The **crankshaft** transforms the pistons' back-and-forth motion into continuous rotation, delivers torque to the propeller, and can drive engine accessories. The offset between each crankpin and the shaft's central axis is the **crank throw**; its magnitude sets the stroke.

The shaft rotates within **main journals supported by the crankcase bearings**. Each piston is linked through a **connecting rod** to a **crankpin**. A four-cylinder crankshaft may have four crank throws; in other arrangements several pistons can share a throw. In a radial engine, many rods may act on one throw; horizontally opposed layouts may connect two pistons to a common crankpin.

Oil passages bored inside the crankshaft distribute lubricating oil to the high-load **plain bearing** surfaces and may also supply oil for a **variable-pitch propeller**.

### Torsional vibration and critical RPM

Engine power pulses repeatedly twist the crankshaft. Even a well-balanced shaft must flex a little under torque and recover, so the impulses can produce **torsional oscillations**. If power pulses coincide with the shaft's natural vibration frequency at certain speeds, engine roughness and damaging stresses can occur.

Manufacturers reduce these effects with a short, supported, suitably balanced and counterweighted crankshaft. An RPM range unsuitable for prolonged operation may be designated **critical RPM**, marked with a **red arc** on the tachometer.

### 7.3 Firing interval and firing order

Each cylinder in a four-stroke engine fires **once every 720°** of crankshaft rotation. A greater number of cylinders provides more regularly spaced impulses, smoothing output torque. **Four cylinders** are generally regarded as the minimum for a reasonably smooth firing interval.

For a uniformly firing engine:

$$
\\text{Firing interval}=\\frac{720^{\\circ}}{\\text{Number of cylinders}}
$$

* **Four cylinders:** 720° ÷ 4 = **180°**.
* **Six cylinders:** 720° ÷ 6 = **120°**.

The **firing order** is the sequence in which individual cylinders develop their power strokes. It depends on crankshaft geometry and cylinder layout. For example, **1–3–4–2** is a possible four-cylinder order; the source also identifies **1–3–2–4 as a Lycoming firing order**. Consecutive numerical firing is generally avoided to distribute loading and vibration.

### 7.4 Connecting rods

Connecting rods transmit piston force into the crankshaft and thereby change reciprocating motion into crank rotation. They are often made from **high-tensile steel with an H-shaped cross-section**, combining strength against alternating compression/tension with relatively low weight.

The large bearing surrounding the crankpin is the **big end**. The opposite connection at the piston is the **small end**.

### 7.5 Pistons and gudgeon pins

The piston, usually produced from **aluminium alloy**, is a close-fitting sliding component within the cylinder. Pressure from expanding gases acts on its crown and passes through the connecting rod to the crankshaft.

Reinforced piston bosses accommodate the **gudgeon pin**, which joins the piston to the connecting rod's small end. Machined circumferential grooves carry sealing and oil-control **piston rings**.

### 7.6 Compression rings

Near the piston crown, **compression rings** limit the leakage of combustion gases into the crankcase. Some pressurized gas passes between piston and bore and forces the ring downward in its groove and radially outward against the cylinder wall, improving the seal. Because a small amount of gas may get past the first ring, a **second or sometimes third compression ring** is fitted.

### 7.7 Scraper or oil-control rings

**Scraper rings (oil-control rings)** distribute the cylinder's lubricating oil and reduce the amount carried into the combustion chamber. Their small contact area creates a relatively high bearing pressure and aids oil control.

Piston rings are often made from a special grade of **cast iron**. This material retains spring action at elevated temperatures and its graphite content contributes useful self-lubrication, particularly during the power stroke when hot gases may burn away the thin oil film.

**Worn rings or rings stuck in their grooves** can permit oil to enter the combustion space, producing conspicuous **blue exhaust smoke** as the oil burns.

### 7.8 Cylinder barrel or block

The **cylinder barrel** is generally made of **alloy steel**. It provides a durable sliding surface for the piston and withstands high internal combustion pressure. The barrel is usually attached to the crankcase using studs and nuts. The piston closes the lower moving end; the cylinder head seals the upper end.

Cylinder surfaces must also reject substantial heat: the source gives about **30% of combustion heat** as being transferred to the cylinder structure. Two cooling arrangements are described:

* **Liquid-cooled cylinder:** coolant flows through a surrounding jacket and carries heat away.
* **Air-cooled cylinder:** external fins increase contact area so air can remove heat directly.

### 7.9 Cylinder head, valve guides and seats

The **cylinder head** closes the upper end of the barrel, creating the combustion chamber. It is generally constructed from **aluminium alloy** to help dissipate heat. The cylinder head carries the inlet/exhaust valves, their **guides and seats**, spark plugs, and supports the **rocker arms**. It may be removable, but more commonly is **screwed and shrunk onto** the cylinder barrel.

* **Valve guide:** Pressed into the head, guides the valve along a straight line and keeps it centered on its seat.
* **Valve seat:** Machined surface contacting the valve face to produce a gas-tight closure. The chapter cites seat angles of **30° or 45°**.
* **Valve:** Controls the induction and exhaust openings. Valve faces are machined to the same angle as their seats and are **lapped** to obtain continuous contact. Valves may have flat, trumpet or mushroom forms. Some exhaust valves have **hollow stems partly filled with sodium** to help with cooling.

### 7.10 Valve springs and valve bounce

Helical **spring-steel valve springs** keep the valve seated except when cam action opens it. A pair of concentric springs is commonly used on each valve, providing an element of redundancy and helping suppress **valve bounce**. Springs are compressed between the head and a cap on the valve stem; **split collets** retain the cap in position.

### 7.11 Valve operating gear and camshaft speed

The **camshaft** is turned from the crankshaft, at **half crankshaft rotational speed**, whatever the number or disposition of cylinders. A particular cam lobe controls each valve's opening. Because a four-stroke cycle occupies two crankshaft revolutions, each valve needs one complete operating sequence during those revolutions, requiring one revolution of the camshaft.

Cam-lobe positions are fixed, so **valve lead, lag and overlap** remain unchanged in crankshaft degrees as engine speed changes. Valve timing is therefore quoted in **crankshaft angles**, not camshaft angles.

### 7.12 Tappet or valve clearance

The operating gear must allow valve components to expand during engine warming without holding a valve open. A small **tappet clearance** is measured between the **rocker pad** and the **valve tip**, usually with a **feeler gauge**, and can be adjusted at the rocker mechanism.

* **Too much clearance:** The valve begins opening **late** and finishes closing **early**.
* **Too little clearance:** Opening becomes **early** and closing **late**; the valve might fail to seat fully, permitting **popping back into the carburettor**. A sticking inlet valve can cause a similar effect.

A **hydraulic tappet** provides automatic adjustment and operates without deliberate running clearance, eliminating tappet noise. It consists of two telescoping elements; pressurized engine oil makes it lengthen to remove any play.

### 7.13 The sump

Mounted under the crankcase, the **sump** receives lubricating oil after it circulates through engine components. In a system that stores all its oil in this lower casing, the sump is also the **oil reservoir**. A sump filter catches debris and helps prevent damage to the oil pumps.

### 7.14 Carburettor, fuel injection, induction and exhaust

The **carburettor** measures incoming airflow and adds finely dispersed fuel in the quantity appropriate for engine running conditions. An aircraft engine needs an appropriate fuel–air mixture despite changes in altitude or aircraft attitude.

An engine may instead use a **fuel injection** arrangement. The **induction manifold** consists of the passages or metal pipes that route the prepared charge or air from the induction system to the cylinders.

The **exhaust system** carries burned gases from each cylinder into heat-resistant steel pipes. Individual branches commonly combine into **one or two main outlet pipes** that discharge hot gases outside the aircraft.

### 7.15 Accessory housing or wheelcase

The **accessory housing**, often bolted to the rear of the crankcase, encloses the engine-driven gear train and provides mounting pads for equipment needed to run the engine or supply aircraft services. Gear drives from the crankshaft can operate **oil pumps, fuel pumps, superchargers and magnetos**. A **starter motor** can also be attached to turn the crankshaft for starting.

Accessories driven from this housing may include **electrical generators**, and systems supplying **hydraulic** or **pneumatic** power.

### 7.16 Direct-drive and reduction-geared propellers

Engines work efficiently at their own suitable crankshaft RPM, which may be too fast for the most effective propeller operation. A **reduction gearbox**, installed between crankshaft and propeller, lets the engine rotate quickly while turning the propeller more slowly. The types cited are **spur gearing** and **planetary gearing**.

Many less powerful engines attach the propeller **directly to the crankshaft** and are therefore called **direct-drive engines**.

An engine may be classified according to its cylinder arrangement, drive method, rotation direction, displacement, cooling, fuel-delivery system and whether it is **supercharged** or **normally aspirated**.
