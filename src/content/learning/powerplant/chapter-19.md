# Chapter 19: Gas Turbines — Lubrication

## The Reasons for Lubrication

Lubricating oil performs several essential functions within a gas turbine engine. **Reducing friction** between moving surfaces is only one of them.

Even when an engine is maintained to a high standard of cleanliness, small amounts of contamination may enter its internal passages. If left circulating, this material can damage bearings or obstruct narrow oilways. The lubricant collects impurities and carries them to the filters, where they are retained until the filter is serviced or replaced.

Most engine bearings contain steel components, which are susceptible to corrosion. The continuous oil film protects these surfaces from oxidation as well as reducing direct contact between moving parts.

**Heat removal** is another important duty. Bearings near the hotter engine sections work under substantial, continuous mechanical loads. Circulating oil takes heat away from these bearings, helping them operate within acceptable temperatures while simultaneously cleaning and protecting them.

Lubricating oil may also act as a **hydraulic working fluid**. In many turboprop installations, some engine oil is routed to the propeller pitch-change mechanism to alter blade pitch.

The principal functions of engine oil are therefore to:

- Minimize friction and wear.
- Carry contamination to the filtration system.
- Protect internal steel surfaces against corrosion.
- Remove heat from bearings and associated components.
- Supply hydraulic pressure for certain engine or propeller functions.

## Lubricating Systems

Most gas turbine engines employ a **self-contained, recirculating oil system**. Oil is delivered to the components that need it, collected after use, and pumped back to a reservoir so it can circulate again.

Two principal arrangements are used:

1. **Pressure relief valve system**, which regulates supply pressure by opening a spring-loaded relief valve.
2. **Full flow system**, in which the pressure pump feeds the lubricating jets directly without a pressure relief valve regulating that supply.

Because turbine installations operate at high temperatures, they normally use **synthetic lubricants**. Their operating and maintenance requirements differ from those of piston-engine mineral oils. Oil quantity is checked immediately after the engine has been shut down. The lubricant is generally not replaced at fixed intervals in the same way as piston-engine oil: synthetic oil resists the thermal deterioration and oxidation associated with mineral oil, while turbine-engine air seals mean that some oil is consumed during operation.

The filters nevertheless require periodic attention. They are removed, cleaned and refitted at specified intervals, and the material found in them is examined for signs of internal wear.

## The Pressure Relief Valve Lubrication System

A pressure-relief arrangement controls the oil delivered to bearing chambers by means of a **spring-loaded valve** in the supply system. The valve begins to open when oil pressure reaches a selected value determined by the engine manufacturer.

That opening pressure is reached by the feed pump at approximately engine idle. As engine speed or oil temperature varies, the valve regulates the supply so the bearing feed pressure remains substantially constant across the intended operating range. Excess oil can be returned instead of being driven through the bearing feed jets.

### Oil supply and distribution

The route shown for the turboprop installation is as follows:

1. Oil leaves the reservoir through a **suction filter**, which keeps large particles from reaching and damaging the pump.
2. The **oil pressure pump** draws oil into the delivery circuit.
3. A finer **pressure filter** removes material that could interfere with downstream components.
4. The **pressure relief valve** maintains the selected pressure at the bearing-chamber oil jets.
5. Oil travels through drilled internal passages and external pipes to the points requiring lubrication.

In this installation, the hollow compressor/turbine shaft forms part of the oil-transfer route. Oil passes from the forward engine section, where it is also used for propeller pitch control, along the shaft to the rear turbine bearings.

### Torquemeter oil pressure

The **torquemeter pump** is a separate pressure-boosting function shown in this system. In some turboprop engines, torquemeter oil pressure can reach **600 psi**. This oil pressure counterbalances the axial force generated by the helical teeth in the propeller reduction gears.

Since that balancing pressure varies with the torque being delivered to the propeller, measuring it provides an indication of **propeller torque**. Reference data accounting for ambient pressure and temperature allow the pilot to determine the minimum torque pressure that should be available in the prevailing conditions.

### Oil return and air removal

After passing through the engine, the oil drains into collecting spaces connected to the **scavenge pumps**. The feed and scavenge pumps are housed in a common pump pack. One pressure pump may be accompanied by several scavenge pumps, which keep the bearing compartments emptied and maintain a **dry-sump** arrangement.

Scavenge pumps move the hot return oil to a cooler. In the illustrated engine this is an **air-cooled oil cooler**, although other engines may use a different cooling method. The cooler removes heat absorbed as the oil travelled through the engine.

The returning oil then reaches a **de-aerator tray**, where trapped air separates from the liquid before the oil flows back into its tank. In the installation illustrated, the tank surrounds the engine intake.

Air may enter the lubrication circuit through seals and through de-aeration, and it must be vented so that pressure cannot accumulate. Simply discharging the air directly to atmosphere would also discard oil mist. Instead, the air-and-oil mixture passes through a **centrifugal breather** in the accessory gearbox, which separates the mist and limits oil loss.

## The Full Flow Lubrication System

A **full flow** arrangement supplies oil directly from the pressure pump to the feed jets without using a relief valve to hold the delivery at one regulated pressure.

This reduces the total quantity of oil that has to be circulated compared with a relief-valve installation. In a relief-valve system, a large amount of pump output can be returned to the tank at high engine speeds; avoiding that bypass flow allows **smaller pressure and scavenge pumps** to perform the required work.

Oil is drawn from the tank through a suction filter, pressurized by the pump and passed through a fine pressure filter to the **distribution galleries**. A **differential-pressure switch** across the pressure filter detects the pressure difference associated with obstruction. An indication may be provided at a ground-servicing panel and, on some installations, repeated on the flight deck.

One distribution gallery feeds an **oil pressure transmitter** and a **low oil pressure warning switch**, which provide cockpit indications of system malfunction. The same gallery supplies the bearings of the accessory drive gearbox. A second gallery carries oil to the bearings supporting the compressor spools.

Oil quantity and oil temperature are also monitored in the cockpit. In the system described, **oil temperature is sensed downstream of the oil cooler**.

The oil nozzles are mounted close to their corresponding bearings. This limits the possibility of local air turbulence deflecting oil away from the intended surfaces. **Thread-type filters** are installed just ahead of the nozzles as a final barrier against any particles that have passed the principal pressure filter.

After lubrication, oil is collected and returned through scavenge pumps. On its way to each scavenge pump, the oil passes a **magnetic chip detector** and a **suction/scavenge filter**.

In the illustrated arrangement, return oil can be cooled by both a **fuel-cooled** and an **air-cooled oil cooler**. The fuel-cooled unit normally meets the cooling demand on its own. If it becomes insufficient, an automatic valve brings the air-cooled unit into use as well.

## The Oil Tank

An engine oil reservoir is usually a **separate tank mounted to the engine side**, but it may instead be incorporated into the intake structure or built into the external gearbox.

For a separate reservoir, the arrangement must provide:

- A method of **gravity filling** and, more commonly, **pressure filling**.
- An indication of oil quantity using a **sight glass**, **dipstick**, or both.
- A **de-aerator tray** that allows air carried in the returning oil to escape before the oil collects in the tank.

## Oil Pumps

The usual oil-transfer device in a recirculating gas-turbine lubrication system is a **gear-type pump**. It has two intermeshing steel gears turning inside a closely fitted aluminium casing.

Rotation of the gear teeth creates an inlet flow and carries oil around the outer edges of the gear chamber. The oil is then discharged through the outlet as the teeth return into mesh. The same general pump design can supply oil under pressure or remove used oil from engine compartments.

Both the **pressure pump** and the **scavenge pumps** are mounted on the accessory housing. A pump pack commonly contains one pressure pump and may contain **up to six scavenge pumps**.

Reliable pump operation is essential. If the oil pump fails, **the engine must be shut down immediately**. To prioritize continued oil supply, the pump-pack drive shaft is **not provided with a shear neck**; the drive is intended to keep working for as long as possible, even where damage occurs.

## Oil Coolers

Oil heat exchangers may transfer heat to **air**, to **fuel**, or to both on engines that use a combined system. With two coolers, electronic oil-temperature monitoring can automatically introduce additional air cooling only when it is necessary. This supports a favourable oil operating temperature and engine thermal efficiency.

The basic cooler operates as a **radiator or heat exchanger**. Oil flows through a matrix containing baffles that lengthen its path. This increases the opportunity for heat transfer to the other medium, such as the fuel passing through tubes in the matrix.

### Fuel-cooled oil cooler

Fuel drawn from wing tanks can be extremely cold and benefits from warming before it reaches the fuel filter. At the same time, engine oil must lose heat. The **fuel-cooled oil cooler** achieves both tasks by transferring heat from the oil to the fuel.

An **oil bypass valve** joins the cooler's inlet and outlet sides. If inlet pressure reaches a predetermined level, the valve allows oil to avoid the matrix and flow directly to the outlet. This protects oil supply if the matrix becomes obstructed and protects the comparatively delicate matrix from excessive pressure caused by oil that is very viscous when cold.

A separate **pressure-maintaining valve** keeps the **oil pressure higher than fuel pressure**. If the heat exchanger's separating surfaces are damaged, the resulting pressure difference therefore favours **oil leaking into the fuel circuit**, rather than fuel entering and contaminating the lubrication system.

## Magnetic Chip Detectors

**Magnetic chip detectors** are inserted into the scavenge lines to capture **ferrous metallic particles** carried in oil returning from the engine. Their purpose is to provide evidence of wear or developing failure in the associated bearing chamber.

A detector is held in its pipeline housing by a **bayonet attachment**. The housing incorporates a **self-sealing valve**, so the detector can be withdrawn for inspection without draining oil from the system.

## The Centrifugal Breather and Vent

The gearbox and bearing housings must be **vented to atmosphere** to prevent the accumulation of excessive internal air pressure. Their vent flow contains suspended droplets of oil, however, and releasing the mist untreated would cause significant lubricant loss.

The oil-laden air is therefore routed through the **centrifugal breather** in the gearbox before the air is discharged.

The breather rotates at high speed. Its centrifugal action forces the heavier oil droplets outward toward **de-aerator segments** around the inside of its outer wall. The recovered oil is flung back into the gearbox and removed by a scavenge pump.

Air has less inertia than the droplets and can pass out through the centre of the rotating separator to atmosphere. Separating the liquid in this way **reduces the amount of lubricating oil lost through the vent**.

## Filters

The lubrication circuit contains several kinds of **filters and strainers**, each placed to stop foreign material being carried repeatedly through the engine.

- **Suction filter:** Located before the pressure pump. This relatively coarse strainer stops larger debris from entering and damaging the pump.
- **Pressure filter:** Installed downstream of the pressure pump. Its fine mesh catches small particles that could obstruct the bearing feed jets.
- **Thread-type filter:** Mounted directly ahead of individual oil jets as a final, or *last-chance*, filtering stage.
- **Scavenge filter:** Fitted to each return line, immediately downstream of the magnetic chip detector, to collect debris coming back from the lubricated component.

Pressure and scavenge filters commonly have a **tubular construction**. Their filtering material may be extremely fine woven wire or a **resin-impregnated fibre** structure.

Some filters have a **differential-pressure switch** monitoring the pressure across the element. Others provide a **pop-up indicator**: a small button becomes visible above the filter casing when blockage reaches a level requiring attention.

## Types of Lubricating Oils

A suitable gas-turbine lubricant needs enough viscosity to maintain a protective, load-carrying film, but it must also remain fluid enough to circulate during a **cold start after prolonged cold soaking**.

Early turbine engines used **mineral-based oils** similar to those of petrol piston engines. The higher operating speeds and temperatures of gas turbines caused these lubricants to burn, leave deposits and oxidize. **Synthetic oils** were developed to provide the required performance over a much wider operating range.

Desirable properties include:

1. **Low volatility:** Limits evaporation, particularly under high-altitude conditions.
2. **High flash point:** The oil's vapour requires a relatively high temperature before it can ignite when exposed to a flame.
3. **High film strength:** The lubricant molecules remain together under compressive loading and adhere to surfaces despite centrifugal forces.
4. **Wide useful temperature range:** A typical gas-turbine oil operating range is approximately **−45 °C to +115 °C**.
5. **Low viscosity:** Permits satisfactory circulation when the lubricant is cold.
6. **High viscosity index:** Indicates that the oil retains a more consistent viscosity as its temperature increases toward normal operating levels.

The comparatively low viscosity used in gas turbines is practical because these engines lack the reciprocating assemblies and heavy-duty gearing encountered in many other engine arrangements.
