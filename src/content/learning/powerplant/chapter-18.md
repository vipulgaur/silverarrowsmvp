# Chapter 18: Gas Turbines — The Exhaust System

## 1. The Jet Pipe

The exhaust system is an important contributor to the performance of a gas-turbine engine. After passing through the turbine, the gases must be guided out of the engine in the required direction and at an appropriate velocity. In a **turbojet**, the speed and direction of this discharged gas strongly influence the thrust produced. In a **turboprop**, the arrangement of the outlet nozzle has a substantial influence on turbine back pressure and the temperature of the gas within the turbine.

The exhaust assembly must also withstand very high temperatures:

- **550–850°C:** Possible temperature range of gases entering the exhaust system.
- **Up to 1,500°C:** Possible temperature when **reheat (afterburning)** is used.

When the jet pipe passes through an aircraft fuselage, the adjacent structure must be shielded from this heat. Protection is provided by leaving a space between the pipe and the surrounding aircraft skin so that cooling air can circulate, and by insulating the pipe. One insulating arrangement uses a fibrous material enclosed between thin stainless-steel layers.

## 2. Jet Pipe Design

The exhaust stream emerging from the turbine travels at approximately **750–1,250 feet per second**, corresponding to about **Mach 0.5**. If gases at this speed are allowed to flow directly into a long jet pipe, turbulence develops within the duct. This disturbance wastes energy, reduces exhaust-system efficiency, and consequently reduces engine thrust.

The jet pipe is therefore arranged to condition the gas flow before final acceleration at the outlet.

### 2.1 Divergent Passage Immediately Behind the Turbine

A basic non-reheat jet-pipe assembly may appear to narrow when viewed from outside. However, its effective internal gas passage is initially **divergent** at the turbine exit. This internal shape is achieved by fitting an **exhaust cone** close to the back face of the rear turbine disc.

The exhaust cone performs two functions:

1. It expands the available flow passage behind the turbine, allowing the gas to slow before entering the longer jet-pipe section. The reduced velocity helps limit turbulence.
2. It keeps the hot exhaust flow away from the rear face of the turbine disc, reducing flow disturbance and protecting the disc against overheating.

The **rear turbine bearing** is located inside the exhaust-cone structure and is supported by **turbine rear support struts**. Streamlined fairings around these struts reduce obstruction and help remove the residual rotational motion, or **whirl**, remaining in the gas after it passes through the turbine. Allowing this whirl to continue down the jet pipe would introduce additional losses.

### 2.2 Convergent Propelling Nozzle

Once the exhaust has passed through the jet pipe, it enters the **convergent propelling nozzle**, which raises its velocity before discharge into the atmosphere.

In a turbojet, the exhaust reaches the **local speed of sound (Mach 1)** at nearly all thrust settings above idle. At this condition the nozzle is described as **choked**.

Choking means that the gas cannot be accelerated to a higher velocity at the controlling section solely by changing the downstream conditions. The chapter associates a further rise in attainable sonic velocity with an increase in gas temperature, such as that produced by reheat.

## 4. Gas Parameter Changes and Exhaust Mach Numbers in Both a Convergent and a Convergent-Divergent Nozzle

### 4.1 Convergent Nozzle

As a subsonic exhaust stream moves along a **converging duct**, the reduction in flow area accelerates the gas. In a turbojet operating at low thrust, nozzle flow may still be subsonic. At nearly all settings above idle, however, the gas reaches its local sonic velocity, **Mach 1**, and the nozzle becomes choked.

At this limiting condition, the gas speed cannot rise further at the controlling section without an increase in the local speed of sound, which depends on temperature.

### 4.2 Convergent-Divergent Nozzle

A **convergent-divergent nozzle** has a narrowing entry, a minimum-area **throat**, and an expanding exit section.

As the gas travels through the convergent portion, its velocity increases while **static pressure falls**. The stream reaches **Mach 1** at the throat. Under the supersonic-flow conditions represented, the gas continues to accelerate through the downstream divergent portion as static pressure decreases further.

The acceleration creates an associated pressure force on the inner walls of the divergent section. The component of this force directed along the nozzle's longitudinal axis contributes additional **forward thrust**.

## 5. The Low Ratio Bypass Engine Exhaust System

A bypass engine has **two separate streams** that must eventually reach the atmosphere: the colder air that travels around the engine core and the hotter gases that have passed through the turbine.

In a **low-bypass-ratio engine**, the exhaust system commonly includes a **mixer unit**. The mixer brings the bypass air into contact with the turbine exhaust and promotes thorough mixing of the two flows **before** they leave the engine. This makes the outlet arrangement more complex than the single-stream exhaust of a pure turbojet.

## 6. The High Ratio Bypass Engine Exhaust System

A **high-bypass-ratio engine** can discharge its cold bypass air and hot core gases in different ways.

### 6.1 Separate Coaxial Exhausts

In the more conventional arrangement, the **cold and hot outlet nozzles are coaxial**—they share a common centreline but discharge through separate passages. The two streams come together **outside the engine**, where mixing occurs in the surrounding air.

### 6.2 Integrated Exhaust Nozzle

An alternative arrangement employs a **common, or integrated, exhaust nozzle**. With this design, the hot and cold streams undergo **partial mixing inside the exhaust system** before leaving the aircraft. The chapter identifies this arrangement as potentially providing greater efficiency.

## 7. Noise Suppression

Engine noise is an important concern because aircraft must comply with operational noise restrictions and noise-certification limits. The sound heard from an aircraft is produced by several sources, but the **engine** makes the principal contribution.

The main engine-related sources are:

- The **compressor**, or the **fan** in a high-bypass-ratio engine.
- The **turbine**.
- The **exhaust jet**.

Although these mechanisms produce noise in different ways, each generally becomes noisier as the relevant **relative airflow speed increases**.

### 7.1 Exhaust-Jet Noise

Reducing **exhaust velocity** has a greater effect on exhaust noise than a similar reduction in compressor or turbine speed has on their respective noise outputs. The difference in velocity between the fast-moving exhaust and the air outside the engine creates a strong **shearing action**. As the two streams meet, violent turbulence and mixing generate noise.

The turbulence is not uniform along the jet. Smaller-scale mixing occurs closer to the nozzle and is associated with **higher-frequency** sound. Larger turbulent structures form farther downstream and generate more **low-frequency** noise.

In a **pure turbojet**, exhaust noise is normally so dominant that the compressor and turbine make a comparatively insignificant contribution to the overall engine noise, except at very low thrust.

### 7.2 Bypass Engines and Changing Noise Sources

Compared with a pure turbojet, a bypass engine has a **lower-speed exhaust**, so its exhaust-jet noise is reduced. However, because the engine handles greater power, the turbine and low-pressure compressor can create higher noise levels.

With a **high-bypass-ratio engine of about 5:1**, the exhaust noise may fall enough that the **fan (low-pressure compressor)** and the **turbine** become the principal contributors to engine noise.

Once exhaust noise has been reduced, further improvements depend more heavily on limiting sound from these other engine components.

### 7.3 Acoustic Linings

**Acoustic linings** can be installed in the engine intake and bypass duct to absorb noise from the fan and associated airflow. They are particularly effective in these regions. Other materials suited to higher temperatures are used farther aft in the engine to reduce noise in hotter areas.

These noise-absorbing treatments involve some disadvantages:

- They add a small amount of **weight**.
- They have slightly greater **skin friction**.
- The combined effect can cause a small increase in **fuel consumption**.

### 7.4 Mechanical Exhaust Mixers and Lobed Nozzles

Older pure-turbojet installations could not always benefit from the same acoustic treatments used in more modern bypass-engine nacelles. Some instead employed specially shaped exhaust outlets, including **corrugated internal mixers** and **lobe-type nozzles**.

A lobed nozzle divides the hot exhaust into separate jets. The spaces and lobes help bring slower surrounding air into contact with the exhaust, encouraging rapid mixing.

**Corrugated internal mixers** were particularly effective at reducing exhaust noise. However, they also introduced performance penalties, which limited their appeal to operators.
