## Reverse Thrust

Aircraft wheel brakes can provide highly effective deceleration, particularly when equipped with anti-skid protection and other advanced braking features. However, the available wheel-braking force depends heavily on the condition of the runway. Water, ice and other conditions that reduce tyre-to-surface friction can significantly limit the effectiveness of even a well-designed braking system.

**Reverse thrust** provides another way to slow the aircraft after touchdown. By redirecting engine flow to produce a force opposing forward travel, it assists deceleration without relying on friction between the tyres and the runway. This is particularly valuable on wet or icy surfaces and during crosswind landings, when dependable deceleration and directional control are important.

Once the aircraft's weight is firmly carried by its **main wheels**, reverse thrust may be selected. Used in conjunction with **ground spoilers**, it can substantially reduce landing distance. The improvement in stopping performance can be considerable when compared with an otherwise similar landing made without reverse thrust.

The three main types of reversing system are:

1. **Clamshell doors** — doors that redirect the hot exhaust through cascade outlets.
2. **External or bucket doors** — external doors that swing into the exhaust stream and turn it forward.
3. **Blocker doors** — devices that divert the cold bypass flow in high-bypass turbofan engines.

Reverser movement is usually produced by **hydraulic or pneumatic actuators**. Depending on the design, motors and driven screwshafts may also provide the mechanical movement required to deploy and stow the reverser. All three arrangements create a braking effect by changing the direction in which the relevant engine airflow is discharged.

## Clamshell Doors

The name **clamshell** describes the appearance of the paired reversing doors: their curved shape resembles the two halves of a shell.

In this arrangement, the doors are commonly powered by a pneumatic mechanism supplied with air from the **high-pressure compressor (P3)**. Pneumatic rams move the doors between the **stowed position**, used for forward thrust, and the **deployed position**, used for reverse thrust.

When the mechanism is stowed, the doors conceal the **cascade vanes** and permit the exhaust to leave by its normal rearward route. Deployment changes both of these conditions: the doors shut off the regular exhaust exit while uncovering the cascade outlets.

The hot exhaust must then pass through the exposed cascade vanes. Their arrangement turns the discharge generally forward, producing thrust that resists the aircraft's forward motion.

The **lower cascade vanes** are designed to direct the flow not only forward but also **outward from the aircraft**. This outward component helps keep expelled hot gases and runway debris away from the engine intake, reducing the likelihood that they will be drawn back into the engine during reverse-thrust operation.

## External Door (Bucket) Reversers

**Bucket reversers** are generally operated hydraulically. Their two large external doors form an arrangement resembling the halves of a bucket without a base, mounted at the rear of the exhaust pipe.

With forward thrust selected, the doors remain positioned so that the exhaust has an unobstructed rearward path. When reverse thrust is commanded, the hinged halves rotate backwards into the discharge stream. The resulting obstruction turns the exhaust **forward**, creating a force that opposes the aircraft's movement.

Unlike a cold-stream blocker reverser, the bucket system acts on the **hot exhaust flow**.

## Cold Stream (Blocker) Reverser

The **cold-stream blocker reverser** is used on **high-bypass-ratio fanjet engines**. It differs from clamshell and bucket systems because it redirects **only the cold bypass airstream**; the other two designs use hot exhaust gas for their reversing effect.

Each engine equipped with thrust reversal has an associated **reverse-thrust lever** in the cockpit. Moving that lever initiates the reversing sequence.

In the blocker arrangement, the reverse-thrust lever controls the **direction and rotational speed of an air motor**. The motor transmits its drive through **flexible shafts**, which move the blocker doors into or out of their operating position.

As the blocker doors move, they cover or expose the **cascade vanes** and determine the route taken by the bypass air. With the reverser deployed, the normal aft passage for cold bypass air is blocked and that air is diverted through the cascades in a direction that generates reverse thrust.

## Indication and Safety Systems

The flight crew must be able to recognize whether the reverser doors are in their normal stowed position or have moved away from it. **Reverse-thrust warning lights** therefore provide a cockpit indication of reverser condition.

These warning lights are generally **amber** and are placed on the forward instrument panel where they can be seen by the crew. A light illuminates when the reverser doors are **unlocked and not in their fully stowed, forward-thrust position**. Its illumination is not restricted to the condition in which full reverse thrust has already been established.

Depending on aircraft type, additional indications may identify such conditions as **reverser operating** or **reverser deployed**.

Because inappropriate activation or a mechanical malfunction could create a serious hazard, the system incorporates operating interlocks and protective responses.

### Five Reverse-Thrust Safeguards

1. **Throttle-idle interlock:** Reverse thrust cannot be selected unless the forward-thrust throttle lever has first been returned to **idle**.
2. **Air/ground interlock:** The reverser cannot be activated until the aircraft's weight is on the **main wheels**, as detected by its air/ground logic.
3. **Deployment-before-power protection:** Engine **rpm in reverse** cannot be raised above idle until the reverser doors have reached the **deployed** position.
4. **Uncommanded deployment during forward thrust:** If the doors unexpectedly deploy while **forward thrust** is selected, the system may automatically reduce the throttle to **idle**.
5. **Uncommanded stowing during reverse selection:** If the doors unexpectedly move to the **stowed, forward-thrust position** while reverse thrust is selected, the reverse-thrust lever automatically moves to the **reverser-deploy position**.

## Restrictions of Use

There is generally **no upper airspeed restriction** governing the initial selection of reverse thrust, although some aircraft have a specified **minimum speed** for its use.

Even with the outward angle built into the lower cascade vanes of a clamshell system, the risk of **re-ingestion** remains. At low forward speed, or when the aircraft is stationary, suction at the engine intake may be strong enough to draw discharged hot gases and associated foreign material back toward the compressor despite the intended direction of the reversed exhaust.

For that reason, the standard operating procedures on some aircraft call for reverse power to be reduced to **reverse idle at approximately 60–80 knots**. The reverser is then returned to the **stowed** position at a lower speed, commonly **around 50 knots**, when there is no longer a useful reason to keep reverse idle available for an immediate return to full reverse power.

These figures describe typical restrictions for the systems concerned; the specified limits depend on the aircraft.

## Ground Manoeuvring Reverse Thrust is not Normally Used

Reverse thrust is **not normally used for manoeuvring on the ground**.

Whenever reverse thrust is in operation, engine indications need close attention, particularly for an excessive **exhaust gas temperature (EGT)**. When reverse rpm is increased, the engines should respond in a sufficiently **symmetrical** manner; unequal reverse thrust can produce **adverse yaw**.

An **inoperative reverser** may also impose an aircraft-performance limitation because the available reverse-braking capability has been reduced. On a twin-engine aircraft with engines mounted on **wing pylons**, the asymmetry from using only the operative reverser may be significant enough that operation of that remaining reverser is also prohibited.
