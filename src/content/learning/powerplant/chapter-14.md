# Chapter 14: Gas Turbines — Air Inlets

## Air Inlet

An engine intake forms part of the aircraft's structure or occupies the forward section of an engine nacelle. Its primary purpose is to deliver air to the **fan or low-pressure compressor face** with as little turbulence and flow distortion as possible. The compressor must receive an orderly supply of air across the aircraft's operating range, including changes in airspeed and angle of attack. Poor inlet airflow can upset compressor operation and lead to **compressor stall**.

### Intake arrangements

The most straightforward arrangement is the **pitot-type intake**, which has a single, generally circular entrance. Its duct shape depends on where the engine is installed:

- **Wing-mounted engines:** The inlet passage is normally comparatively straight.
- **Aft-mounted engines:** The duct may curve into an **S-shape** to direct the incoming air towards the engine. The Boeing 727 and Lockheed TriStar are examples of aircraft using this arrangement.

The curvature of an S-duct makes it more vulnerable to disturbed or unstable airflow, particularly when a crosswind is present during take-off.

A pitot intake is designed to exploit **ram effect**, recovering as much of the pressure associated with forward motion as possible. Its ram-pressure losses are relatively low as altitude increases. However, as flight speed approaches the speed of sound, a shock wave can form at the intake lip, reducing intake efficiency.

### Airflow speed and pressure

For **subsonic operation**, an inlet normally widens along the direction of flow, forming a **divergent duct**. As the incoming air travels through this increasing cross-sectional area, its velocity falls and its pressure rises before reaching the compressor. The result is a slower-moving, higher-pressure airflow at the compressor face.

The pressure inside the intake also changes with aircraft speed:

1. **Aircraft stationary, engine operating:** Air is drawn rapidly through the inlet by the engine. The resulting inlet pressure is **lower than ambient atmospheric pressure**.
2. **Aircraft beginning to move:** Forward motion contributes to the intake's pressure recovery, so inlet pressure begins to rise.
3. **Ram pressure recovery:** At the speed where pressure within the inlet has risen back to the surrounding atmospheric pressure, **ram pressure recovery** has been reached. This normally occurs at approximately **Mach 0.1–0.2**.
4. **Further acceleration:** Increasing forward speed produces additional ram compression. The higher inlet pressure raises the engine's overall compression ratio and can increase thrust **without a corresponding increase in fuel flow**.

## Operational Considerations

### Take-off

Smooth airflow reaching the compressor is especially important while the engine is being accelerated to take-off power. Significant turbulence or distortion at the compressor entry may lead to **stall or surge**.

The air inlet cannot maintain equally stable flow at every angle of attack. Crosswinds can further disturb the incoming air, with the problem particularly noticeable in **aft-mounted engines supplied through S-shaped ducts**, such as those installed on the **TriStar** and **Boeing 727**.

Crews must follow the procedure specified in the applicable aircraft operating manual. A typical precaution is a **rolling take-off**: begin moving the aircraft forward, then increase thrust smoothly so that the take-off power setting is reached by approximately **60–80 knots**. Forward movement helps establish a more satisfactory flow into the intake during engine acceleration.

### Icing

Ice accumulation at the inlet becomes possible when atmospheric conditions favour icing. Conditions identified for particular attention include:

- Ambient temperature **below +10 °C**.
- **Visible moisture** in the surrounding air.
- **Standing water** on the runway.
- **Runway visual range (RVR) below 1,000 metres**.

When the relevant icing conditions are present, the **engine anti-icing system** should be activated in accordance with the aircraft's operating requirements.

### Damage

Airflow can be disrupted not only by the overall shape of the inlet but also by damage or an uneven surface inside it. Changes in the duct's internal profile can produce turbulence before the air reaches the compressor and may contribute to **compressor stall or surge**.

Intake inspection therefore needs to pay attention to:

- Physical damage to the intake structure.
- Skin panels that are uneven or no longer properly aligned.
- Roughness or other irregularities on internal surfaces.

### Foreign Object Ingestion

Compressor blades can be damaged when loose objects are drawn into the intake, especially while the aircraft is **on or close to the ground**. Before starting an engine, the surface ahead of its inlet must be checked for **stones, loose material and other debris**.

Engine position affects exposure to foreign objects. A **wing-mounted engine** has an inlet relatively close to the ground and is consequently more exposed to debris. An **aft-fuselage-mounted engine** has its intake higher above the ground and generally suffers less foreign-object ingestion.

### In-flight Turbulence

Severe atmospheric turbulence can disturb the airflow entering the engine enough to interfere with compressor operation. The operating handbook provides a **turbulence penetration speed** and appropriate engine settings, expressed as **rpm** or **engine pressure ratio (EPR)**, to reduce the risk of compressor malfunction.

Depending on the aircraft and the conditions, selecting **continuous ignition** may be advisable or required. This lowers the likelihood of a **flameout** if turbulent airflow disrupts combustion.

### Ground Operations

A major cause of compressor damage is **foreign object damage (FOD)**. Damage to compressor blades changes their intended geometry and can result in:

- Reduced engine performance.
- Compressor stall.
- Engine surge.

Preventing FOD begins with keeping foreign material out of aircraft movement and engine-running areas. The pilot's **external pre-flight inspection** must also confirm that the intake is clear of debris.

Protection continues after a flight. **Intake and exhaust covers** should be installed as appropriate to prevent contaminants from entering the engine and to prevent **windmilling**.

During **engine start, taxi and reverse-thrust operation**, the engine can ingest nearby loose objects. Power should therefore be kept as low as practicable within the applicable operating procedure to limit the possibility of damage.

**Personnel safety** is equally critical. Operating gas turbine intakes have caused fatal and serious injuries by drawing people towards or into the engine. Anyone required to work near a running engine must exercise extreme care.
