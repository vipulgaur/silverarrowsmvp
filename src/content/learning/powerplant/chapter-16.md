# Chapter 16: Gas Turbines — Combustion Chambers

## 1. The Task of the Combustion Chamber

A gas turbine combustion chamber provides a controlled space in which **compressed air** from the compressor is mixed with fuel delivered through **fuel spray nozzles** and burned. Its job is to release as much of the fuel's available heat as possible while keeping the combustion process at **approximately constant pressure**. The result must be a steady, evenly distributed flow of hotter, expanded gas moving towards the turbine assembly.

Designing a combustor involves several competing requirements: maintaining a flame in fast-moving air, distributing heat without damaging engine components, using fuel efficiently, and restricting pollutants in the exhaust. Fuel economy has become increasingly important as fuel costs have risen. At the same time, concern about exhaust smoke and atmospheric pollution has encouraged improvements to combustion efficiency and chamber design.

## 2. The Temperature Increase Allowed

The temperature of gas leaving the combustor cannot be increased without limit. **Nozzle guide vanes and turbine blades** have material temperature restrictions, and even a brief exceedance of the permissible temperature may cause turbine components to break up, potentially with catastrophic consequences.

Consequently, the combustor must allow very high temperatures where burning occurs while delivering gas to the turbine at a temperature its components can withstand.

## 3. The Temperature Increase Required

Inside the combustion region, modern materials permit local gas temperatures **above 2,000°C**. Before the gases leave the combustion chamber, however, their temperature needs to fall to roughly **1,000–1,500°C**. Air reaching the combustor may already have been heated to approximately **600°C** during compression, so fuel must raise its temperature substantially beyond that value.

These higher temperatures relate to **full-power operation**. At reduced engine power, fuel flow and the required temperature rise are lower. The combustion chamber must therefore work dependably and burn fuel efficiently across a broad range of engine settings, rather than at only one airflow and fuel-flow combination.

## 4. The Flame Rate of Kerosene

Air enters the combustion system at a speed comparable with its velocity through the engine inlet; speeds approaching **500 ft/s** can occur. Kerosene vapour, however, has a flame propagation speed of only about **1–2 ft/s**. A flame placed directly in a 500 ft/s stream would be blown out almost immediately.

To keep the flame established, the air must be slowed between the compressor outlet and the **primary combustion zone**, where it mixes with fuel. A diffuser reduces flow velocity and increases static pressure before the air enters the chamber. The static pressure reached in this region is the highest in the engine, but the initial slowdown is not sufficient on its own: the flow entering the flame region must be slowed further and made to recirculate.

Air directed towards the primary zone passes through an inlet **snout**, then through a **perforated flare** and **swirl vanes**. These features contribute to the lower local velocities and circulating airflow needed for stable burning.

## 5. Primary Air

Approximately **20% of the total air** supplied to the combustion chamber is classed as **primary air**. This portion is associated with the fuel-mixing and burning process.

As it passes through the flare and swirl vanes, its velocity decreases and the airflow begins to recirculate. This recirculation is important because it prevents the burning mixture from being carried away from the fuel nozzle faster than the flame can propagate.

## 6. Secondary Air

Air that does not enter through the snout travels into the gap between the **flame tube** and **air casing**. A portion enters the flame tube through the **secondary-air holes**.

Secondary air represents approximately **20% of the total airflow**. It interacts with the swirling primary flow to create a **toroidal vortex**—a ring-shaped region of relatively low-velocity air, comparable in shape to a doughnut or smoke ring. The vortex holds the flame close to the nozzle, stabilizing combustion instead of allowing the flame to be swept farther down the flame tube.

Temperatures near the centre of the primary zone can reach approximately **2,000°C**, which is too high for the nozzle guide vanes and turbine blades. The gas therefore requires additional cooling before it reaches the turbine.

## 7. Tertiary Air

The remaining approximately **60% of the air** is designated **tertiary air**. It is introduced progressively into the flame tube, where it **dilutes and cools the combustion products** before they enter the turbine assembly. Tertiary airflow also provides cooling for the chamber's surrounding air-casing walls.

A chamber can therefore maintain a hot, stable flame locally while still supplying lower-temperature gas farther downstream.

## 8. The Combustion Chamber Components

An early multiple-chamber engine uses several separate flame tubes, each surrounded by its own air casing. More recent cooling arrangements can employ **transpiration cooling**: a thin film of air passes between laminated layers of the casing wall to protect the structure from heat.

### 8.1 Igniters and interconnectors

Most gas turbine engines use only **two igniters**. One functioning igniter may be enough to light an engine, but the engine still needs a way to spread combustion to chambers that have no igniter. This is the purpose of the **interconnector** between adjacent chambers.

Once the first chamber lights, its internal pressure rises relative to the neighbouring unlit chamber. Burning gas moves through the interconnector because of this pressure difference and ignites the mixture in the next chamber. The process continues from chamber to chamber until all are alight. As their pressures become equal, the pressure-driven flow through the interconnectors stops.

### 8.2 Sealing ring and thermal growth

The combustion chamber is secured to the compressor at its forward end, so expansion cannot take place freely in that direction. A **sealing ring** at the turbine end allows the hot chamber to lengthen into the **nozzle box**—the engine region immediately ahead of the nozzle guide vanes—without losing its gas-tight seal.

### 8.3 Corrugated joints

**Corrugated joints** admit tertiary airflow into the flame tube in stages. This produces a progressive reduction in gas temperature before the gases reach the nozzle guide vanes.

## 9. The Multiple Combustion Chamber System

The straight-through **multiple combustion chamber** arrangement developed from Sir Frank Whittle's original concept. It appeared in earlier axial-flow engines and has continued in centrifugal-compressor designs such as the **Rolls-Royce Dart**.

The assembly consists of **eight or more individual combustion chambers** positioned around the engine behind the compressor. Each chamber has its own flame tube and separate air casing. A comparable arrangement was fitted to the **Rolls-Royce Avon**, an axial-compressor engine widely used over many years on military and civil aircraft.

Its identifiable external features include **snouts**, **interconnectors**, a **fuel manifold** and **drain tubes** linking the chambers.

## 10. The Fuel Drain System

If fuel enters the chambers but fails to ignite during starting, the result is a **wet start**. A significant amount of unburned fuel may then be left inside the engine. Attempting another start without removing that fuel could produce a long, extremely hot and dangerous flame from the rear of the engine.

Two measures are used to remove the fuel:

1. **Drain the accumulated liquid fuel.** Drain tubes connect the lowest part of one chamber to the next lower chamber so that fuel can run down towards the bottom of the engine. At the **six-o'clock position**, a drain valve allows the fuel to leave the lowest chamber. The valve is **spring-loaded open**, but normal combustion-chamber pressure holds it **closed** while the engine runs.
2. **Clear the remaining fuel by a blow-out cycle.** The starter rotates the engine for approximately the duration of a normal full starting cycle. During this procedure, the **HP fuel cock remains closed** and the **ignition system is automatically deselected**. Compressor airflow passes through the combustion spaces and helps the traces of fuel still present in the chambers and jet pipe to evaporate.

## 11. The Tubo-annular Combustion Chamber System

A **tubo-annular** arrangement is also called **cannular** or **can-annular**. In contrast to a multiple-can system, individual flame tubes are placed together inside **one common air casing** rather than each having its own casing. This creates a more compact combustion assembly.

The common housing surrounds several flame tubes and incorporates their snouts, interconnectors and connections to the turbine inlet. The illustrated assembly also shows an **igniter plug**.

## 12. The Annular Combustion Chamber System

An **annular combustor** has **one continuous, ring-shaped flame tube** located between an **inner air casing** and an **outer air casing**. It does not contain a collection of separate can-shaped flame tubes. Air and fuel are distributed around the common annular combustion space, after which the gases flow into the turbine through the nozzle guide vanes.

### 12.1 Advantages of an annular arrangement

Relative to earlier chamber designs, the annular system offers several benefits:

1. **Reduced length:** For an equal power output and the same diameter, an annular chamber is only about **75% as long as a tubo-annular chamber**.
2. **No inter-chamber flame propagation problem:** The flame occupies one continuous combustion space.
3. **Smaller casing area:** Compared with a tubo-annular design, there is less air-casing area to cool, reducing the cooling-air requirement.
4. **Higher combustion efficiency:** Very little fuel remains unburned, allowing carbon monoxide to oxidize into **carbon dioxide**, which is described here as non-toxic.
5. **More uniform turbine loading:** The gas pressure distribution reaching the turbine is more even, reducing variations in loading around the turbine.

## 13. The Air/Fuel (Stoichiometric) Ratio

The chemically correct mixture that gives maximum heat release is **15 parts of air to 1 part of kerosene by mass**. This is the **stoichiometric ratio**, written **15:1**.

In a piston engine, operating at such a mixture can contribute to detonation and dissociation because of the associated pressure peaks. A gas turbine does not experience the same combustion-pressure peaks, so the primary zone can operate at the chemically correct mixture.

The **primary zone** burns fuel with air at approximately **15:1 by weight**. Once secondary and tertiary air have been added to the combustion products, the **overall** engine air/fuel ratio becomes much leaner, typically from approximately **45:1 to 130:1**.

## 14. Pressure Losses in the Chamber

Gas-turbine combustion is regarded as a **constant-pressure process**, although the actual chamber experiences a small pressure reduction. Airflow must be mixed and made turbulent enough to sustain good combustion, and those processes cause losses.

The pressure fall through the chamber is usually about **3–8% of the pressure at its inlet**.

## 15. Combustion Stability

After the engine reaches **self-sustaining speed**, burning continues without continuous assistance from the ignition system. Self-sustaining speed is the speed at which the engine can continue accelerating following a start **without the starter motor**.

Conditions can nevertheless arise in which ignition is required again. A **flameout** is the extinction of combustion and may follow an unusual disturbance such as ingesting large quantities of water while taking off from a contaminated runway. Combustion can also stop if the air/fuel mixture becomes excessively weak. This is particularly possible during descent with the throttle closed back, when **fuel flow is low but airflow remains relatively high**.

**Combustion stability** means that burning remains smooth and the flame stays established across a wide range of **air/fuel ratios** and **air mass flows**. As air mass flow increases, the range between the rich and weak stability limits becomes narrower. If the operating condition passes beyond the stable region, the flame goes out.

The **ignition loop** lies within the wider stable-burning region. This shows that **establishing a new flame is more difficult than sustaining an existing one**. If a flameout occurs at considerable airspeed or altitude, reducing one or both may be necessary to permit relighting.

## 16. Relighting

Whether an engine can be restarted during flight depends on the aircraft's **altitude and forward airspeed**. These limits are represented by a **relight envelope**, defining the combination of conditions at which an in-flight relight is expected to be successful for a serviceable engine.

Forward airflow causes the engine to **windmill**, rotating the compressor so that it continues to deliver air. With this airflow available, fuel is admitted by opening the **HP fuel cock** and the ignition system is energized.

The pilot selects a dedicated **relight switch**, whose operation is separate from the normal engine-start circuit.

## 17. Combustion Efficiency

**Combustion efficiency** measures how successfully the combustor releases the heat energy available in the fuel. Modern gas turbine combustors are highly effective at converting the fuel's potential heat into the combustion process.

At high power, efficiency can reach approximately **99%**. Even at **idle**, it can remain around **95%**. Over the engine's normal operating range, these high efficiencies are obtained despite substantial changes in the **overall air/fuel ratio**.

## 18. Fuel Spray Nozzles

The high efficiency of modern combustion chambers depends in part on the way fuel is delivered. A fuel nozzle must **atomize** the liquid into very small droplets or **vaporize** it so that the fuel can burn almost completely. This must happen within the limited length of the combustor and despite the high speed of the compressor airflow.

Fuel pressure presents an additional difficulty during starting. The engine-driven **high-pressure fuel pump** is connected to the high-speed gearbox, but the gearbox is turning slowly during the early stages of a start. The pump therefore cannot yet provide the approximately **1,500–2,000 psi** generally required for an effective spray through a fixed-size orifice.

A fixed-orifice spray changes considerably as fuel pressure increases:

- **Low pressure:** The fuel tends to remain in a continuous liquid film or **bubble**.
- **Intermediate pressure:** The outer edges of the film separate, producing a **tulip-shaped** flow pattern.
- **High pressure:** The liquid film becomes shorter and breaks into a fine, well-atomized spray.

Because a simple fixed orifice does not atomize effectively at starting pressures, other nozzle arrangements are required.

## 19. The Airspray System

An **airspray nozzle** uses **high-velocity air** to break up the fuel stream instead of relying entirely on high fuel pressure. As satisfactory atomization is possible at relatively low fuel pressure, the system can be supplied by a **gear-type fuel pump**. Such a pump is lighter than the more complex **plunger-type pump**.

## 20. The Duplex System

A **duplex spray nozzle** provides the effect of a variable-sized fuel outlet by separating fuel delivery into a **primary circuit** and a **main circuit**.

At low pressure, a **pressurizing valve** prevents fuel from reaching the main discharge passage. Fuel instead flows through the **primary line** to a small **primary orifice** capable of producing a fine spray at low pressure. As the engine accelerates during the start and pump pressure rises, the pressurizing valve opens. Fuel can then also pass through the **main orifice**, increasing the delivery in addition to the primary flow.

## 21. The Vaporizing Tube System

In a **vaporizing-tube** combustor, fuel leaves its feed tubes and enters **vaporizing tubes located inside the flame tube**. Primary air enters through the fuel-feed-tube opening and through additional holes close to the flame-tube entrance.

Within the vaporizing tubes, the fuel flow turns through **180°**. Heat transferred from the surrounding combustion process causes the fuel to vaporize inside the tubes before it is discharged into the flame tube for burning.
