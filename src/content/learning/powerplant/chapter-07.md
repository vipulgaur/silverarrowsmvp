# Chapter 7: Piston Engines — Mixture

## 1. The Chemically Correct Ratio

A spark-ignition piston engine can burn an air–fuel charge over a relatively broad range of mixture strengths. At the rich end, combustion is possible at approximately **8 parts air to 1 part fuel by weight**; at the weak (lean) end, it may still occur at about **20:1**.

The **chemically correct air–fuel ratio is 15:1 by weight**. At that proportion, the oxygen supplied by the air is sufficient to combine with the hydrogen and carbon in the fuel without either ingredient being left unused in the ideal combustion reaction.

Chemically correct does **not** necessarily mean most suitable for running the engine. Combustion temperatures at this ratio can be sufficiently high to encourage **detonation**, with a resulting loss of useful power. The mixture used in service must therefore account for both combustion efficiency and operating temperature.

## 2. The Practical Mixture Ratio

A 15:1 mixture should, in theory, produce a particularly high combustion temperature and power output. In an operating engine, however, fuel and air are not distributed with perfect uniformity. One portion of a charge—or one cylinder compared with another—may receive more fuel than intended, while another is relatively weak.

For maximum-power operation, making the overall mixture **slightly rich** is preferable to risking an excessively weak mixture in an individual cylinder:

- With **moderate richness**, the oxygen can still be used up in combustion. The remaining fuel has little additional power-producing value, and the extra fuel can slightly reduce effective **volumetric efficiency**. Its cooling action, however, can be beneficial.
- With a **weak mixture**, some of the oxygen inducted into the engine is not used. Power then falls considerably more rapidly than it does with a similarly small departure toward the rich side.

Consequently, an engine operated for high power rather than minimum fuel consumption may use an overall air–fuel ratio of approximately **12.5:1**. This reduces the likelihood that uneven mixture distribution will leave a cylinder so weak that its contribution to total engine power becomes markedly reduced.

## 3. Problems Caused by Weak Mixtures

As the mixture becomes weaker than 15:1, the greater proportion of nitrogen in the cylinder contributes to **slower combustion** and a **lower burning temperature**. Engine power decreases because less fuel energy is released in each charge.

Fuel consumption may nevertheless decrease by a greater proportion than power. Under the operating conditions described here, this improves fuel economy by reducing **specific fuel consumption (SFC)** as the mixture is weakened below the chemically correct value.

For a moderate-power, economical cruise, a ratio of around **18:1** may be used. Because the charge burns more slowly at this setting, **earlier ignition timing** may be required to give combustion sufficient time to develop.

Excessive leaning is harmful. Combustion can continue so late in the cycle that the mixture is still burning when the **exhaust valve opens**. The valve may then be subjected to excessive heat, risking **distortion or cracking**. Heat remaining in the cylinder as the **inlet valve opens** can also ignite the incoming mixture within the induction system. This produces the condition described as **popping back** through the induction manifold.

Late combustion can additionally overheat the engine because some heat is released too late to be turned into mechanical work by expansion. The **cooling system** must dispose of that unused heat.

The correct operating mixture therefore varies with **engine speed and the power demanded**.

## 4. Slow Running and Starting

**Starting and low-speed running require a rich mixture** for two reasons.

### 4.1 Limited Fuel Vaporisation in a Cold Engine

Fuel must be present as vapour and mixed with air before it can burn. When the engine is cold, there is not enough heat to vaporise much of the fuel, so primarily its **lighter fractions** form a combustible vapour. Supplying a rich mixture makes enough vapour available inside the cylinders for ignition and continued operation.

**White exhaust smoke or vapour** may be visible during this period. Moisture can condense in the exhaust because water is a combustion product, while cold, damp intake air can contribute to the effect. The white appearance normally fades as the engine warms to its usual operating temperature.

### 4.2 Dilution by Exhaust Gases at Low Engine Speed

The exhaust valve is designed to remain open for a short period after its theoretical closing point (**valve lag**). At normal engine speeds, the momentum of the outgoing gases improves the clearing of burnt gas and helps the next charge enter.

At low speeds, this movement is less energetic. A greater amount of exhaust gas remains in the cylinder; at very low speed, the descending piston may even draw burnt gas back into the cylinder before the exhaust valve closes. Because the fresh charge is then diluted, **additional fuel richness is needed to maintain smooth combustion and steady running**.

## 5. Take-off Power

A take-off setting using full engine power calls for a mixture enriched to approximately **10:1 (air:fuel by weight)**.

The extra fuel absorbs heat as it vaporises and helps limit combustion temperature. It does not all burn, however: insufficient oxygen is available to combine with it completely. Consequently, fuel supplied beyond that needed for combustion has a cooling function rather than an additional direct power-producing function.

The high power at take-off arises mainly because **a greater mass of charge enters the cylinders per unit time**, not simply because the mixture has been made richer.

Incomplete combustion of a rich charge can produce **carbon monoxide (CO)** alongside **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**. Under very rich conditions, some carbon may remain uncombined with oxygen and pass into the exhaust as **black smoke**.

## 6. Climbing Power

Engine power depends on both **rotational speed** and the **mean effective pressure** acting in the cylinders during the operating cycle. Raising power generally increases both, while also raising charge temperature and the risk of detonation.

For the higher-power conditions associated with climbing, the mixture may be enriched to approximately **11:1**. Vaporisation of the additional fuel cools the charge and makes detonation less likely.

## 7. Cruise Power

Cruise normally requires less engine power than take-off or climb. The reduced power demand allows the mixture to be weakened for better fuel economy. An **air–fuel ratio near 18:1** is identified for economical cruise operation, reducing fuel use for the required moderate power.

## 8. The Exhaust Gas Temperature Gauge

The mixture can be adjusted by observing how engine indications change as the fuel supply is leaned:

- Moving from **fully rich** toward the chemically correct mixture generally increases the heat released in combustion.
- On a **fixed-pitch propeller** installation, increasing useful power may be noticed as a rise in **engine rpm**; **airspeed** may also increase.
- A more precise indication is the change in **exhaust gas temperature (EGT)**.

### 8.1 EGT Indication

The EGT system uses a **thermocouple** installed in the exhaust pipe of the hottest cylinder. The thermocouple produces a voltage related directly to temperature, and the connected instrument converts that voltage into a temperature indication.

### 8.2 Mixture Adjustment Using EGT

Move the mixture control **slowly** toward lean while watching the EGT indication. The temperature climbs toward a maximum close to the **chemically correct ratio of 15:1**.

**Peak EGT is not the selected operating setting** in this description because of the associated danger of detonation. After finding the peak, the mixture is moved back toward rich until EGT has fallen by the amount specified in the aircraft's **flight manual**. This establishes the appropriate **rich-cruise** setting.

Leaning beyond the chemically correct point first causes **EGT to fall while cylinder head temperature (CHT) rises**. With still more extreme leaning, **both EGT and CHT decrease**. For an economy-cruise mixture, the flight manual likewise specifies the temperature reduction to use rather than relying on a guessed setting.

Mixture adjustment is normally undertaken at **cruise-power settings**. The mixture control is returned to **fully rich whenever engine power is changed**, as described in the chapter's operating guidance.

## 9. Diesel Engines

Aircraft diesel engines usually work with a **lean mixture**. Their intake-air supply is **not throttled** in the same way as that of a conventional spark-ignition piston engine: air enters freely, and the operating condition depends on the amount of fuel delivered.

The detonation problem described for conventional spark-ignition engines is not encountered in the same way in diesels. Diesel running temperatures are, however, generally higher, making an **effective and dependable cooling system** especially important.

Aero-diesels also dispense with a separate **mixture-control lever**. They use a **single-lever operating arrangement**, comparable in concept to the simplified engine-control arrangement used on some turboprop aircraft.
