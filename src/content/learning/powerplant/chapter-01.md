# Chapter 1: Piston Engines — Introduction

## 1\. Introduction to Piston Engines

### 1.1 Why internal combustion became important to aviation

Early attempts at powered flight were limited by the engines available at the time. Steam engines could generate mechanical power, but their **high weight and poor efficiency** made them unsuitable for the demands of flight. In a steam engine, the fuel burns outside the engine's working mechanism; a substantial amount of the heat produced is lost to the surroundings instead of becoming useful output.

A major change occurred when combustion was brought **inside the engine itself**. The textbook attributes the development of this principle to **Beau de Rochas in 1862** and the production of a successful working engine based on it to **Nikolaus Otto in 1876**.

### 1.2 How a piston engine produces useful power

A typical spark-ignition piston engine works by converting fuel energy into the mechanical rotation needed to drive a propeller or other machinery:

1. **Induction:** A charge consisting of fuel and air enters a cylinder.
2. **Compression:** A piston moves within the cylinder and compresses the charge into a smaller space.
3. **Ignition and expansion:** Ignition raises the temperature of the mixture rapidly. The resulting rise in cylinder pressure pushes the piston downwards.
4. **Conversion to rotation:** A **connecting rod and crankshaft** convert the piston's straight-line movement into rotary movement.
5. **Exhaust:** The products of combustion leave the cylinder and pass to the atmosphere.

In energy terms, the engine first changes the **chemical energy of the fuel into heat** through combustion, then converts part of that heat into **mechanical work**.

### 1.3 Main internal-combustion engine arrangements

The introductory classification distinguishes three broad groups:

* **Compression-ignition engines:** Diesel-type engines, in which ignition results from compression rather than an electric spark.
* **Spark-ignition reciprocating engines:** These may operate on **two-stroke** or **four-stroke** cycles.
* **Wankel rotary engines:** An internal-combustion design that uses rotary rather than conventional reciprocating movement.

This portion of the textbook concentrates on the **four-stroke spark-ignition piston engine**, a familiar type of aircraft powerplant.

## 2\. Fundamental Engineering Terminology

### 2.1 Force

**Force** is an influence capable of starting movement in a body that is free to move, stopping a moving body, or changing its direction of movement. A force can also cause a body to accelerate.

Newton's relationship between force, mass and acceleration is:

$$
F = m a
$$

Where:

* **F:** Force.
* **m:** Mass of the body.
* **a:** Acceleration produced.

The usual units are **newtons (N)** or **pounds-force (lbf)**. Inside a piston engine, the pressure generated during combustion applies a force to the piston and drives it along the cylinder.

### 2.2 Work

**Work** is performed when a force causes movement through a distance **in the direction of that force**. Thus, the amount of work depends on both the applied force and the distance moved.

$$
W\_{\\mathrm{done}} = Fd
$$

Work can be expressed in **joules (J)** or **foot-pounds (ft lbf)**. In the engine, expanding gases exert a force on the piston while it travels from the upper region toward the lower region of the cylinder; that force acting over the piston stroke constitutes work.

### 2.3 Energy

**Energy** describes the ability or capacity to carry out work. It may take several forms, including:

* **Heat energy**.
* **Light energy**.
* **Chemical energy**.
* **Kinetic energy**, associated with motion.
* **Potential energy**, associated with position or stored capability.

The standard unit is the **joule (J)**.

The **law of conservation of energy** states that energy is not created or destroyed; it is **converted from one form to another**. Applied to a piston engine, the chemical energy stored in the fuel becomes thermal energy during burning, and some of that thermal energy is then converted into mechanical energy by the piston and crankshaft.

### 2.4 Power

**Power** is the *rate* at which work is performed. It distinguishes the total quantity of work from how quickly that work is accomplished.

$$
P = \\frac{W\_{\\mathrm{done}}}{t}
$$

A piston can perform work during one stroke; an engine completing many strokes each minute can therefore deliver a measurable **rate of work**, or power.

* One **watt (W)** equals one **joule per second (J/s)**.
* Power may also be stated in **horsepower (hp)**.
* The textbook uses the conversion **1 hp = 33,000 ft lbf/min**.

## 3\. Dynamics: Newton's Laws of Motion

**Dynamics** deals with forces and the motion they produce. Newton's laws apply to visible engine parts, such as pistons and crankshafts, and also to **air**, even though the movement of air is less obvious. This is especially important because **air is the working fluid inside a piston engine**.

### 3.1 Newton's First Law — Inertia

An object remains at rest, or continues moving at a constant speed along a straight path, unless an **external force** changes that condition.

The resistance of a body to a change in its motion or state of rest is known as **inertia**.

**Engine applications:**

* The fuel–air charge needs a driving force to enter the cylinder; it does not begin flowing merely because an inlet passage exists.
* After the piston is moving, its inertia opposes attempts to stop or change its motion.

Inertia is a **property of the body** rather than a separately measured force. The textbook therefore assigns no particular unit to Newton's first law and also calls it the **law of inertia**.

### 3.2 Newton's Second Law — Force, Mass and Acceleration

For a given mass, acceleration increases when the applied force increases. For a given force, acceleration becomes smaller as the mass increases.

$$
F = ma
$$

In an operating engine, burning fuel increases the pressure of the gas in the cylinder. That pressure supplies the force which accelerates the piston. Consequently, **changing the cylinder pressure changes the force available to move the piston**.

The chapter also introduces **momentum**, the product of a body's mass and velocity:

$$
\\text{Momentum} = mv
$$

Unlike the general property of inertia, momentum describes a **moving body** and has measurable units, such as **kg m/s**. The textbook refers to Newton's second law as the **momentum law**.

### 3.3 Newton's Third Law — Action and Reaction

Whenever one body applies a force to another, the second body produces an **equal force in the opposite direction**.

Examples include:

* **Gun recoil:** A gun moves backwards as it expels a bullet forwards.
* **Water hose reaction:** A hose can move or snake about because water is expelled through its nozzle.
* **Jet-engine operation:** Propelling a mass of gas in one direction produces an opposing reaction on the engine.

Newton's third law is consequently called the **reaction law**.

## 4\. Thermodynamics

**Thermodynamics** concerns heat and pressure energy and, particularly for engines, the response of gases and vapours when their **temperature** or **pressure** changes.

### 4.1 First Law of Thermodynamics

Heat and mechanical energy can be **converted into each other**. Their equivalence can be measured, and the relationship between the amounts exchanged is constant.

Two useful examples make the connection clear:

* **Mechanical energy becoming heat:** Moving surfaces rubbing together without lubrication generate heat through friction; the resulting temperature increase can be measured.
* **Heat energy becoming mechanical energy:** Burning fuel produces heat in an engine cylinder, while the piston and crankshaft convert part of this energy into useful mechanical movement.

### 4.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics

Heat does not, by itself, transfer from a **cooler region to a hotter region**. Such a transfer requires energy supplied from an external source.

* A **hot radiator** naturally releases heat to the surrounding cooler air.
* A **refrigerator** must consume energy to keep its interior colder than its surroundings.

These laws help explain how engines extract useful work from high-temperature gases and why temperature differences matter in thermal systems.

## 5\. Bernoulli's Theorem

### 5.1 Energy in moving fluids

**Daniel Bernoulli (1700–1782)**, a Swiss scientist, investigated the behaviour of fluids in motion. The treatment in this chapter divides the total energy in a flowing gas or liquid into three components:

1. **Potential energy:** Associated with its height or position.
2. **Pressure energy:** Associated with the pressure of the fluid.
3. **Kinetic energy:** Associated with its movement and velocity.

For **steady, streamlined flow of an ideal fluid**, the total of these energy components remains constant along the flow.

### 5.2 Relationship between airspeed and pressure

In the simplified aerodynamic example used by the book, changes in height are not significant, so the potential-energy component is treated as constant. The essential trade-off is then between **kinetic energy and pressure energy**:

* When the fluid's **velocity rises**, its kinetic energy rises and its **static pressure decreases**.
* When the fluid's **velocity falls**, kinetic energy falls and static pressure can increase.

The key idea is that a change in one form of energy is balanced by a change in another, so the total remains constant within the assumed conditions.

## 6\. Venturi Tube and Constant Mass Flow

### 6.1 Construction of a Venturi tube

A **Venturi tube** is a shaped duct with three main regions:

1. A **convergent inlet**, whose cross-sectional area progressively decreases.
2. A narrow **throat**, at which the duct has its smallest area.
3. A relatively longer **divergent outlet**, whose area gradually increases again.

It is therefore also described as a **convergent–divergent duct**.

### 6.2 Continuity of mass flow

For steady flow through the duct, the same mass of air must pass each cross-section in a given time, provided there is no addition, loss, or accumulation of air between them. The basic relationship is:

$$
\\dot{m} = \\rho A v
$$

Where:

* **$\\dot{m}$:** Mass flow per unit time.
* **$\\rho$:** Air density.
* **$A$:** Flow cross-sectional area.
* **$v$:** Flow velocity.

The product of **area × density × velocity** is therefore constant for the steady flow described. This is the **continuity equation**.

### 6.3 Changes through the duct

As the airflow reaches the constricted throat, the available passage becomes smaller. To keep mass flowing through it, the air must move **faster**. The book describes the corresponding changes as follows:

|Part of the Venturi|Velocity|Pressure|Temperature|
|-|-|-|-|
|Convergent duct|Increasing|Decreasing|Decreasing|
|Narrowest throat|Maximum|Minimum|Minimum|
|Divergent duct|Decreasing|Increasing|Increasing|

The link to Bernoulli's theorem is that **faster flow at the throat corresponds to lower pressure**. The pressure depression created at this point is an important principle used in **carburettor operation**, examined later in Chapter 8 of the source textbook.

## 7\. Gas Laws

The operation of a piston engine involves repeated changes in **gas pressure, volume and temperature**. Gas laws describe how these variables are related under specified conditions.

### 7.1 Boyle's Law — Constant temperature

**Boyle's law** states that, for a fixed quantity of gas held at a **constant temperature**, pressure and volume change in opposite directions.

$$
PV = K
$$

Here:

* **$P$:** Absolute gas pressure.
* **$V$:** The volume occupied by the gas at that pressure.
* **$K$:** A constant for the stated conditions.

If the available volume decreases, pressure increases; if volume increases, pressure decreases. This law assumes the temperature remains constant and the pressure is stated on an **absolute** basis.

### 7.2 Charles's Law — Constant pressure

In the form given in the chapter, **Charles's law** (also referred to there as **Gay-Lussac's law**) relates gas volume to **absolute temperature** when pressure remains constant.

$$
\\frac{V}{T} = K
$$

Therefore, an increase in absolute temperature is accompanied by an increase in volume when the pressure is held constant. The temperature in this relationship must be an **absolute temperature**, not an ordinary Celsius reading.

### 7.3 Combined Gas Law

The **combined gas law** brings together the effects of pressure, volume and absolute temperature:

$$
\\frac{PV}{T} = K
$$

Equivalently:

$$
PV = KT
$$

For a given quantity of gas changing from one condition to another:

$$
\\frac{P\_1V\_1}{T\_1} = \\frac{P\_2V\_2}{T\_2}
$$

In this expression, the subscripts **1** and **2** identify the initial and final gas conditions. The relation is useful for understanding how all three gas properties change during piston-engine operation.

### 7.4 Applying the laws to a four-stroke engine

As a piston travels repeatedly between the top and bottom of its cylinder, it changes the space available to the gas. At the same time, gas is admitted, compressed, heated through combustion, expanded and exhausted. Consequently, **pressure, volume and temperature do not remain fixed during the complete operating cycle**.

The four movements of the piston are known as the **four strokes**. They take **two complete crankshaft revolutions**, and only **one stroke** is the useful power stroke. The text draws a distinction between **four piston strokes** and **five theoretical events**, because **combustion is an event in its own right**, occurring between compression and the power-producing expansion.

The five events are:

1. **Induction**.
2. **Compression**.
3. **Combustion**.
4. **Power**.
5. **Exhaust**.

## 8\. Diesel Engines

### 8.1 Historical development

The history presented in the textbook distinguishes the early development of **cold-fuel compression ignition** from the name that later became commonly associated with such engines.

* **1891 — Herbert Akroyd Stuart:** The book credits Stuart with a **cold-fuel injection system**, similar in principle to systems later used in road-vehicle and aircraft engines.
* **1892 — Rudolf Diesel:** Diesel designed and patented a related engine arrangement, described in the book in connection with a **hot-bulb system** and compressed-air fuel delivery used to aid starting by warming the fuel.
* The term **Diesel engine** subsequently became the widely used name for compression-ignition engines, even though the text attributes the earlier cold-fuel concept to Akroyd Stuart.

Developments in cold-fuel compression ignition were attractive because they made engines capable of **higher operating speeds, lower weight and simpler maintenance**.

### 8.2 Diesel engines in aircraft

Compression-ignition powerplants are not new to aviation: **aero-diesels appeared in the late 1920s**.

In overall mechanical layout, a diesel resembles a conventional petrol-fuelled reciprocating engine. One historical difference noted in the source is that **diesel reciprocating components were made somewhat heavier** to accommodate the greater loads associated with their **higher compression ratios**.

The textbook links renewed interest in aviation diesels to improvements in:

* **Structural and engineering materials**.
* **Supercharging**.
* **Engine design and construction**.

These improvements made diesel engine weights comparable with conventional engines and allowed favourable **power-to-weight ratios**. As examples of the developments discussed by the source, it mentions certified conversion programmes being trialled with the **Piper Warrior PA-28** and the **Cessna 172**.
