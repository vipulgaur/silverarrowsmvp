# Chapter 15: Gas Turbines — Compressors

## 1. Types of Compressor

Before entering the combustion chambers, the air flowing through a gas turbine must undergo compression. Fuel is then introduced into this compressed air, and the resulting gases expand through the turbine section.

Gas turbine compressors generally fall into two groups:

- **Centrifugal-flow compressors**, which direct air radially away from the centre of a rotating impeller.
- **Axial-flow compressors**, in which the main airflow continues along the length of the engine.

In either arrangement, the compressor receives mechanical drive from a turbine through an interconnecting shaft.

## 2. Centrifugal Compressors

### 2.1 Advantages and limitations

**Strength, straightforward construction and relatively low manufacturing cost** are important advantages of a centrifugal compressor. Its ruggedness and ease of production made it especially attractive in early gas turbine designs.

Its limitations become apparent when compared with an axial compressor having an **equal frontal area**. The axial type is capable of passing a considerably greater mass of air and achieving a substantially higher overall pressure ratio. Since engine thrust depends partly on the mass flow passing through the engine, a centrifugal-compressor engine of the same frontal area will generally develop less thrust than an equivalent axial-compressor installation.

These characteristics explain why centrifugal compressors are less common in large, modern propulsion engines, despite their structural and manufacturing advantages.

### 2.2 How centrifugal compression takes place

A turbine-driven **impeller** spins at high speed. Air enters continuously at the central region, called the **eye**, and passes into passages formed by the rotating vanes. Centrifugal action carries the air outward toward the impeller circumference.

Two changes occur as it moves through the impeller:

1. **Pressure rises** because the vane passages diverge as the air travels outward.
2. **Velocity increases** because rotating machinery transfers energy to the air.

At the impeller outlet, the air enters a **diffuser** made up of stationary, widening passages. These passages slow the moving air and convert a substantial portion of its **kinetic energy** into **pressure energy**.

Approximately half the overall pressure increase occurs inside the impeller, with the remaining half occurring in the diffuser.

A single centrifugal stage normally provides a pressure ratio in the region of **4:1**; the discharge pressure is therefore roughly four times the inlet pressure. Additional pressure rise can be obtained by placing **two centrifugal stages in series**. More than two stages are generally impractical because the tip speeds and centrifugal forces necessary for a third stage become excessively high. Consequently, compressor pressure ratios above approximately **15:1** are not regarded as achievable by this arrangement in the designs described here.

The outlet casing also contains **cascade vanes** in its elbow sections. These guide the air around large changes in direction with relatively little loss and assist in completing the diffusion process.

## 3. Axial-Flow Compressors

### 3.1 Rotor and stator arrangement

An axial compressor, like a centrifugal compressor, converts energy supplied by rotating machinery into increased air pressure. However, it uses alternating rows of airfoil-shaped blades rather than a centrifugal impeller and separate radial diffuser.

There are two kinds of blade row:

- **Rotor blades** turn with the compressor drum and are attached to discs or other rotating structure.
- **Stator blades** remain stationary and are secured to the compressor casing.

A **compressor stage** consists of **one rotor row followed by one stator row**. The spaces between adjacent blades form passages of increasing effective flow area in both rows.

As air passes through the **rotor**, work supplied by the driving turbine raises its energy. Both its velocity and pressure increase. In the following **stator**, some of the velocity energy is transformed into additional pressure, so that the air leaves the stage at a higher pressure.

The rotor therefore fulfils a role comparable to the **impeller** in a centrifugal compressor, while the stator performs a function comparable to that of the **diffuser**.

### 3.2 Pressure ratios and compressor stages

Each axial stage produces a relatively small increase in pressure, commonly about **1.1:1 to 1.2:1**. The pressure rise in an early stage may be only around **3 psi**. High overall ratios are possible because a succession of stages acts upon the air, with the pressure increases accumulating as it passes rearward.

An axial compressor can contain numerous stages within one shaft-driven assembly, or **spool**. Some engines use **as many as three separate spools**. The RB211 is an example of a design capable of an overall compressor ratio of about **35:1**, with the final stage contributing a pressure rise of as much as **80 psi**.

Such pressures also produce considerable heating: **compressor outlet temperatures may reach approximately 600°C**. Some engines employ both **axial and centrifugal** sections in a combined compressor system.

### 3.3 Maintaining axial airflow velocity

The air passes through the annular passage between the **rotor drum** and the **outer compressor casing**. This passage is termed the **air annulus**.

As compression proceeds, the air occupies progressively less volume. To prevent its axial velocity from changing excessively, the cross-sectional area of the annulus must become progressively smaller toward the high-pressure end.

This convergence may be produced by:

- Tapering the **outer casing** inward.
- Increasing the diameter of the **rotor drum**.
- Using both changes together.

## 4. Airflow Control and Compressor Stability

### 4.1 Why the compressor needs airflow control

Achieving high pressure ratios makes it harder for a compressor to operate efficiently at every engine speed. As rotational speed decreases, the **compression ratio also drops**, so the air in the rear stages remains at a greater volume than it would at the design operating speed.

The rear part of the compressor has a relatively small passage area. If the air occupies too much volume, it encounters restriction and may become **choked or turbulent**. Airflow then slows through the compressor, including in the front stages.

A sufficiently disturbed balance between blade motion, axial airflow and pressure ratio can produce **compressor stall**. If conditions continue to deteriorate, stall can develop into **surge**, potentially accompanied by a momentary reversal of airflow through the engine.

### 4.2 Compressor stall

Air does not approach a rotating compressor blade at an angle determined by axial airflow alone. The **axial velocity of the air** combines with the **rotational velocity of the blade** to create a resultant relative flow direction. That relative flow determines the angle at which air strikes the blade: its effective **angle of attack**.

A stall becomes possible when the relationship between these two velocities departs sufficiently from the condition for which the blade was designed.

Several circumstances can upset that relationship:

1. **Sudden acceleration with excessive fuel delivery.** An increase in combustion-chamber back pressure reduces the axial flow speed.
2. **Operation outside the intended engine-rpm range.** The rotating blades move faster or slower than their design condition.
3. **Disturbed airflow at the intake.** Turbulence or disruption reduces the effective axial velocity reaching the compressor.
4. **Dirty or damaged compressor parts.** Poorer compression performance is accompanied by reduced axial airflow speed.
5. **Dirty or damaged turbine parts.** A loss of turbine driving power weakens compressor performance and reduces axial flow speed as the compression ratio falls.
6. **Abrupt deceleration producing an excessively weak mixture.** Lower combustion-chamber back pressure can cause axial airflow velocity to increase too much in relation to blade speed.

Once a blade row starts stalling, the passage of air through the engine becomes partially disrupted. Two typical indications are **increased engine vibration** and a **rise in exhaust gas temperature (EGT)**. The temperature increase follows from the reduced quantity of air reaching the combustion system: less air is available to cool the combustion products.

Stall may begin locally, even at one blade. It can then spread through an entire compressor stage and, if not corrected, affect a much greater part of the engine.

### 4.3 Compressor surge

**Surge** is a severe condition in which compressor airflow breaks down completely. In extreme cases, gases are driven momentarily in the reverse direction and may be discharged from the intake with a loud report.

A surge can produce several noticeable effects:

- A loud bang or other abrupt noise.
- A major increase in **EGT**.
- A pronounced **loss of thrust**.
- **Yaw** caused by unequal thrust when one engine is affected.

Fuel-system malfunction and incorrect engine handling are common precipitating factors. Particularly severe surges can impose large bending forces on compressor blades. These forces may cause rotor blades to strike stator vanes, creating the risk of major damage.

**If surge occurs, the affected engine's throttle is to be closed slowly**, as stated in the operating discussion for this chapter.

### 4.4 Methods of preventing stall and surge

An engine must be capable of running below the high-rpm region where its compressor is most efficient. This necessarily changes both blade rotational speed and axial airflow speed, making a means of stability control essential.

The compressor-control arrangements covered here are:

1. **Variable inlet guide vanes (VIGVs).**
2. **Variable stator vanes.**
3. **Compressor bleed valves.**
4. **Multi-spool compressor arrangements.**
5. **Active clearance control.**

### 4.5 Variable inlet guide vanes (VIGVs)

VIGVs are used where low engine speed and changes of rpm can make the first compressor stages especially vulnerable to stall. They are mounted **ahead of the first rotor row** and can pivot automatically to alter the direction of air entering the rotating blades.

At low compressor rpm, the guide vanes supply a greater amount of **swirl**. This changes the relative direction of the incoming air so that the rotor blades encounter it at a more suitable angle of attack. Correcting the flow direction in this way helps the engine accelerate **smoothly and rapidly** without stalling the front stages.

### 4.6 Variable stator vanes

Controlling the air entering the first rotor is not sufficient for all operating conditions. Further downstream, the angle of the airflow approaching later rotor rows may also become unsuitable when compressor speed falls below its optimum value.

**Variable stator vanes** therefore pivot automatically. As compressor rpm reduces, the vanes **progressively close** to direct the flow toward the following rotor stage at an acceptable angle of attack.

### 4.7 Compressor bleed valves

With the engine running slowly, lower compression causes the air in the rear compressor stages to occupy excessive volume. The resulting restriction lowers mass flow through the rear stages and reduces the velocity of the air entering the forward stages, increasing the risk of stall.

A **compressor bleed valve** placed in an **intermediate compressor stage** can release part of this air during low-rpm operation or while the engine accelerates. By removing some of the excess flow:

- The rear stages experience less choking.
- Air velocity through the earlier stages improves.
- The likelihood of compressor stall while the valves are open is reduced.

Bleeding compressor air has a cost, whether the air is being released for stall control or diverted to aircraft systems. Less mass flow continues through the engine, resulting in **less thrust at the same throttle setting**, **higher specific fuel consumption (SFC)**, and **higher EGT** because there is less cooling air available.

### 4.8 Multi-spool compressors

Early axial compressor designs increased their pressure ratios mainly by putting more stages onto **one shaft**. Although this raised maximum compression, it also made low-speed operation harder to manage. Blade shapes were optimized for conditions close to high engine rpm, when axial air velocity and rotor rotation produced the required relative airflow angle.

Reducing the speed of a single compressor shaft changed the balance of these velocities. The resulting change in angle of attack encouraged stall, particularly away from the design rpm.

A more flexible arrangement divides the compressor into **two or three independent sections**. Each section is mechanically linked by its own shaft to a corresponding turbine. The compressor section, shaft and driving turbine together form one **spool**.

The spools operate at different speeds; in particular, the **high-pressure (HP) compressor rotates faster** than the **low-pressure (LP) compressor**. As the throttle closes, the LP spool can be arranged to lose speed more quickly than the HP section. This helps maintain a more suitable relationship between blade rotational velocity and axial airflow throughout a wider operating range, greatly reducing the likelihood of stall.

### 4.9 Active clearance control

A further means of regulating compressor airflow is to adjust the effective clearance between blade tips and the compressor casing, especially near the **high-pressure end**.

Because compressor stability depends upon the relationship between blade rotational speed and air velocity, control over the annulus size provides another means of influencing flow conditions.

**Air cooling applied to the outside of the compressor casing** causes the metal casing to contract. The reduction in its diameter alters the clearance between the casing and blade tips. Cooling air normally flows through **tubing arranged around the exterior casing**. Controlled dimensional changes allow the desired clearance to be obtained over more of the engine's operating range.

### 4.10 Compressor surge envelope

The risk of compressor surge depends on the combination of **airflow** and **pressure ratio**. Compressor performance can therefore be represented on a graph of pressure ratio against airflow.

The desired operating path is known as the **working line** or **design line**. A separate **surge line** defines the boundary beyond which the compressor enters an unstable operating region. The space between these lines constitutes the **safety margin**.

Airflow-control equipment, such as compressor bleeds, helps the engine remain along its intended operating line with sufficient margin below the surge boundary.

## 5. Compressor Construction

### 5.1 Shafts, discs and casing

The compressor's rotating shaft is supported by **bearings**. A coupling joins it to the turbine shaft and permits small differences in shaft alignment.

Compressor rotor blades are carried on **discs** mounted around the rotor shaft. The high centrifugal forces acting on rotating blades make strong, secure attachments essential. A common arrangement uses a **dovetail-shaped blade root** retained in the disc by a **pin or locking tab**.

On small engines, conventional blade attachment systems can add undesirable disc weight and become difficult to manufacture. An alternative is the **blisk**, in which the blades and their supporting disc are made as one integral component.

Compressor casing materials depend on the operating temperatures of the particular section:

| Compressor location | Casing material |
|---|---|
| Forward stages | Aluminium alloy |
| Intermediate stages | Steel alloys |
| High-pressure region | Nickel-based alloys capable of tolerating the high temperatures |

### 5.2 Rotor blades

The rotating compressor blades have an **aerofoil cross-section**. They are usually produced from **drop-forged stainless steel** and machined to close tolerances before installation on the rotor disc.

Blade height decreases from the front of the compressor toward its rear because the annular airflow passage becomes narrower as compression increases. **Titanium** can be used in some low-pressure stages, where temperatures are lower.

A dovetail attachment is not necessarily completely tight when an engine is stopped. Blades may be relatively free to move within their mounting slots until centrifugal force seats them firmly against their attachments during rotation. At low ground windmilling speeds, that movement can produce an audible metallic rattle.

### 5.3 Stator vanes

Stationary compressor vanes also use **aerofoil profiles**. They are connected to the outer casing either individually or through **stator retaining rings** that are fastened to the casing.

At the front of the compressor, the stators may be installed in groups or **segments**. Longer vanes are sometimes joined at their inner ends by a **shroud**. This arrangement reduces the vibration that airflow might otherwise induce.

Earlier stators were manufactured from aluminium alloy, but this metal provided poor resistance to **foreign-object ingestion damage**. Steel and nickel-based alloys have better fatigue properties and are less prone to cracking or impact erosion. Titanium may be used in the cooler forward stages but is unsuitable farther aft where compressor temperatures become excessive.

Titanium components also present a hazard if serious rubbing occurs. A mechanical fault may create sufficient frictional heating between moving and stationary parts to **ignite titanium**, causing costly damage and potentially threatening airworthiness.

### 5.4 Fan blades

The first-stage blades in a high-bypass-ratio engine are large **low-pressure compressor blades**, generally called **fan blades**.

Early fan blades were commonly made from **solid titanium**, which provided considerable strength without excessive mass. Low blade weight is especially important because the fan assembly must tolerate the unbalanced forces associated with a blade failure.

To prevent aerodynamic instability, older titanium fan blades incorporated **mid-span supports known as snubbers**. While these helped stabilize the blades, they added weight. Some designs needed two snubbers, which also interfered with the supersonic airflow near the outer portions of the blades.

Trials using **carbon-fibre** designs did not provide the desired results because the material's flexibility reduced their effectiveness; this approach was largely abandoned in the designs discussed here.

## 6. Compressor and Turbine Contamination

Contamination of the compressor or turbine degrades the aerodynamic shapes upon which efficient engine operation depends. As deposits accumulate, the engine's operating efficiency and overall performance can fall significantly.

The material building up on **compressor blades** is commonly associated with **sea salt** and **industrial pollution**. Deposits change the effective blade surface and reduce aerodynamic efficiency.

In the **turbine**, fuel-borne sulphur can cause **sulphidation**. Sulphur-based deposits develop on the turbine blades and nozzle guide vanes, changing their designed profiles and eventually damaging or eroding their surface finish.

### 6.1 Desalination wash

If salt deposits are the main problem, early cleaning with **fresh water** can prevent the need for a more aggressive process. The water can be introduced while the engine is being **motored by the starter**, or while the engine runs at **idle speed**. This procedure is known as a **desalination wash**.

### 6.2 Performance recovery wash

Where freshwater cleaning is insufficient, an **emulsion-type surface cleaner** may be sprayed into the intake using the same engine conditions. This is a **performance recovery wash**.

The cleaning action also benefits the turbine, and regular treatment may prolong the useful service life of some engines.

### 6.3 Abrasive cleaning

A more forceful cleaning method, particularly applicable to some **centrifugal-compressor** engines, introduces abrasive granules through the intake while the engine is running at idle.

These granules may be made from **crushed walnut shells**, or **crushed apricot stones** as used in American practice. Much of the material burns away in the combustion chambers, so it is generally **less effective at cleaning turbine components** than the liquid-washing method.
