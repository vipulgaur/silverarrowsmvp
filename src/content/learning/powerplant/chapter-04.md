# Chapter 4: Piston Engines — Cooling

## 1. The Reasons for Cooling

A piston engine is a **heat engine**: it uses the energy released by burning fuel to drive mechanical components and produce useful output. Only part of the fuel's heat energy becomes mechanical work. Even under favourable conditions, its **thermal efficiency is approximately 25–28%**. Consequently, **more than 70%** of the energy supplied by the fuel does not become useful engine output.

The engine's energy losses are distributed in different ways:

- **Exhaust gases:** Approximately **40%** of the fuel's energy leaves with the exhaust. Some engines recover part of this otherwise wasted energy by using the exhaust to drive the turbine of a **turbocharger** (a turbine-driven supercharger).
- **Heating of engine components:** Approximately **32%** contributes to raising the temperature of engine parts. Unless this heat is removed at an appropriate rate, component temperatures can become excessive.

### 1.1 Problems Caused by Excessive Temperature

An overheated piston engine can suffer several distinct problems:

1. **Structural damage or failure.** Components exposed to excessive heat may lose the ability to withstand their operating loads.
2. **Deterioration of lubricating oil.** If the oil becomes too hot, its lubricating characteristics may break down, leaving engine parts less adequately protected.
3. **Pre-ignition.** The incoming fuel–air charge may ignite **before the spark plug fires**, because a sufficiently hot region in the cylinder initiates combustion prematurely.
4. **Knocking or detonation.** Combustion may become abnormal and unstable **after ignition by the spark plug**. This differs from pre-ignition, in which ignition begins before the intended spark.

Both **pre-ignition** and **knocking/detonation** reduce the engine's available power.

### 1.2 Problems Caused by Excessive Cooling

Keeping an engine as cold as possible is not the objective. Operating at an unnecessarily low temperature is also undesirable:

- **Thermal efficiency:** Good thermal efficiency depends on the engine reaching a sufficiently high working temperature.
- **Oil viscosity and friction:** Cold lubricating oil is more viscous and creates greater internal friction. The resulting increase in **friction horsepower** reduces the **brake horsepower** available as useful output.
- **Fuel vaporisation:** At low temperatures, liquid fuel changes into vapour less readily. This can interfere with the preparation of the fuel–air mixture and its subsequent combustion.

### 1.3 The Required Temperature Balance

Efficient operation therefore depends on maintaining the engine at **the highest temperature consistent with safe operation**, not merely providing maximum cooling. Since the external air temperature and the engine's own heat production both change, a **cooling system** is needed to regulate the working temperature under different conditions.

## 2. Liquid and Air-cooled Systems

The arrangement of an engine's cylinders depends partly on its required power output and on the cooling method selected. The two principal arrangements for removing engine heat are **liquid cooling** and **air cooling**.

### 2.1 Liquid Cooling

A liquid-cooled engine carries heat away from the cylinders and cylinder heads using a circulating coolant. The coolant is a mixture of **water and glycol**, the glycol providing antifreeze protection.

The cooling circuit contains the following main items:

- **Cooling passages** formed around the cylinders and in the cylinder heads, where the circulating liquid absorbs engine heat.
- An **engine-driven pump** to move coolant through the system.
- A **thermostat** to regulate operating temperature.
- A **header tank**, serving as the coolant reservoir.
- **Connecting pipes** between the header tank, engine and radiator.
- An **air-cooled radiator** to transfer heat from the circulating liquid to the surrounding airflow.

After collecting heat inside the engine, coolant is routed through the radiator, where passing air removes that heat. The circulation system then returns the liquid through the cooling circuit. The radiator is located in the **propeller slipstream**, so it receives an airflow even when the aircraft is stationary on the ground.

### 2.2 Direct Air Cooling

In an air-cooled installation, heat passes **straight from the engine surfaces into the cooling air**, rather than first being carried to a radiator by a liquid. That air comes from two sources:

- The **propeller slipstream**.
- Airflow produced by the **aircraft's forward movement**.

The engine is enclosed by a **cowling**. Besides making the installation aerodynamically cleaner, the cowling guides air around the cylinders so that cooling is distributed across the engine, instead of excessively cooling its forward-facing parts.

Some installations allow cooling airflow to be changed by adjusting **cowl flaps** or **gills** at the back of the cowling.

## 3. Air Cooling

### 3.1 Why It Is Widely Used

Compared with a liquid-cooled engine of similar power, an air-cooled piston engine generally has:

- **Fewer moving parts** in the cooling arrangement.
- **Simpler construction** and a relatively small cooling-system maintenance requirement.
- **Lower weight**.

These characteristics explain the widespread use of air cooling in aircraft piston engines.

**Liquid cooling**, however, provides more effective heat removal, more precise control of engine temperature, and can produce **less aircraft drag**. It is therefore advantageous for some **high-speed aircraft with powerful engines**, despite the added system components.

### 3.2 Factors Affecting the Effectiveness of Air Cooling

#### Air Temperature

Cooling depends on the temperature difference between the engine and the outside air. **Cooler ambient air removes engine heat more rapidly.** The available cooling therefore changes with climate and altitude, both of which affect outside-air temperature.

#### Speed of the Airflow

Heat removal also depends on the **rate of airflow across the cylinders**. This changes as aircraft speed and the propeller slipstream change.

During **ground running**, the aircraft has little or no forward speed, making overheating a particular concern. Some installations use a **fan behind the propeller** to provide a more consistent flow of cooling air.

#### Cooling Fins

Cylinder surfaces are provided with **fins** to increase the area exposed to moving air. Greater exposed surface area allows more heat to pass from the cylinder to the air.

Fin spacing is a compromise:

- Fins must be close enough to provide a substantial cooling surface.
- They must not be so closely spaced that they create excessive resistance to airflow, increase the pressure opposing flow, reduce cooling-air quantity and raise drag.

An average fin spacing is approximately **five fins per inch**. The fins have **thin cross-sections** and can be extended where local heating is more severe, such as near **cylinder exhaust ports**. This helps equalise temperatures across the component.

### 3.3 Baffles

**Baffles** are guides that direct cooling air around the cylinder surfaces rather than allowing it to bypass them. They need to fit closely against the engine and **seal effectively against the cowling** so that the intended air passes over the cylinders.

The passage around the baffles is designed to retain a reasonably **uniform cross-sectional area**. Avoiding unsuitable changes in flow area helps prevent unnecessary airflow slowing and the associated drag. The arrangement of the inter-cylinder baffles is shown in **Figure 4.2**.

### 3.4 Engine Construction and Heat Transfer

Effective cooling also depends on the materials from which the engine is built. Where practicable, parts are made from materials that transfer heat readily. **Aluminium alloys** are commonly used because of their good thermal conductivity.

Some cylinder heads are made of **steel**. To improve heat transfer, the face of the combustion chamber can be provided with a **substantial copper deposit**.

### 3.5 Cowlings, Cowl Flaps and Gills

The cowling must fit properly and maintain its intended shape. **Dents, projections or other irregularities** can disrupt the designed airflow, causing both **higher aerodynamic drag** and **poorer cooling**.

Opening the **cowl flaps or gills** affects airflow in several related ways:

1. The external flow over the nacelle lowers pressure at the cooling-air outlet.
2. The lower outlet pressure makes it easier for heated air to leave the cowling and supports a strong airflow over the engine.
3. Air passing over the cylinders absorbs heat and expands.
4. With a suitable outlet opening, the heated airflow can accelerate as it leaves.
5. Increased exit speed produces a **reaction force** that can partly offset the drag associated with the engine installation.

Not all heat taken from the engine must be discharged directly outside. Some can be put to use as **cabin heating** or, when selected, as **warm air supplied for carburettor ice removal**.

## 4. Cylinder Head Temperature Gauge

Some aircraft allow the pilot to monitor engine heating through a **cylinder head temperature (CHT) gauge**.

The system uses a temperature sensor fixed to an engine cylinder head. Where the engine has **only one sensor**, it is normally placed on the **hottest cylinder**, which is usually among the **rear cylinders**.

The sensor is a **thermocouple**. It generates an electrical voltage that varies **directly with temperature**. The signal operates a sensitive moving-coil instrument known as a **galvanometer**. Although the mechanism responds to voltage, the cockpit instrument is **calibrated to show temperature**, not volts.

## 5. Operational Procedures

Cooling-system design must accommodate different operating conditions. Forward motion normally supplies sufficient cooling airflow in flight, but particular combinations of engine power and aircraft speed can still lead to inappropriate temperatures.

### 5.1 Climb

During a climb, the engine is normally operating at **high power**, so heat production is high. At the same time, the aircraft is travelling more slowly than in normal cruise, which reduces cooling airflow.

This combination increases the likelihood of overheating. For cooling considerations, **best rate-of-climb speed (VY)** is preferable to **prolonged flight at best angle-of-climb speed (VX)**, because the faster airflow at VY provides better cooling.

### 5.2 Descent and Thermal Shock

In descent, **reduced engine power** produces less heat. If the aircraft descends rapidly at high airspeed, the greater quantity of cold air flowing across the cylinders may cool them too quickly.

A sudden temperature reduction is called **thermal shock**. The resulting stresses can **fracture engine components**, with engine cylinders being particularly vulnerable.

Aircraft equipped with cowl flaps or gills have a more direct way to regulate cooling. Simpler light aircraft usually depend on **airspeed** to control cooling airflow.

### 5.3 Use of Cowl Flaps or Gills

The flap position should correspond to the engine's heat output and the cooling available:

| Operating condition | Cooling consideration | Typical cowl-flap or gill selection |
|---|---|---|
| **Take-off / high power at low speed** | Engine heating is high while airflow is relatively limited. | **Open** to increase cooling-air flow; this also increases drag. |
| **Descent** | Lower engine power and potentially higher airspeed can cause excessive cooling. | **Closed** to limit cooling. |
| **Cruise at altitude** | Colder outside air improves heat removal. | May be **partly closed** to maintain the desired engine temperature. |

### 5.4 Ground Operation

High-power operation on the ground should normally be restricted. With the aircraft stationary, **propeller slipstream is the main source of cooling air**, and it may not be sufficient to prevent overheating.

During ground running, the pilot should closely monitor:

- **Cylinder head temperature.**
- **Oil temperature.**

The oil system also has a direct cooling function: it removes heat from **internal engine parts**, including the **pistons and valves**, that the external cooling air does not reach directly.

### 5.5 Effect of Fuel–Air Mixture

**Mixture strength** influences the cylinder head temperature. The highest CHT values are associated with a **lean mixture selected for economy or endurance cruise**.

### 5.6 Engine Shutdown

Before stopping the engine, it should be operated at approximately **1,000–1,200 rpm** to help prevent **spark-plug fouling**. Taxiing should already have allowed the engine to cool and its temperature to stabilise.

An engine shut down while still extremely hot can cool unevenly, potentially damaging its components.

### 5.7 Cooling of Modern Aero-diesel Engines

Modern aero-diesel installations often use either **liquid cooling** or a **combined liquid-and-air arrangement**. Liquid cooling distributes heat more uniformly and provides more precise temperature control, enabling **tighter tolerances between moving engine parts**.

Possible disadvantages include **coolant leakage** and **additional weight**, but the improvements in cooling and temperature control can outweigh these disadvantages.
