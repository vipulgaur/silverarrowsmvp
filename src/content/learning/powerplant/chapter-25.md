# Chapter 25: Gas Turbines — Fuels

## 1. Introduction

An aviation engine requires fuel that can be supplied reliably, burned efficiently and handled safely. An ideal fuel for either a **gas turbine** or a **piston engine** would have eight important characteristics:

1. **Reliable flow:** It should move freely through the fuel system throughout the aircraft's operating conditions.
2. **Complete combustion:** It should burn thoroughly under all anticipated operating conditions.
3. **High calorific value:** It should release a large amount of heat energy when burned.
4. **Non-corrosive properties:** It should not corrode the components with which it comes into contact.
5. **Harmless combustion products:** Substances left after combustion should not damage the engine.
6. **Low fire risk:** The fuel should present as little fire hazard as practicable during handling and operation.
7. **Good starting characteristics:** It should allow the engine to start readily.
8. **Lubricity:** It should provide sufficient lubrication for fuel-system components that rely on the fuel for this purpose.

Although fuel properties and additives can be selected to address these requirements, achieving all of them to the highest possible standard would make the fuel prohibitively expensive. Fuel specifications therefore represent compromises between desirable characteristics and cost.

## 2. Gas Turbine Fuels

**Kerosene-based fuels** are used in gas-turbine-powered aircraft. Their characteristics differ, particularly in **specific gravity (SG)**, **flash point**, and the temperature at which wax formation becomes a concern.

### 2.1 Jet A-1 (AVTUR)

**Jet A-1**, also designated **AVTUR (aviation turbine fuel)**, is a kerosene-type fuel with the following nominal properties:

- **Specific gravity:** 0.80 at 15°C.
- **Flash point:** 38.7°C, described as a medium flash point.
- **Waxing point:** −50°C.

### 2.2 Jet A

**Jet A** is similar to Jet A-1 but has a **waxing point of −40°C**. It is normally available only in the **United States**.

### 2.3 Jet B (AVTAG)

**Jet B**, also known as **AVTAG (aviation turbine gasoline)**, is a **wide-cut mixture of gasoline and kerosene**. Its nominal characteristics are:

- **Specific gravity:** 0.77 at 15°C.
- **Flash point:** −20°C.
- **Waxing point:** −60°C.
- **Boiling range:** Wider than that of Jet A-1.

Jet B can serve as an alternative to Jet A-1. However, its very low flash point makes it highly flammable, so it is **generally avoided in civilian aircraft for safety reasons**.

## 3. Fuel Colour

Gas turbine fuels are **not artificially dyed**. Their natural appearance may vary from **straw yellow** to **completely colourless**.

## 4. Cloudy Fuel

A fuel sample that looks **cloudy or hazy** may contain either air or water. The movement of the cloudiness helps distinguish between them:

- **Air contamination:** The cloudy region moves **upwards fairly quickly** as air rises through the sample.
- **Water contamination:** The cloudiness moves **downwards more slowly** as water settles towards the bottom.

A cloudy appearance is most commonly associated with **water in the fuel**.

## 5. Jet Fuel Additives

### 5.1 FSII — Fuel System Icing Inhibitor

Some water is normally present in fuel, including water dissolved within it. This can produce both **icing problems** and **microbiological contamination**.

#### Icing

As an aircraft climbs, low temperatures cool the fuel. The amount of water that can remain dissolved decreases, so **small water droplets separate from the fuel**. Continued cooling can freeze these droplets into **ice crystals**. Accumulated ice may then obstruct fuel-system components and restrict the fuel supply.

#### Fungal growth and corrosion

The fungus **Cladasporium resinae** is associated with turbine fuel. Where water is present, it can multiply rapidly and develop **long, green filaments**. These growths may block parts of the fuel system.

The fungus also produces **corrosive waste products**, which are particularly harmful to **fuel-tank sealing materials**.

Adding **FSII** helps limit these water-related problems, including fuel-system icing and conditions that encourage fungal growth.

### 5.2 HITEC — Lubricity Agent

**HITEC**, a lubricity additive, improves the fuel's lubricating action and thereby helps **reduce wear in fuel-system components**.

### 5.3 Static Dissipater Additives

High-flow-rate fuel transfer equipment can generate **static electricity** as fuel moves through the system. **Static dissipater additives** reduce the resulting electrostatic hazard, although they do not remove it completely.

### 5.4 Corrosion Inhibitors

**Corrosion inhibitors** protect **ferrous-metal components** in fuel-handling installations, including **pipelines and storage tanks**. Certain corrosion inhibitors also improve the **lubricity** of some gas turbine fuels.

### 5.5 Metal Deactivators

Some metals, especially **copper**, can act as catalysts that promote **fuel oxidation**. **Metal deactivator additives** suppress this catalytic effect.

## 6. Water in the Fuel

Fuel inevitably contains some water. The quantity present depends in part on the manufacturer's **quality-control measures** and on precautions taken during **storage and transfer**.

Once fuel is in the aircraft's tanks, further steps can help minimise its water content and reduce the likelihood of contamination.

### 6.1 Water Drains

After refuelling, allowing the fuel to **settle** gives suspended water droplets an opportunity to separate. Because water is **denser than the fuel**, droplets collect at the **bottom of the tank**, where they can be removed using the **water drain valve**.

### 6.2 Fuel Heater

Fuel may be **heated before passing through the engine fuel system**. One gas turbine arrangement routes the fuel through a **heat exchanger heated by compressor delivery air**, helping remove ice crystals that may have formed during exposure to the extremely low temperatures encountered at altitude.

Some installations also use a **fuel-cooled oil cooler**. This arrangement performs two functions at once: the **engine oil loses heat**, while the **fuel gains heat**. It therefore provides oil cooling and fuel warming through the same heat-exchange process.

### 6.3 Atmosphere Exclusion

After the tanks have been filled, the **air remaining in the tank spaces** can introduce further moisture. Keeping the tanks full reduces this air space and the amount of atmospheric moisture available to contaminate the fuel.

However, a full tank also requires consideration of **thermal expansion**. If the ambient temperature rises after the tank has been topped up, the fuel's volume increases and fuel may **overflow through the tank vent system**.

## 7. Waxing

At sufficiently low temperatures, **heavy hydrocarbons** in the fuel can separate out as **paraffin-wax crystals**. This is known as **waxing**.

Wax deposits can **block fuel filters** and interfere with the operation of the **fuel control unit**.

Two measures reduce the risk:

1. **Refinery control:** Keep the proportion of heavy hydrocarbons in the fuel low.
2. **Fuel heating:** Use a heater within the engine fuel system to counter wax formation.

## 8. Boiling

A fuel's **boiling temperature depends on the pressure acting on its surface**. As an aircraft climbs, the pressure acting on the fuel decreases. The reduced pressure increases the likelihood of **fuel boiling and vapour formation**.

Vapour can interrupt liquid-fuel flow by creating a **vapour lock**. If this cuts off the fuel supply, the **engine stops**.

**Booster pumps installed inside the fuel tanks** help prevent this problem by **pushing fuel towards the engine**, instead of relying solely on engine-driven pumps to **draw or suck fuel from the tanks**.

## 9. The Effects of Specific Gravity (SG)

**Specific gravity (SG)** is also called **relative density**. Its value changes inversely with temperature: as fuel temperature rises, its specific gravity decreases; as temperature falls, its specific gravity increases.

The **heat released by fuel** is directly related to its specific gravity, so changes in fuel density can influence **engine power output**. In modern aircraft, the **fuel control unit usually compensates automatically** for density changes, making their effect on engine operation relatively small.

Changing the specific gravity also changes the **weight of the fuel carried**, and therefore changes the **aircraft's weight**.
