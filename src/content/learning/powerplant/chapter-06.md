# Chapter 6: Piston Engines — Fuel

## 1. Types of Fuel

The usual fuel for spark-ignition aircraft piston engines is **aviation gasoline (AVGAS)**. It is made from petroleum, a mineral-oil source containing compounds of **hydrogen and carbon**. Diesel and jet fuels also originate from petroleum, but their properties differ because the refining and **cracking** processes produce different hydrocarbon mixtures.

Fuel-dispensing equipment for AVGAS is identified by **red colour coding** to help prevent a different type of fuel from being delivered into an AVGAS installation.

### 1.1 Manufacturing Specifications and Grades

Aviation gasoline must meet tightly controlled production specifications. The specification referenced for aviation gasoline is **DERD 2485**, issued under the Directorate of Engine Research and Development (DERD) system. Within a specification, individual **fuel grades** identify particular performance characteristics; a grade designation is not itself an additional DERD specification number.

The AVGAS grades and identifying properties are:

| Fuel grade | Performance number | Colour | Specific gravity | Lead content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AVGAS 100LL** | 100/130 | Blue | 0.72 | Low lead |
| **AVGAS 100** | 100/130 | Green | 0.72 | High lead |
| **AVGAS 80** | 80/87 | Red | 0.72 | Very low lead |

**AVGAS 100 and AVGAS 100LL have the same 100/130 performance designation**, but their lead contents differ. Their **green and blue colours**, respectively, provide a quick means of distinguishing them.

Some aviation authorities permit approved aircraft to use **motor gasoline (MOGAS)** rather than AVGAS. In the United Kingdom, the permissions referenced for such installations are **Airworthiness Notices 98 and 98a**; further information is identified in **CAA Safety Sense Leaflet 4a**.

MOGAS is generally **more volatile** than AVGAS. This makes **vapour lock** and **carburettor icing** more likely, so it cannot be treated as an automatically interchangeable substitute for aviation gasoline.

### 1.2 Calorific Value

A fuel's **calorific value** expresses how much heat is released when a given amount burns. The stated measurement is **British thermal units per pound (BTU/lb)**.

Calorific value depends on the fuel's chemical make-up. A greater proportion of **hydrogen** is associated with higher heat output. It also has a relationship with **specific gravity**: in the comparison used here, fuel with a higher specific gravity has a higher calorific value.

## 2. Volatility

**Volatility** describes how readily a liquid becomes a vapour, whether because heat is supplied or because the liquid can evaporate into a gas in contact with it.

In a carburetted piston engine, fuel enters the moving airflow through the **carburettor**. How thoroughly the fuel mixes with that air depends substantially on how readily it vaporizes. The mixing interval is brief, so not every part of the fuel supply has time to vaporize immediately; some continues as **tiny liquid droplets** and evaporates farther along the **induction system**.

### 2.1 High Volatility and Low-Pressure Boiling

A liquid begins to boil when its vapour pressure exceeds the pressure of the atmosphere acting on its surface. At increasing altitude, ambient pressure falls, allowing fuel to boil at a **lower temperature**. This behaviour is known as **low-pressure boiling**.

Excessive volatility also brings unwanted effects. Fuel may evaporate excessively from tanks, or it may form vapour in parts of the fuel-delivery system where pressure is low, particularly around the inlet side of a pump. Such conditions encourage **cavitation** and **vapour lock**, as well as increasing the likelihood of carburettor icing under suitable atmospheric conditions.

## 3. Fuel Stability

Some hydrocarbons present in gasoline react readily with **oxygen** from the surrounding air. Their oxidation changes the fuel chemically and may produce:

- **Heavy, gummy or resin-like deposits**; and
- **Corrosive compounds**.

To limit this deterioration, aviation gasoline includes **oxidation inhibitors**. These additives reduce the tendency of unstable hydrocarbons to react with oxygen while the fuel is stored or handled.

## 4. Sulphur Content

Sulphur and sulphur-containing compounds are undesirable in aviation gasoline because combustion can convert them into **sulphur dioxide**. When this product combines with moisture in the exhaust gases, **corrosive sulphurous acid** may form and attack the exhaust system.

The maximum sulphur content specified for aviation gasoline is **0.001%**. Keeping the sulphur concentration very low reduces the risk of corrosion associated with these combustion products.

## 5. The Combustion Process

The charge in a spark-ignition engine must burn at a **controlled rate**. Proper combustion is therefore a progressive burning process, **not an instantaneous explosion**.

The constituent proportions used for describing this process are:

| Substance | Composition |
|---|---|
| **Gasoline vapour, by weight** | **84.2% carbon**, **15.8% hydrogen** |
| **Air** | **78% nitrogen**, **21% oxygen**, **1% other inert gases** |

During combustion:

- Hydrogen from the fuel reacts with oxygen and produces **water vapour (H₂O)**.
- Carbon from the fuel reacts with oxygen and produces **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**.
- Nitrogen and the other inert constituents do not actively participate in the chemical burning reaction. Nevertheless, they form a large part of the gas mass that becomes hot and expands to create **pressure energy** in the cylinder.

Nitrogen has a further important influence: by moderating the burning process, it prevents the extremely rapid temperature and pressure rise that would make the event too violent to extract useful piston work.

### 5.1 Flame Rate

After the spark lights the compressed charge, a **flame front** travels through the mixture. In normal operation it spreads smoothly at approximately **60–80 ft/s**, allowing cylinder temperature and pressure to increase progressively rather than suddenly.

The highest combustion pressure should occur **after combustion is completed**, ideally with the crankshaft approximately **8°–10° after top dead centre (ATDC)**. At this position, the piston has only just passed TDC and cylinder volume remains relatively small because of the **ineffective crank angle**. The resulting pressure can then act effectively on the descending piston.

If peak pressure occurred at or before **top dead centre (TDC)**, it would oppose the piston's intended movement and could produce a tendency for the engine to rotate backwards.

### 5.2 Variable Ignition Timing

Combustion takes a finite time, so the spark needs to occur **before TDC** if peak cylinder pressure is to appear at approximately **8°–10° ATDC**.

Although flame travel time is reasonably steady, crankshaft and piston speeds change markedly with engine rpm:

- **At low engine speed:** The ignition point must be **retarded**. Otherwise, the charge may finish burning too early and create peak pressure while the piston is still approaching TDC.
- **At higher engine speed:** The crankshaft turns through more degrees during the same burning interval. Ignition must therefore be **advanced**, initiating combustion sooner in crankshaft-angle terms while keeping the useful pressure peak in the desired position after TDC.

### 5.3 Variations in Flame Rate

Flame speed is not perfectly constant. It becomes **slightly faster** when the mixture is **richer**, or when **cylinder pressure is increased**.

Aircraft engines operating at high power require an **enriched mixture** to maintain stable combustion. Because that richer charge burns more rapidly, achieving maximum power may call for **slightly retarded ignition timing**, or simply avoiding any further ignition advance.

## 6. Anti-detonation Properties

Greater compression of the fuel–air charge before ignition can lead to a higher pressure during burning. Higher combustion pressure supports increased **engine output** and **thermal efficiency**.

The engine's **compression ratio** determines the compression pressure that can be developed, but usable compression is limited by the fuel's tendency to **detonate or knock**. A fuel that resists detonation better permits operating conditions that would be unsuitable with a lower anti-knock grade.

### 6.1 Detonation (Knocking)

**Detonation** is abnormal, unstable burning **after normal ignition has already begun**.

In ordinary combustion, the flame front moves steadily through the charge, warming and igniting the unburnt mixture just ahead of it. The last unburnt portion is known as the **end gas**. As burning progresses, that end gas is heated by the approaching flame and compressed by the expansion of the gases that have already burned.

If the temperature and pressure rise far enough, the remaining end gas can reach its ignition condition almost simultaneously and burn explosively. During detonation the reported flame rate may rise to about **1,000 ft/s**, compared with **60–80 ft/s** during normal combustion. The violence of the event depends partly on how much end gas remains when it ignites.

### 6.2 The Effects of Detonation

An end-gas explosion can damage an engine in several ways:

- **Piston damage:** Severe local heating may burn the **piston crown** and eventually cause it to collapse.
- **Combustion-chamber overheating:** High temperatures may split, distort or burn **valves**, and may burn **spark-plug electrodes**.
- **Mechanical shock:** The abrupt pressure increase imposes a severe **shock load** on internal engine components, potentially causing mechanical failure.
- **Loss of power:** The pressure rise occurs before the piston reaches the ideal crank position, creating **back pressure** that the piston must overcome instead of turning that energy efficiently into useful output.

### 6.3 Detonation and Diesel Engines

In the terminology applied to diesel-engine operation here, **detonation-type ignition** is a normal characteristic of compression-ignition combustion. Diesel engines are constructed to withstand the higher associated pressures. Their reciprocating and other mechanical components therefore tend to be **heavier**.

### 6.4 The Causes of Detonation

Anything that raises the charge temperature **before normal combustion** increases the possibility of abnormal end-gas ignition. Examples include supplying **heated induction air** for carburettor de-icing and excessive compression or temperature rise in a **supercharger**. A burning process that continues for too long also provides more opportunity for the end gas to reach its detonation conditions.

The main contributing factors are:

1. **Incorrect mixture strength.** When high power is demanded, the mixture must be sufficiently **rich**. With a fixed air quantity, adding fuel can permit a higher power setting without the onset of detonation; an unsuitable, weaker mixture raises the risk.
2. **Excessive charge temperature or pressure.** Conditions such as carburettor heat at high power, overheated cylinders, or **high boost combined with very low rpm** can overheat or over-compress the charge before it burns.
3. **Incorrect ignition timing.** Excessive advance starts combustion too early and increases the temperature exposure of the unburnt charge.
4. **Insufficient cooling or carbon deposits.** Carbon, sometimes called **coke**, can coat combustion-chamber surfaces and interfere with heat transfer. Cylinder-head temperature may then become excessive.
5. **Cylinder-head and combustion-chamber design.** Detonation becomes more likely if a flame must travel a long distance through hot gas. The chamber's dimensions, **spark-plug positions, valve positions, compression ratio and cooling arrangements** all affect this risk.
6. **Incorrect fuel grade.** Fuel whose anti-knock performance is below the requirement for a particular engine may detonate under operating conditions that the engine is designed to encounter.

### 6.5 The Recognition and Prevention of Detonation

Detonation can produce a hard **metallic knocking or “pinking”** sound. Rapid pressure waves strike the chamber walls and cause the characteristic vibration. In an aircraft, the sound may be masked by **propeller noise**; severe high-power detonation may therefore damage the engine before the pilot becomes aware of it.

Reducing the likelihood of detonation involves engine design as well as operating technique:

- **Shortening flame travel:** A compact combustion chamber reduces the distance and time needed for complete burning. **Two spark plugs** initiate two flame fronts and can shorten the combustion interval further.
- **Controlling where burning starts:** Positioning the initial flame near a hot area, such as the **exhaust valve**, can direct the unburnt end gas away from the hottest chamber region into a relatively cooler area.
- **Adjusting engine operating conditions:** For the **same power**, operating at a **higher rpm** with a **finer propeller pitch** can allow a smaller throttle opening. This reduces cylinder pressure, while the higher speed shortens the time during which the end gas is exposed to detonation-producing conditions.

The general principle is that detonation risk can be reduced by limiting **temperature**, **pressure**, or **time** available for uncontrolled end-gas ignition.

## 7. Fuel Quality Control

Fuel resistance to detonation is measured by comparing its behaviour against standardized reference hydrocarbons in a **special single-cylinder test engine**.

The two reference fuels are:

- **Iso-octane:** High resistance to detonation, assigned a reference rating of **100**.
- **Normal heptane:** Detonates readily, assigned a reference rating of **0**.

A candidate fuel is burned under controlled conditions and matched to a blend of the reference fuels producing an equivalent degree of knock. For example, if the reference blend is **95% iso-octane and 5% normal heptane**, the tested fuel is assigned an **octane rating of 95**.

The octane number therefore indicates the fuel's **anti-knock quality**, not simply the proportion of a single ingredient actually present in the fuel.

### 7.1 Weak- and Rich-Mixture Performance Numbers

Early comparison tests used the air–fuel mixture that produced the greatest tendency to detonate. That does not represent every engine operating condition: **economical, weaker mixtures** are commonly associated with cruise operation, while **richer mixtures** are needed for **take-off and climb**.

Aviation gasoline is therefore characterized by **two performance ratings**, sometimes termed the **performance number** or **performance index**. For **AVGAS 100LL**, the designation **100/130** means:

- **100:** Anti-knock performance under **weak-mixture** conditions.
- **130:** Anti-knock performance under **rich-mixture** conditions.

An engine designed for a particular minimum grade **must not be operated with a lower grade**, because the weaker fuel could detonate under the engine's required conditions. If the specified grade is unavailable, a **higher-rated** fuel is the acceptable substitution described here.

## 8. Fuel Additives

A small amount of **tetraethyl lead (TEL)** may be added to gasoline to increase resistance to detonation. Its anti-knock effect comes from suppressing the formation of **peroxides**, compounds that favour abnormal combustion.

Fuel formulations eventually achieved anti-knock performance above that of pure iso-octane. To compare these fuels, testing uses **iso-octane treated with TEL**. Values above the basic 100-octane reference cannot be represented simply as an iso-octane percentage, so **performance numbers** provide the higher-range rating scale.

For example, a grade marked **100/130** has a **rich-mixture performance number of 130**, although it is commonly referred to in practice as **100-octane fuel**.

### 8.1 The Advantages of High Octane (Anti-detonation) Ratings

A fuel with stronger anti-knock performance can make the following possible:

1. **Higher compression ratios**, which improve thermal efficiency and can reduce fuel consumption while increasing engine power.
2. **Higher induction pressures**, allowing a supercharger to increase the amount of charge admitted and produce more power from the same engine.

For a particular engine, output is related directly to the **mass of mixture burned per unit time**. Raising induction pressure increases the mass entering the cylinders, although the amount admitted still depends fundamentally on **throttle-butterfly position**.

## 9. Pre-ignition

**Pre-ignition**, also described as **running-on**, occurs when the fuel–air charge starts burning **before the spark plug provides its normal ignition spark**.

It is usually caused by a locally overheated point in the combustion chamber, for example:

- Glowing or **incandescent carbon deposits**; or
- Excessively hot **spark-plug electrodes**.

The resulting symptoms can include **rough running, continued running-on, and loss of power**. Unlike detonation, which is abnormal burning **after the intended spark**, pre-ignition starts from an unintended heat source **before the scheduled spark**.

## 10. Thermal Efficiency

Combustion releases substantial heat energy, but an operating piston engine cannot convert all of it into useful shaft work.

Major losses include:

- Heat passing into **cylinder walls** and **piston crowns**.
- Heat retained by **exhaust gases** that leave the cylinder while still at high temperature.
- Work expended in overcoming the engine's **internal friction**.

Even under favourable conditions, **slightly less than 30%** of the fuel's available heat energy is converted into **useful work at the propeller shaft**.

Very volatile fuels introduce additional operational losses and problems: evaporation from tanks; **vaporization on the low-pressure inlet side of the fuel pump**; pump **cavitation**, where vapour bubbles form around the impeller; and the formation of **vapour locks**. Their volatility can also increase susceptibility to **carburettor icing** when atmospheric conditions permit it.

## 11. Diesel Engine Fuel

Diesel aircraft engines may use **AVTUR**, a **paraffin-based aviation turbine fuel**.

Compared with AVGAS, AVTUR is:

- **More widely available**;
- **Less volatile**;
- **Less prone to fire**, making it operationally safer in the comparison described.

Aviation fuels are dispensed by **volume**, normally expressed in **US gallons, imperial gallons or litres**. Different fuels have different densities, so the same volume does not necessarily contain the same mass or energy.

| Fuel | Specific gravity |
|---|---:|
| **AVGAS** | **0.72** |
| **AVTUR** | **0.80** |

With the greater specific gravity of AVTUR, a given **volume** contains more fuel mass and therefore more energy in the comparison used here. For an **equal volume of fuel carried**, this can contribute to greater potential aircraft range than the corresponding AVGAS volume, subject to the engine and aircraft characteristics.
