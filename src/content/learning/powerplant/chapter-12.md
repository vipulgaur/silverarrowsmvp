# Chapter 12: Piston Engines — Propellers

## 1. Introduction

### Purpose of a Propeller

An aircraft propeller converts mechanical power supplied by the engine into **thrust**. Its rotating blades accelerate a relatively large quantity of air backwards. Under Newton's third law, the reaction to this rearward acceleration provides the forward force required to propel the aircraft. Compared with some other reaction-based propulsion methods, a propeller generally accelerates a larger mass of air through a smaller change in velocity.

### Blade Geometry

A propeller is made up of two or more aerofoil-shaped blades secured to a hub, which in turn is driven by the engine through the propeller shaft. As they revolve, the blades act like small rotating wings.

Each blade has a **root**, **tip**, **leading edge**, **trailing edge**, **chord**, and cambered aerofoil section. The chord is the straight reference line between the leading-edge radius and the trailing edge. Propeller terminology differs from wing terminology in two places: the forward-facing cambered side of the blade is traditionally the **back**, whereas the more nearly flat surface facing aft is the **face**, also called the pressure or thrust face. The rounded portion near the hub is the **shank**. The blade's attachment end, where a variable-pitch system connects to it, is the **butt**.

### Blade Terminology

Several geometric and aerodynamic quantities are used to describe how the revolving blade travels through the air:

- **Plane of rotation:** The plane traced by the rotating propeller.
- **Blade angle (pitch angle):** The angle between a blade section's chord and the plane of rotation.
- **Angle of advance / helix angle:** The angle representing the actual direction of the blade's motion through the air, formed by its rotational and forward velocities.
- **Angle of attack:** The angle between the blade's chord and the relative airflow.
- **Geometric pitch:** The theoretical forward distance a propeller would advance during one revolution if its blade followed an ideal helix without slipping.
- **Advance per revolution (effective pitch):** The real forward distance travelled by the aircraft during one propeller revolution.
- **Slip:** The difference between the theoretical geometric pitch and the actual forward advance per revolution.
- **Thrust:** The axial force produced by the rotating blade; **propeller torque** is the opposing aerodynamic turning moment.

## 2. Propeller Blade Angles and Aerodynamics

### Pitch, or Blade Angle

A blade's **pitch angle** is measured between its chord line and the propeller's plane of rotation. Small blade angles correspond to **fine pitch**, while a larger angle is described as **coarse pitch**. Changing the blade angle changes how much aerodynamic load the blade places on the engine.

### Angle of Attack

A rotating blade advances along a **helical path** because its motion combines propeller rotation with aircraft forward travel. The relative airflow is therefore determined by both **rotational speed** and **true airspeed (TAS)**. The chord is set at a small angle to that airflow; an ideal operating blade angle of attack is approximately **2°–4°**.

### Blade Twist or Wash-out

A point near a blade tip covers more distance in each revolution than a point closer to the hub; consequently, local rotational velocity increases with radius. If pitch angle were unchanged along the blade, the outer sections would tend to develop disproportionately high aerodynamic loads, making blade bending forces unnecessarily large. To spread useful thrust more evenly, blade angle **decreases progressively from root to tip**. This twist, or **wash-out**, allows blade sections at different radii to operate at a more suitable angle of attack.

## 3. Fixed Pitch Propellers

### Disadvantages

A **fixed-pitch propeller** has a blade angle established during manufacture and not adjustable in flight. The blade's angle of attack consequently changes when TAS or rotational speed changes, because these quantities alter the direction of relative airflow. At a given rpm, **higher TAS tends to reduce blade angle of attack**; at a given TAS, **higher rpm tends to increase it**.

### Propeller Efficiency

Extreme combinations of forward speed and propeller rpm can put a fixed-pitch blade outside its effective operating angle-of-attack range:

- During a **high-speed, low-power dive**, the blade angle of attack may fall close to zero.
- During a **low-TAS, high-rpm climb**, blade sections may reach a stalling angle of attack.

Neither condition provides efficient thrust. Since pitch cannot change to match airflow direction, one particular airspeed–rpm combination provides the best efficiency for a given fixed-pitch propeller. Efficiency is commonly in the region of **80%–90%** and is defined by the ratio of useful thrust power to the engine power driving the propeller:

$$
\text{Propeller efficiency (\%)}=\frac{\text{Thrust power}}{\text{Engine power}}\times100
$$

Because rpm on a fixed-pitch installation depends on **throttle setting and TAS**, a high-speed dive can overspeed the engine unless power is reduced. Conversely, at rest on the ground, the engine may be unable to reach its rated rpm even with the throttle fully open.

## 4. Variable Pitch (Constant Speed) Propellers

### Advantages over Fixed Pitch Propellers

On a piston engine, **manifold pressure (boost)** and **rpm** together establish the power setting. An installation with independent **power** and **rpm** levers allows these variables to be changed separately, improving the engine–propeller combination's efficiency, fuel economy and wear characteristics.

A **variable-pitch propeller** allows the pilot to select an rpm and the blade pitch needed to achieve it. Once an rpm is selected, a **Constant Speed Unit (CSU)** or **Propeller Control Unit (PCU)** automatically changes blade angle as TAS, manifold pressure or other operating conditions vary. Automatic governing is effective only while pitch remains between the unit's **internal fine and coarse stops**. Some variable-pitch installations can also **feather** their blades.

### Alpha and Beta Range

A variable-pitch mechanism may provide an angular range extending from **feathered pitch**, its most coarse position, through normal operating pitch to **reverse pitch**:

- **Alpha (flight) range:** Between **feathered** and **flight-fine** pitch.
- **Beta (ground) range:** Between **flight-fine** pitch and **reverse** pitch.

**Ground fine** is finer than flight fine and lies in the beta range. Reverse pitch extends beyond ground fine into a blade orientation that produces a braking effect instead of normal forward thrust.

### Actuation and Control

Variable-pitch mechanisms require both a force to **move the blades** and a control system to **select and hold the angle**. Pneumatic actuation is theoretically possible but not normally encountered; electrical actuation is comparatively rare. Hydraulic movement, typically using **engine lubricating oil**, is preferred. When greater force is required, a small **booster pump** incorporated into the CSU/PCU raises the supply pressure.

## 5. Single Acting Propeller — Principle of Operation

In a **single-acting** mechanism, the blades surround an engine-driven hub and a pitch-change cylinder sits in front of it. Boosted engine-oil pressure moves the cylinder's hydraulic piston, normally **rearwards toward fine pitch**. Linkages behind the piston act on offset pins at the blade butts, converting straight-line piston travel into blade rotation.

Movement in the opposite direction—toward **coarse pitch**—is supplied mechanically, usually by a spring, centrifugal counterweights or both. A few designs use compressed gas in place of the spring and reverse the direction of hydraulic operation. A combined spring-and-counterweight arrangement also allows the spring to carry the blades into **feather** after engine rpm has dropped and centrifugal force has weakened.

### CSU/PCU Functions — Single Acting

The governing valve has three functional positions:

1. **Feed oil to fine pitch:** Blade angle decreases and propeller rpm rises.
2. **Shut off oil flow (hydraulic lock):** Blade angle and rpm remain steady.
3. **Drain the fine-pitch oil to scavenge:** Springs/counterweights move blades toward coarse pitch and rpm falls.

## 6. Double Acting Propeller — Principle of Operation

A **double-acting** pitch-change unit uses hydraulic pressure on **either face of its piston**. The actuator can use the same mechanical links as a single-acting assembly or a cam-slot and bevel-gear mechanism to rotate the blade roots. Its larger front-mounted pitch-change cylinder has a sealed piston and sealed fore-and-aft linkages to keep the two hydraulic chambers separate.

Feeding **fine-pitch oil** to one side turns the blades finer; directing **coarse-pitch oil** to the other side makes them coarser. The opposite chamber drains as required. Because oil power is available in both directions, **centrifugal counterweights or spring assistance are not necessary**.

## 7. The Constant Speed Propeller — Operation

The pilot uses the **rpm lever** to select a speed. The governing unit then attempts to hold that speed despite variations in airspeed, altitude and power. Maintaining constant rpm requires a balance between **engine torque** and **propeller aerodynamic torque**.

- **On speed:** Actual rpm equals the chosen rpm, so no pitch correction is needed.
- **Overspeed tendency:** Engine torque is greater than propeller torque. The governor commands **coarser pitch**, increasing propeller blade loading and torque until the rpm falls to the selected value.
- **Underspeed tendency:** Propeller torque is greater than engine torque. The governor commands **finer pitch**, reducing propeller torque and allowing rpm to increase to the selected value.

When the desired speed returns, the governor progressively closes the relevant valve ports and stops pitch movement.

## 8. The Simple Constant Speed Unit

A **CSU** is driven mechanically by the engine, commonly through gearing at the front of the crankcase behind the propeller. The drive operates both the governor and a small pump which raises engine-oil supply pressure to a useful pitch-control level; approximately **120–200 psi** is described as suitable.

### Flyweights, Speeder Spring and Pilot Control

The drive rotates a set of **L-shaped centrifugal flyweights**. Increasing rpm forces the weights outward and creates an upward force on the central, **double-landed pilot/control valve**. A **speeder spring** produces a downward force opposing the flyweights.

The cockpit rpm lever adjusts the spring through a **rack-and-pinion** mechanism:

- Moving the rpm lever **forward** compresses the spring more strongly, encouraging the pilot valve to move downward and commanding a higher selected rpm.
- Moving it **rearwards** reduces spring compression and sets a lower rpm.

### The “On Speed” Condition

When selected and actual rpm agree, **upward flyweight force exactly balances downward speeder-spring force**. The central pilot valve is then neutral: its lands block both oil passages to the pitch-change cylinder. The trapped oil hydraulically holds the propeller at its existing pitch.

### The “Overspeed” Condition

If increased engine torque causes rpm to rise, the flyweights develop more centrifugal force and move outward. Their action **raises the pilot valve** against its spring, admitting pressure oil to the **coarse-pitch** side of the actuator and exposing the **fine-pitch** side to drain.

Blade pitch and angle of attack increase; the larger aerodynamic reaction raises propeller torque and slows the system toward selected rpm. As the speed error disappears, the flyweights return toward their balanced position, both ports close and normal **on-speed** operation resumes.

If propeller torque temporarily exceeds engine torque, speed drops and centrifugal flyweight force decreases. The speeder spring now pushes the control valve **below its neutral position**; the **fine-pitch** passage connects to pressure oil while the **coarse-pitch** passage opens to drain.

Finer pitch reduces blade loading, aerodynamic reaction and propeller torque, so engine torque again accelerates the propeller. As rpm increases, flyweight force returns to balance the spring, the valve moves back to neutral and both ports close. In ordinary governing, the valve movements and rpm corrections are **small and progressive**.

## 9. Propeller Control Unit — PCU

A **PCU** performs the governing function of a CSU but commonly incorporates additional controls for turboprop installations. Through a linkage to both the PCU and the **Fuel Control Unit (FCU)**, engine power and propeller speed are coordinated, allowing quicker acceleration without excessive stress on the turbine, compressor or other hot-section components.

### Mechanical Feathering Lever

An additional mechanical lever above the standard governor components can lift the control valve fully into its **feather** position when the engine's **high-pressure (HP) cock** is shut in flight.

### Valve Lift Solenoid and Piston — Autofeathering

Some turboprops automatically feather if a **low-torque signal** is received while **high power is selected**, reducing pilot workload during critical phases such as take-off or go-around. A PCU **valve-lift solenoid** is energized along with an electrically driven feathering pump. Feathering oil reaches the valve-lift piston and raises the governor valve into a fully coarse/feather command. Pressure then flows to the **coarse-pitch side** of the actuator while the fine side drains, until the blades reach an **internal feather stop**. In this edge-on position the blades produce essentially no forward or rearward aerodynamic driving force, so the propeller stops unless mechanically driven.

### Pitch Lock Solenoid — Ground Fine and Reverse Pitch

Many turboprops and certain powerful piston installations can move the blades to **ground fine** or into **reverse pitch**. Ground fine is a few degrees finer than the flight-fine limit. The PCU's **pitch-lock solenoid** permits pressure oil to operate an internal pitch-lock arrangement, removing the normal flight-fine restriction and allowing ground-fine or reverse blade travel. Ground-fine selection on mechanically linked gas-generator/propeller turboprops is treated separately from normal in-flight governing.

## 10. Feathering and Unfeathering

If an engine on a multi-engine aircraft fails, its governor may initially try to recover the lost rpm by driving the blades to **flight fine**. With the airflow now turning the propeller and engine, this **windmilling** condition can impose very high drag and yaw toward the failed engine. Continued windmilling also risks further engine damage or fire.

**Feathering** turns the blade sections substantially edge-on to the oncoming airflow, greatly reducing the drag-producing tendency and stopping or minimizing windmilling. A system for **unfeathering** is useful especially on training aircraft, where failed-engine or asymmetric-flight exercises may require an engine restart; it need not be used after a genuine mechanical failure.

### Centrifugal Latch (Feathering Stop)

A parked single-acting propeller is normally left at **fully fine pitch** with pressurized oil trapped in the fine-pitch chamber. During the hours after shutdown, this oil may gradually leak past close-clearance control-valve surfaces; the feathering spring would then move the blades toward feather, making the next engine start excessively demanding on its starter.

A **centrifugal latch** prevents unwanted feathering at low rpm. Its pins engage a mechanism on the rear of the pitch-change piston, restricting further movement after about **5° blade travel**. Latches typically engage below approximately **700 rpm** and release when centrifugal force increases during start and warm-up, usually as rpm passes that value toward about **1100–1200 rpm**. Oil pressure then restores the fine-pitch stop position. If the engine fails while rpm falls **below the latch release setting**, the latch can block intentional feathering; therefore the feathering action must be completed before rpm drops that far.

### Feathering a Double Acting Propeller

Without spring or counterweight assistance, a double-acting propeller requires **oil pressure** to feather. A protected reserve of oil, often separated within the dry-sump main oil tank, supplies an **electrically driven feathering pump**.

To feather, the rpm lever passes the coarse-pitch setting and enters the feather gate, lifting the PCU/CSU pilot valve fully. Oil is directed to the **coarse-pitch side** of the actuator while the fine-pitch side drains. Pressing the cockpit feather button energizes a **hold-on relay** and the pump relay. Once the piston reaches the feather stop, pressure rises and operates a **pressure-operated cut-out switch (POCOS)**. This interrupts the button hold-on circuit, the button releases and the pump stops.

### Unfeathering a Double Acting Propeller

The windstream can restart blade rotation once the blades are moved slightly away from fully feathered. The rpm lever is returned from the feather gate to a setting near that of the operating engine, commanding **fine-pitch supply** and **coarse-pitch drain**. The feathering pump is run by pressing the feather button until the propeller begins windmilling; the button is then pulled out manually against the hold-on mechanism to stop the pump. Engine restart continues under the aircraft's approved procedure.

## 11. Beta Range Operation

On certain turboprops, normal **alpha-range** governing is used during take-off at higher speeds, in flight and at the beginning of a high-speed landing rollout. **Beta range is for ground operation only**. During landing rollout, the **flight-fine stop** inside the pitch mechanism is removed or bypassed so the blades can move to **ground fine**. A cockpit lever—sometimes the throttle—commands the transition. Indicating lights confirm that the relevant propellers have reached the commanded range.

Ground fine produces a marked **aerodynamic braking effect**. Normal power control can be used during taxi and early take-off acceleration, and the flight-fine stop re-engages automatically as increasing TAS demands coarser pitch.

More recent systems may allow blade travel of approximately **+8° to −3°** in beta, with the negative end representing **full reverse**. The transition remains restricted to appropriate ground phases, while subsequent control may be coordinated through a **power lever with PCU governor override**. With power reduced, blades can move toward reverse so a lower blade angle provides additional braking. Increasing power then increases rpm without the governor opposing the commanded beta blade setting. A spring-loaded beta control valve returns the arrangement toward alpha governing when beta selection is removed.

## 12. Synchronizing

Differences in propeller rpm on a multi-engine aircraft create an audible **beat frequency** and increase vibration. A synchronization system compares the rpm of a **master engine** with that of one or more **slave engines**, then adjusts the slave governor setting until the rpm difference disappears.

In one system a **magnetic rpm pickup** on each engine sends a signal to a control unit. The controller powers a **torque motor** which alters the slave governor's speeder-spring setting. When both propellers turn at the same speed, the controller stops sending corrections. A **synchroscope**, often illustrated as small propellers on an indicator, may show differences by rotating when the engines are out of synchronization.

## 13. Synchrophasing

Synchronizing controls **rpm**, but it does not ensure that adjacent blades pass through particular angular positions relative to each other. **Synchrophasing** further reduces cabin and exterior noise by holding neighboring propellers at a favorable **relative blade phase angle**, thereby reducing the pressure-pulse interference of their blade tips. Some aircraft allow the pilot to fine-adjust phase for the quietest result.

## 14. Reduction Gearing

### Purpose

A powerful engine may need a relatively large-diameter propeller to convert its output efficiently into thrust. If the large propeller were directly coupled to a fast-turning crankshaft, **blade-tip speed** could become high enough to create compressibility effects and blade flutter. A **reduction gearbox** allows the engine to run at its appropriate high rpm while the propeller turns more slowly.

### Parallel Spur Gear

A gearbox with **parallel input and output shafts** is relatively straightforward and inexpensive, but requires more space at the engine's front. Such layouts have been used on **V-type, in-line, liquid-cooled engines**, including the **Rolls-Royce Merlin and Griffon**.

## 15. Torque Meter

### Purpose

A **torque meter** provides an indication of engine power transmitted to the propeller. Depending on the installation, it can display **lb.ft**, **N·m**, **percentage**, **psi**, or another calibrated power-related scale.

### Types and Operation

Two basic sensing methods are used:

- **Electronic sensing:** The torsional twist of an intermediate drive shaft varies with transmitted power. An electronic sensor detects the twist angle and converts it into a torque indication. This arrangement can be relatively light and reliable.
- **Oil-pressure sensing:** Axial thrust developed by a helical gear or the torque reaction applied to a stationary ring gear produces a mechanically regulated oil pressure, which the gauge interprets as torque.

In the ring-gear oil-pressure arrangement, the engine input shaft drives planet pinions around a gear intended to remain stationary. Torque reaction tries to turn this stationary gear backwards, against a restraint formed by **lever-operated pistons** acting in oil-filled cylinders. The oil comes from a **torque-meter pump**. One piston governs a **bleed orifice**. At lower torque, more oil can escape through the bleed opening. Greater torque attempts to rotate the ring gear farther and moves the pistons to restrict the bleed, raising oil pressure until the reaction force balances. The measured pressure therefore follows propeller torque.

## 17. Diesel Engines

Aircraft diesel engines characteristically operate at **lower rpm and higher torque** than many conventional gasoline piston engines. Their high torque can produce good **static thrust**, improving take-off capability. They may drive **constant-speed propellers** with more blades than comparable gasoline-engine propellers. Reduction gearing can match engine and propeller speeds more effectively.

Modern diesel installations frequently integrate fuel delivery and propeller control into a **single-lever** arrangement similar in concept to turboprop control. **FADEC (Full Authority Digital Engine Control)** coordinates fuel scheduling, blade pitch, torque monitoring and other parameters electronically.
