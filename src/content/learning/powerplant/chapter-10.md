## Indirect Fuel Injection

In many spark-ignition aircraft piston engines, fuel is supplied through a **low-pressure, continuous-flow indirect injection system**. Instead of admitting fuel through a carburettor, the system directs a steady spray of fuel into each cylinder's induction passage, positioned as near to its inlet valve as practicable. The fuel and intake air then enter the cylinder together.

This arrangement has several operational advantages:

- The injection equipment works with **comparatively low fuel pressure**.
- Supplying fuel at the individual inlet passages gives **good distribution between cylinders**.
- It avoids the particular icing difficulties associated with a carburettor.
- The fuel pump can deliver continuously; its action **does not need to be synchronised** with the engine's induction and combustion events.

Some installations retain the general air-metering principle used in carburettor systems, but introduce fuel into the intake under pressure rather than drawing it into the air stream through a carburettor discharge jet.

### Controlling Airflow, Fuel Flow and Mixture

The **air throttle** controls the quantity of air admitted to the engine. Its movement also operates a **fuel-metering valve**, which varies metered fuel pressure and delivery as engine airflow and speed requirements change. The two controls work together so that the fuel supplied is appropriate for the airflow being admitted.

The pilot can further modify mixture strength through a **manually operated mixture-control valve**. This changes the fuel-pressure conditions at the metering unit, allowing the air–fuel ratio to be adjusted for altitude and other operating requirements.

The continuous-injection arrangement does **not need a special idling circuit**. It also does not require a separate, dedicated priming installation for starting: the electric fuel pump can supply fuel through the normal injection equipment for priming.

### Principal Components and Indications

A typical installation includes:

1. A **fuel pump arrangement** supplying the pressurised fuel.
2. A **fuel/air control unit** coordinating fuel flow with the amount of intake air.
3. A **fuel manifold (distribution) valve** supplying the individual cylinder lines.
4. A **discharge nozzle for each cylinder**, delivering fuel into its induction port.

The system also contains the ordinary **throttle valve**, which regulates airflow, and a **fuel-pressure gauge**. By using the pressure indication with the appropriate calibration, the pilot can establish the required mixture for the prevailing altitude and engine power setting.

## The Fuel Pumps

Fuel delivery is deliberately greater than the engine's immediate consumption. A **recirculation route** carries away the excess fuel so that the supply can be maintained without requiring the pump to match instantaneous engine demand exactly.

Two pumps are connected **in parallel**:

- The **mechanically driven pump** provides the normal fuel supply while the engine is running.
- The **electrically driven pump** can supply pressurised fuel when the engine-driven pump is stationary or unavailable.

The plumbing allows the electric pump's output to **bypass the mechanical pump**. As a result, the electric pump can be used for **priming**, can assist with **starting**, and offers a fuel-supply option in an **emergency**.

## The Fuel/Air Control Unit

The fuel/air control unit is installed at the **intake manifold**. It incorporates three linked control elements:

1. **Air throttle assembly (throttle valve).**
2. **Throttle metering valve (metering fuel valve).**
3. **Mixture control valve.**

### Air Throttle Assembly

The air throttle butterfly is mechanically connected to the pilot's **throttle lever**. Changing its opening varies the amount of air reaching the engine. Unlike a carburettor induction system, this intake manifold does **not contain a Venturi or comparable airflow restriction** for drawing fuel into the stream.

### Throttle Metering Valve

The fuel-control mechanism is attached to the throttle assembly and includes a **metering fuel valve** linked to the air throttle. When the pilot moves the throttle, the associated metering valve changes fuel flow to the manifold distributor in relation to the air being admitted.

This coordinated action establishes the **basic air–fuel ratio** by keeping fuel delivery proportionate to airflow.

### Mixture Control Valve

A second valve responds to the pilot's **mixture lever**. It modifies the pressure acting on the metering system by **bleeding off fuel pressure**. This permits the pilot to make the fuel supply richer or weaker than the basic throttle-metering setting when required by the operating conditions.

### Fuel-Pressure Indication

The **fuel-pressure gauge** displays **metered fuel pressure**, not merely an arbitrary supply reading. When the gauge is calibrated for the installation, its indication can be used to adjust mixture according to the selected **engine power** and **altitude**.

## The Fuel Manifold Valve

Mounted on the **engine crankcase**, the manifold valve serves as the central distribution point for the fuel that has already passed through the metering system.

Its ports operate together:

- **Engine stopped:** With no delivery pressure, **all the outlet ports remain closed**, preventing fuel from passing into the injection lines.
- **Pressure established:** Rotation of the engine or operation of the electric booster pump causes fuel pressure to rise. The distributor then **opens all the nozzle outlets at the same time**, allowing fuel to reach each cylinder.

A **ball valve** makes sure that the distribution ports are **completely open before fuel begins to pass through them**.

## The Discharge Nozzle

Each engine cylinder has its own **discharge nozzle**, fitted in the **cylinder head** and pointed towards the **inlet port**. The spray is therefore delivered into the intake passage rather than being injected directly into the combustion chamber.

Discharge nozzles are manufactured with different **calibration ranges**. For a particular engine, they are selected and installed **as a matched set**, with the **same calibration for every nozzle** in that set. This supports consistent distribution of metered fuel among the cylinders.

## Diesel Engines

The basic **storage and transfer arrangement** for an aircraft diesel installation resembles that found in other light aircraft. **Rigid tanks in the wings** normally hold most of the fuel. The supply is carried towards the engine using the effects of **gravity and ram-air pressure** where applicable, passing through the fuel-system components in sequence:

1. A **common strainer**.
2. A **fuel selector valve**.
3. **Water traps**.
4. **Fuel heaters**.
5. **Filters**.
6. An **engine-mounted delivery system**.

Diesel fuel can be delivered to the cylinders using different injection arrangements. A preferred modern approach is **common-rail injection**, in which very high pressure is maintained close to the cylinders. A representative installation operates at approximately **1,800 bar (26,000 psi)**.

Injection is commanded electronically by a **dual-channel Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC)**. The FADEC opens the electrically operated injectors at the required **moment** and for the necessary **duration** to provide the demanded fuel quantity.

## Electronic or Common Rail Injection

An older diesel installation may use a **distributor-type injection pump** to deliver fuel to the injectors. A common-rail design replaces this arrangement with **one extremely high-pressure pump** feeding a shared pressure-storage manifold, the **common rail**. An example common-rail pressure is approximately **2,000 bar (29,000 psi)**.

The common rail holds the pressurised fuel and sends it to the individual **computer-controlled injector valves**. Those valves can be operated by:

- **Solenoids**, using electromagnetic actuation; or
- **Piezoelectric actuators**, used in more recent designs.

In installations such as the **DA40**, the **FADEC** determines both the **timing of injection** and the **quantity of fuel injected** during each injection event. Its decisions are informed by several engine and environmental inputs, including **air temperature**, **air density**, and **throttle position**.

High-performance injector actuators, combined with electronic timing and fuel-metering control, allow **more efficient fuel use** and **more effective management of engine power**.
