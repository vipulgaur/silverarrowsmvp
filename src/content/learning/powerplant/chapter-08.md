# Chapter 8: Piston Engines — Carburettors

## 1. The Basic Requirements of a Carburettor

A carburettor prepares the fuel–air charge needed by a spark-ignition piston engine. It must meter fuel in relation to the air entering the engine, rather than deliver fuel at an unchanging rate.

A satisfactory carburation system must meet three operating requirements:

1. **Appropriate mixture at every power setting.** The fuel-to-air proportion must respond to the throttle from idle to full power. The system must also accommodate changes in airflow when the engine accelerates or decelerates.
2. **Operation throughout the approved environment.** Fuel metering must remain effective across the aircraft's operating range of temperatures and altitudes.
3. **Starting and shutdown provisions.** The arrangement must permit the engine to start readily and may include a positive means of stopping fuel delivery when the engine is shut down.

### 1.1 Comparison of fuel-metering arrangements

**Float-chamber carburettor.** This is the least expensive and mechanically simplest arrangement, making it common on light aeroplanes. Its main disadvantages are a relatively high susceptibility to carburettor icing and changes in metering caused by some flight manoeuvres.

**Injection carburettor.** Fuel delivery can be measured more accurately, giving closer control of mixture ratio. Compared with a float-chamber carburettor, the system is less disturbed by manoeuvring and is less susceptible to icing.

**Direct fuel injection.** Distributing fuel at the engine rather than relying on a conventional carburettor gives particularly good distribution between cylinders. This arrangement is regarded as economical, is not affected by flight manoeuvres in the same way, and has comparatively little icing susceptibility.

These systems can incorporate **manual mixture control** so that the pilot can select an economical mixture for cruise. Fuel-flow instruments, fuel-pressure instruments and exhaust-gas-temperature (EGT) indications may also be provided to help select the required setting.

A **diesel engine does not require a carburettor**. Its induction system takes filtered air to the cylinders, but the incoming air is not controlled by the type of throttle used for a conventional spark-ignition carburetted engine.

### 2.1 The 'U' Tube Principle

If liquid is poured into both sides of a U-shaped tube, its level settles at the same height in the two arms when the pressure applied to each surface is equal. Creating a pressure difference makes the liquid move toward the region of lower pressure.

In a carburettor, one arm is enlarged to form the **float chamber**, which serves as a small fuel reservoir. A float operates a **needle valve** to admit replacement fuel and hold the level approximately constant. Fuel may reach the chamber from the main tanks under gravity or by means of the aircraft's fuel pumps.

### 2.2 The Venturi Principle

**Bernoulli's theorem** describes how the energy of a smoothly moving fluid is distributed. Pressure, temperature and velocity are linked: when the fluid's flow conditions change, the different energy components adjust.

A Venturi makes the intake passage narrower at its throat. Air travelling through that restriction speeds up, while its **static pressure and temperature decrease**. The resulting low-pressure region can be connected to the fuel reservoir by a discharge tube. Fuel is then drawn into the airstream because the pressure above the fuel in the float chamber exceeds the pressure at the discharge point.

In the simple carburettor:

- **Engine suction** moves air from the air intake, through the Venturi and into the induction manifold.
- The **throttle butterfly** sets the amount of air permitted to reach the engine. Opening the throttle raises induction-manifold pressure.
- **Metering jets** restrict and regulate the amount of fuel carried from the float chamber to the discharge point.
- Increasing airflow through the Venturi normally creates a larger pressure reduction at its throat and therefore a greater fuel-driving pressure difference.

The fuel reservoir is supplied by **gravity feed**, an **electric booster pump**, or an **engine-driven fuel pump**. The float and its needle valve regulate the inflow. On installations using pumps, a **fuel-pressure gauge** enables their operation to be monitored.

The pressure at the carburettor air entry, or atmospheric pressure as appropriate, acts on the fuel in the chamber. At the Venturi throat, static pressure is lower. The resulting differential moves fuel along the discharge tube and into the induction airflow. A larger pressure differential produces greater fuel delivery, while the **main jet's bore** determines how much fuel can pass for a given pressure difference. Jet size is therefore a factor in mixture strength.

Although these components provide workable fuel metering, a carburettor consisting only of the basic arrangement can maintain a satisfactory air–fuel mixture over a **limited range of conditions**. Several additional circuits are needed for reliable operation across the full power range.

## 3. Modifications to the Simple Carburettor

### 3.1 The Pressure Balance Duct

For predictable metering, the air pressure applied to the surface of fuel in the float chamber needs to reflect the pressure present at the **carburettor air intake**. A vent hole opening to the outside atmosphere cannot consistently achieve this. Local pressure around the intake varies with aircraft speed and manoeuvres, and those changes may not be transmitted to a chamber vented elsewhere.

A **pressure-balance duct** provides a direct passage from the float chamber to the intake. The two regions therefore experience appropriately matched pressure changes, preserving the intended pressure difference across the main fuel jet. The same duct can also provide air to a diffuser and, on certain installations, form part of the altitude-mixture-control arrangement.

### 3.2 The Diffuser

A simple main jet tends to deliver a progressively **richer mixture** as the engine's speed and the airflow through the Venturi rise. The reason is that fuel and air do not respond to flow changes in exactly the same way.

One correction is a **diffuser well**. As the engine accelerates away from idle, fuel in the well falls to a lower level. The falling level uncovers a succession of holes through which air is admitted into the fuel-discharge passage. The extra air reduces the effective pressure difference driving fuel flow and therefore opposes the tendency of the mixture to become increasingly rich.

Mixing this air with the fuel before discharge also assists its **vaporization**, particularly when the engine is operating at low speed.

### 3.3 The Principle of the Air Bleed Diffuser

Suction applied to a tube whose lower end is under liquid will lift a **continuous column** of liquid. If a small opening is made in the tube, air can enter under the same suction conditions. The liquid then travels mixed with small air bubbles rather than as an unbroken column.

This process is called **air bleeding** or **diffusion**: air is deliberately admitted to the fuel flow, helping break the fuel into smaller portions. It also weakens the suction acting solely on the liquid and restricts the amount of liquid drawn through the tube. This is the operating principle used by an air-bleed diffuser.

## 4. Slow Running Systems

At idle or very low engine speed, only a small amount of air enters the induction system. The depression at the main Venturi throat is then too weak to draw sufficient fuel through the **main jet**. A separate low-speed delivery circuit supplies the fuel instead.

The almost-closed throttle creates a substantial pressure drop near and beyond its edge. A **slow-running passage**, with its own carefully sized jet, runs from the float chamber to an outlet near the **lip of the throttle valve**. The pressure difference at that outlet draws fuel from the float chamber even while airflow through the main Venturi remains low.

The slow-running circuit includes the following details:

- The **slow-running jet** is calibrated to produce the comparatively **rich mixture** required for idling.
- An **air bleed** opening into the choke tube below the throttle helps atomize the fuel.
- A **cross-drilled passage through the throttle butterfly** spreads the mixture across the induction-manifold area.
- A small hole entering that cross-passage from the choke-tube side admits air, mixing it with fuel drawn through the throttle passage before delivery to the engine.

As the throttle is opened, suction at its lip decreases but airflow, and therefore depression, at the Venturi throat increase. The **main metering jet begins operating** as the slow-running fuel flow declines. Both circuits need to be adjusted so that this transfer is gradual. If the transition is poorly matched, a temporary lean condition can cause a **flat spot**—an engine hesitation or failure to respond smoothly during acceleration.

### 4.1 Slow-running fuel cut-off

A shut-off valve is commonly fitted in the idle delivery passage. When the pilot operates this valve, the passage closes and the engine's mixture becomes progressively weaker until combustion stops. The fuel cut-off may have its own cockpit control or form part of the mixture-control lever.

Stopping in this manner helps prevent two undesirable conditions:

- **Continued irregular firing** due to pre-ignition after an attempted shutdown.
- **Liquid fuel condensation inside cylinders**, which could wash lubricating oil off the cylinder walls and reduce lubrication during the next start.

## 5. Mixture Control

Climbing into less dense air reduces the **mass of air** entering the cylinders for a given intake velocity. The decrease in ambient density also reduces the Venturi's pressure drop, but the consequent reduction in **fuel delivery** does not occur in exactly the same proportion as the reduction in air mass. Without correction, the mixture becomes richer with increasing altitude, wasting fuel during economical cruise.

Mixture controls allow the pilot to reduce fuel delivery so that the fuel–air ratio can be adjusted to the conditions.

### 5.1 Needle Type

With a **needle-operated mixture control**, the cockpit lever moves a tapered needle in the fuel passage from the float chamber to the main jet. Moving the needle changes the area of the metering orifice and therefore changes how much fuel can flow.

In the arrangement illustrated, moving the needle fully down **closes the fuel supply to the main jet**, providing an engine shut-off function.

Since the **fuel jet is the smallest opening in the fuel system**, contamination can obstruct it. A **fuel strainer** may therefore be installed in the fuel line immediately upstream of the jet or jets.

### 5.2 Air Bleed

An alternative way to change mixture is to alter the **air pressure acting on fuel in the float chamber**. This varies the pressure difference between the float chamber and the Venturi and therefore varies the rate of fuel discharge.

A small passage from the float chamber toward the Venturi reduces pressure in the chamber. A cockpit-controlled valve also controls the admission of external air:

- With the admission valve **fully open**, float-chamber air pressure is at its greatest and the mixture is **fully rich**.
- As the valve is **closed**, chamber pressure falls. Reduced pressure difference through the main jet means **less fuel flows**, producing a weaker mixture.
- Moving the control to **idle cut-off** connects the float-chamber airspace with a suction pipe on the engine side of the throttle. The large depression reduces float-chamber pressure enough to stop fuel delivery and shut the engine down.

## 6. Power Enrichment

The leaner settings suitable for economical cruise are not appropriate at power outputs **above the cruise range**. A **richer mixture** is then required to help prevent **detonation**. Carburettors obtain this richer mixture either by adding another fuel feed or by arranging for the basic system to be rich at high power and reducing float-chamber pressure during cruise to make the mixture leaner.

### 6.1 Power Enrichment or Economizer Jet

A **power-enrichment jet**, also called an **economizer jet**, adds fuel as the throttle approaches high-power settings. An extra needle valve linked to the throttle remains completely closed at throttle settings below those needed for **maximum cruising power at sea level**. Above that setting, the valve progressively opens; at **full throttle**, it is fully open and supplies additional fuel.

On some engines the enrichment valve does not follow throttle movement directly. Instead, a **sealed bellows operated by manifold pressure** controls it, allowing the additional fuel to correspond more closely to the engine's actual power requirement.

### 6.2 The Back Suction Economizer

A **back-suction economizer** uses pressure changes downstream of the throttle to regulate pressure in the float chamber. At a high-power setting, pressure close to the open throttle is only slightly below atmospheric pressure. There is little suction applied to the float chamber, so the main jet can deliver a **rich high-power mixture**.

When the throttle is moved toward its cruising setting, the faster flow near the partly closed throttle creates a stronger local depression. An **economizer passage and its air jet** convey that depression to the float-chamber airspace. Chamber pressure falls and fuel delivery through the main jet decreases, providing the **leaner mixture required for economical cruise**.

## 7. The Accelerator Pump

Airflow through a carburettor responds almost immediately when the pilot moves the throttle open. Fuel delivery responds more slowly. Consequently, a sudden throttle opening initially adds air faster than fuel, causing the mixture to become temporarily **too weak**. This produces the acceleration hesitation called a **flat spot**, or in a more severe case a **weak cut**.

A throttle-linked **accelerator pump** prevents this by injecting an extra quantity of fuel into the Venturi each time the throttle is opened. The injection bridges the interval until the normal metering circuit has increased its fuel flow enough to match the new airflow.

Certain accelerator pumps include a **calibrated bypass or bleed around the pump piston**. If the throttle is opened slowly, fuel can pass through the bleed instead of producing a large additional discharge. Other designs incorporate a **delayed-action plunger** so that an additional measured amount of fuel continues to reach the engine for several seconds after the throttle has stopped moving.

## 8. Priming

A **priming pump** introduces starting fuel into the **induction manifold near an inlet valve**, helping the engine start before normal carburettor fuel metering is established. When an aircraft has no dedicated priming arrangement, the permitted starting procedure on certain aircraft may use several throttle movements to operate the accelerator pump and provide priming fuel.

**Repeated throttle pumping should not be used outside a procedure that specifically permits it**, because fuel accumulation in the intake can increase the likelihood of a **carburettor fire**.

### 8.1 Fuel delivery and priming in a light aeroplane

A simple single-engine light-aircraft arrangement has **rigid fuel tanks in the wings**, refuelled by the **overwing method**. Fuel is drawn from the selected tank by a mechanical or electric pump and passes through the **tank selector** and a **filter or strainer** before entering the carburettor. Pumps may be of the **gear** or **vane** type.

For priming, a separate hand-operated **priming pump** can take fuel from the filter housing and send it directly to the inlet manifold. The fuel system incorporates **fuel quantity and pressure indications** for monitoring. Tank drains enable accumulated water to be removed before flight.
