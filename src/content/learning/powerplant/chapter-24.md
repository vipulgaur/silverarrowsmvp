# Chapter 24: Gas Turbines — Auxiliary Power Units and Engine Starting

## The Auxiliary Power Unit (APU)

An aircraft needs electrical, hydraulic and pneumatic services even when its main propulsion engines are shut down. As aircraft systems became more complex, an independent power source became increasingly important, particularly during ground operations. The **auxiliary power unit (APU)** supplies power for these services without requiring the main engines to run. It also reduces reliance on external equipment provided by an airport.

A compact **gas turbine engine** is particularly suitable for this role because it can deliver substantial power with relatively little weight. An APU can normally draw fuel from the aircraft's existing fuel system, avoiding the need to provide an entirely separate fuel installation.

A common configuration is a **free-turbine turboshaft engine**. This arrangement offers several advantages:

- A relatively small, lightweight turbine can produce power in the region of **600 horsepower**.
- A turbine mechanically separate from the gas-generator compressor can drive an **accessory gearbox**. Changes in the loads connected to this turbine therefore do not directly impose the same load changes on the compressor.
- The free turbine is normally governed to run at a **constant speed**. A generator driven by it can consequently produce electricity at a constant frequency without a separate constant-speed drive.
- Pneumatic services may be supplied by taking compressed air directly from the APU compressor. More commonly, the free turbine turns a separate **load compressor** that supplies compressed air to the aircraft systems.

## APU Operations in Flight

Although the APU was originally intended mainly for ground use, it was later developed to operate during flight. It can provide an **alternative source of system power** if a main engine fails. This capability became especially significant for twin-engine aircraft operating long routes under **Extended Twin Operations (ETOPS)** requirements.

APU design gives particular importance to **simplicity, durability and reliability**. It may need to start while the aircraft is already at a considerable altitude, and then continue carrying electrical or pneumatic loads at an altitude higher than its maximum starting altitude.

As an example of the differing limits, the **Lockheed L-1011 TriStar** APU was described as capable of:

- **In-flight starting up to 25,000 ft**.
- **Delivering power up to 31,000 ft**.

Thus, the altitude at which an APU can remain operational and supply power need not be identical to the altitude at which it can be started.

## APU Control and Operation

Compared with a main propulsion engine, the APU normally needs fewer cockpit controls and provides a smaller set of flight-deck indications. The available indications can include **compressor rotational speed**, **turbine gas temperature** and **system-fault warning lights**.

Automatic monitoring equipment plays a central role in protecting the unit. Sensors can initiate an automatic shutdown when a **fire**, **system malfunction**, **operating-limit exceedance** or **overload** is detected. The automatic shutdown system consequently serves a protective governing function.

An APU air inlet can have a **single-entry or double-entry** arrangement. It commonly incorporates a motor-driven inlet door. Selection of the APU master switch causes this door to open; following shutdown, it closes automatically after the necessary cooling period. Because a gas turbine relies on airflow, its available power decreases with increasing altitude as the **density of the air falls**.

### APU Location and Typical Use

The APU is generally installed where its exhaust and operation present minimal risk to personnel working around the aircraft. The **tail section** is a common location.

Starting is usually carried out with an **electric starter supplied by the aircraft battery**. Once running, the APU is permitted to settle at suitable **speed and temperature** before electrical, hydraulic or pneumatic loads are applied. Although the unit may not be capable of supplying every system simultaneously, it can provide the services necessary for safe aircraft operation.

Common periods of operation include **engine starting and taxiing on the ground**, as well as selected in-flight situations following a main-engine failure. The APU is also commonly selected before landing.

## Ram Air Turbines

Some aircraft have a **ram air turbine (RAT)** as an additional emergency power source. It is separate from the APU and uses the aircraft's forward motion through the air to obtain power.

The RAT contains a turbine wheel driven by the **relative airflow** resulting from the aircraft's speed. Two arrangements are possible:

- An **internally mounted turbine** supplied with ram air through a controllable duct or valve.
- A turbine that is **extended into the external airstream** when emergency power is required.

The installation is generally designed to be **fail-safe**: loss of aircraft power can cause the RAT to deploy or be selected automatically. Its shaft drives a gearbox connected to an **electrical generator**, a **hydraulic pump**, or equipment providing the relevant emergency supply. The resulting power can maintain essential electrical services or the hydraulic systems required for primary flight controls.

## The Requirements of a Starting System

A gas turbine cannot begin operating merely by admitting fuel. Three conditions must be established to initiate combustion and allow the engine to accelerate:

1. **Compressor rotation and airflow:** the compressor/turbine assembly must first be turned so that air reaches the combustion chambers.
2. **Fuel admission:** fuel must be supplied to the combustion chambers.
3. **Ignition:** an ignition source must be available to light the air–fuel mixture.

A practical system also needs two independent operational capabilities:

- **Motoring without ignition:** the engine must be capable of being turned over with the igniters inactive. This is the **blowout**, **motoring-over** or dry-out cycle used to remove fuel after an unsuccessful or **wet start**, or to help cool an engine following a **hot start**.
- **Ignition independent of the starting sequence:** igniters must be capable of operating without the starter motor, notably for an in-flight relight.

## Starter Motor

The starter's task is to establish the initial rotation of the **high-pressure (HP) compressor**. The two common methods on civil gas turbines are the **air starter motor** and the **electric starter motor**.

All starter systems have a prescribed **duty cycle**: a maximum permitted period of continuous operation. Depending on the installation, the starter may then require a cooling interval before another start attempt. These time restrictions protect the starting equipment against overheating or damage.

## The Air Starter Motor

An air-turbine starter is widely used because it is **lightweight, straightforward to operate and economical**. It operates using a relatively **low-pressure, high-volume** supply of compressed air and is attached to the engine's **accessory gearbox**.

The normal preferred order of supply sources is:

1. **Aircraft APU**.
2. **Ground power/air-supply unit**.
3. **Cross-bleed supply** from an already-running engine.

Compressed air reaches the starter through an **electrically operated start valve**. Air enters the starter's turbine housing, spins the **turbine rotor**, and is discharged through an exhaust outlet. A **reduction gear** converts rotor motion into the required starter-shaft rotation, which passes through a **sprag-clutch/ratchet drive** to the engine accessory gearbox.

Fuel and ignition may be introduced together during the starting procedure, or ignition may already be selected when fuel is admitted. After the engine has begun turning, the **HP fuel cock** is opened. Successful ignition, or **light-up**, produces a rise in **exhaust gas temperature (EGT)** and causes the engine to accelerate more quickly.

Once engine speed has risen beyond **self-sustaining speed** to the specified starter-cutout point, the supply valve closes. As the engine advances toward idle, the **sprag clutch disengages**, preventing the running engine from driving the starter. Without this protection, the starter could overspeed and be destroyed by centrifugal loading.

Some installations also have a **flyweight cutout switch**. When engine speed rises slightly beyond the self-sustaining threshold, this switch interrupts the electrical supply holding the starter-air valve open, automatically ending the starting-air phase.

## The Electric Starter Motor

Electric starting was the original arrangement used on gas turbine engines. It remains in use on **smaller business/executive jets and helicopters**, but a conventional electric starter is less attractive on large modern engines because of its **weight**.

For smaller engines, a **starter/generator** provides a useful alternative. One machine performs two tasks, improving the usefulness of the installation relative to the mass of equipment carried.

A conventional electric starter is coupled to the **accessory gearbox**, allowing it to turn the compressor during start. Its drive normally incorporates an **automatic clutch-release mechanism**, often of a pawl-and-ratchet or related overrunning type. The mechanism has three functions:

1. It limits the amount of starting torque transmitted to the engine.
2. It allows the running engine to turn faster than the starter as engine speed rises toward idle.
3. It disconnects the starter drive once starter assistance is no longer required.

A known clutch problem is **crash re-engagement**. This may occur if the starter is energized again before the driven engine spool has slowed enough for proper engagement of the clutch components.

The gearbox arrangement for a **starter/generator** differs from that for a conventional starter. Since the machine must continue rotating after the engine starts in order to generate electricity, it must remain **permanently engaged** with the gearbox. Its electrical control circuitry is correspondingly more complex.

## Normal Start Cycle

### Air Supply and Starter Ducting

A typical twin-spool turbofan installation uses an **air-turbine starter on each engine**. Compressed air with **low pressure and high volume** can be obtained from the APU, a ground starting cart, or the opposite engine.

The starting arrangement uses the normal **engine bleed-air distribution ducts**, with the direction of flow through the relevant connections arranged so that air feeds the starter. **Non-return protection** prevents the starting air from flowing backwards into the engine compressor: protection is provided at the **low-pressure (LP) bleed outlet** and through the non-return feature of the **high-pressure (HP) shut-off valve**.

### Monitoring the Start

The two instruments requiring the closest attention are:

- **Exhaust gas temperature (EGT)**, which shows the thermal response to ignition and acceleration.
- **HP compressor rotational speed (N2)**, which confirms that the compressor accelerates through the expected stages.

Other indications that may be relevant are **fuel flow**, **LP spool speed (N1)**, **starter duct pressure** and any **start-valve warning light**. Together these readings allow the pilot to establish whether starting is developing normally.

### Starting Sequence and Engine Response

1. **Starter selection:** Operating the start control energizes the starter. At first, **fuel and ignition remain off**. The HP compressor begins rotating and moves air through the combustion chambers.
2. **Fuel and ignition introduction:** Once the compressor has reached the engine's specified starting RPM, fuel and ignition are selected. The relevant switch is held as required until the start has succeeded.
3. **Light-up:** Combustion should begin within the allowed interval—**around 20 seconds is given as a typical example**. EGT rises sharply because fuel initially accumulates in the combustion process. As that excess fuel is consumed, the rate of temperature increase moderates.
4. **Turbine assistance:** The combustion gases begin to drive the turbine, reducing the proportion of torque that must be provided by the starter and helping the engine accelerate.
5. **Increasing fuel flow:** The **fuel control unit (FCU)** meters increasing quantities of fuel as compressor RPM increases. As the mixture becomes relatively richer, a **second pronounced EGT rise** can be observed.
6. **Self-sustaining speed:** The engine reaches a speed at which it can continue to accelerate without starter assistance. For the example twin-spool engine, this occurs at approximately **30% N2**. The starter is deliberately kept engaged for a little longer to improve the likelihood of reaching idle smoothly.
7. **Starter and ignition cutout:** A speed-actuated switch, which may be associated with the N2 indication, can terminate starter and ignition operation. EGT reaches a starting peak; once airflow corresponds to the fuel flow required at idle, the temperature begins falling toward **idle EGT**.
8. **Ground idle:** After speed and temperature settle, the fuel/ignition start control may be released and the after-start checks completed. Indicative idle speeds for the example are approximately **60% N2** and **25% N1**.

These characteristic indications apply to a normal engine start **regardless of whether the starter is pneumatic or electric**.

## Operation of the Blowout Cycle

When a start attempt has introduced fuel but failed to ignite it, **unburnt fuel** can remain in the engine. Attempting another ignition without first clearing it may produce **torching**—a substantial flame emerging from the exhaust.

The **blowout** or **motoring-over** facility permits compressor rotation **without fuel or ignition**. Air passing through the combustion system removes or allows evaporation of the accumulated fuel before a further start is attempted.

In the type of modern turbofan installation described, the **air starter duty cycle may be about 3–5 minutes**. If light-up fails within the prescribed interval, the fuel and ignition control can be switched off while the starter continues rotating the compressor to clear the fuel. Any additional attempt must remain within the permitted starter duty cycle and applicable cooling requirements.

## In-flight Starting

Following an engine **flameout**, it may be possible to restart the engine using the airflow produced by its own rotation in flight. This is a **windmill relight**.

A windmill relight involves selecting **fuel and ignition without necessarily energizing the starter motor**. Successful light-up is assessed using the **EGT and engine RPM** indications. An increase in either indication can provide evidence that combustion has resumed.

## Starting Malfunctions

The principal indications used to recognize an abnormal start are again **EGT** and **HP-compressor speed**. Both require close attention throughout the starting sequence. The fuel-and-ignition control should remain immediately accessible until the engine's starting parameters have stabilized, enabling prompt termination if a malfunction occurs.

### The Hot Start

A **hot start** is an excessive temperature rise during engine starting. The EGT may initially follow its normal pattern, with the abnormally rapid approach to the temperature limit becoming apparent only several seconds later.

Because the temperature can increase very rapidly, the ability to **cut off fuel and ignition immediately** is essential. Even a small exceedance of the applicable EGT limit makes the engine unserviceable until the required maintenance action has been completed.

The underlying problem is normally **excessive fuel relative to the airflow available to support combustion and cool the turbine gases**. Contributing conditions can include:

- Throttles not set correctly at idle during preparation for starting, or moved away from that setting unintentionally.
- Inadequate engine rotation or acceleration, resulting in insufficient airflow.
- Partial engine seizure or restricted movement associated with ice.
- A **tailwind**, especially when attempting a second start while substantial **residual engine heat** remains.

### The Wet Start

In a **wet start**, the engine fails to light up even though fuel is being admitted. Typical evidence is:

- **No EGT rise** indicating combustion.
- Engine RPM increasing only to, and then remaining at, the speed achievable by the starter.
- **Fuel-flow indication** confirming that fuel is still entering the engine.

An unrecognized wet start can leave the combustion system saturated with fuel. If this fuel later ignites suddenly, it may produce severe **torching** at the exhaust.

The appropriate response described for this condition is to **close the HP fuel-and-ignition control** while allowing the starter to continue turning the compressor for the specified **motoring-over/blowout interval**, rather than simply stopping the motoring immediately. Another start is attempted only after the accumulated fuel has been cleared and the applicable starting limits have been satisfied.

### The Hung Start

A **hung start** occurs when combustion has started but engine acceleration stops **below self-sustaining speed**. The EGT is **higher than would normally be expected at the stabilized RPM**, although it may still be below the published maximum temperature limit.

Remaining in this condition can damage the engine. The **HP fuel cock must be closed**, and the cause investigated.

A common explanation is **insufficient airflow through the engine**, such as that caused by a **contaminated compressor**. With inadequate airflow, the combustion gases cannot supply enough turbine power to assist the starter and carry the engine through self-sustaining speed to idle. When starter assistance ends, the engine cannot continue accelerating as required.

## Engine Rundown Time

**Rundown time**, also called **spooldown time**, is the interval between closing the **HP fuel cock** and the engine finally ceasing to rotate.

The time taken by each engine to stop can provide a useful indication of mechanical condition. Comparing rundown times between engines, or noticing a change in an engine's normal rundown behaviour, may give early warning of a developing malfunction.
