## 1. The Aeroplane Structure and Flight Controls

An aeroplane is a power-driven heavier-than-air aircraft deriving its lift in flight chiefly from aerodynamic reactions on surfaces which remain fixed under given conditions of flight. Before entering the cockpit for Exercise 1 (Aircraft Familiarisation), every student pilot must understand the structural groups of the airframe: the fuselage, wings (mainplanes), empennage (tail unit), undercarriage (landing gear), and powerplant.

![Fig-01-01: Principal Components of the Training Aeroplane](Cutaway diagram of a single-engine training aeroplane showing fuselage, mainplane, ailerons, flaps, fin, rudder, tailplane, elevator, undercarriage, and nose cowling)

```
PRIMARY CONTROL SURFACE      COCKPIT INPUT            AXIS OF ROTATION     PRIMARY MOTION
Ailerons (Outboard Wing)     Control Column Left/Right Longitudinal Axis    Roll
Elevator / Stabilator        Control Column Fore/Aft   Lateral Axis         Pitch
Rudder (Vertical Tail)       Rudder Pedals Left/Right  Normal (Vertical)    Yaw
```

## 2. Cockpit Layout and Flight Instruments

The cockpit is organised logically so that primary flight instruments sit directly ahead of the pilot-in-command in the standard Basic-T arrangement, while engine gauges, electrical switches, and avionics occupy the centre and right-hand panels.

![Fig-01-02: Standard Six-Pack Flight Instrument Panel](Annotated cockpit instrument panel showing Airspeed Indicator, Attitude Indicator, Altimeter, Turn Coordinator, Heading Indicator, and Vertical Speed Indicator)

## 3. Primary Flying Controls and Trim Systems

Moving the control column or rudder pedals deflects the primary aerodynamic surfaces at the extremities of the airframe. Trim tabs relieve the pilot of continuous control pressures during sustained flight attitudes.

![Fig-01-03: Primary Control Linkages and Trim Tab Operation](Schematic of control column, rudder pedals, cable linkages, elevator trim wheel, and balance tab aerodynamic forces)

## 4. Ancillary Controls, Flap Selector and Carburettor Heat

In addition to throttle and mixture controls on the quadrant, the pilot manages carburettor heat (or alternate air), trailing-edge flaps, parking brake, and cabin ventilation controls.

![Fig-01-04: Throttle Quadrant, Mixture and Carburettor Heat Controls](Detail view of cockpit pedestal showing throttle lever, friction nut, rich-to-idle cut-off mixture control, carburettor heat plunger, and flap selector)

## 5. Fuel System Management and Tank Selection

Understanding fuel tank capacity, usable fuel quantity, fuel grade/colour (AVGAS 100LL dyed blue), tank venting, and the fuel selector valve is essential prior to engine start.

![Fig-01-05: Training Aeroplane Fuel System Schematic](Fuel system diagram showing left and right wing tanks, fuel vents, selector valve, strainer drain, electric auxiliary pump, and engine-driven pump)

## 6. Electrical System, Alternator and Circuit Breakers

The electrical system typically operates on a 14-volt or 28-volt direct-current bus supplied by an engine-driven alternator and buffered by a main storage battery.

![Fig-01-06: DC Electrical Bus, Master Switch and Ammeter](Electrical schematic showing split Master Switch BAT/ALT halves, bus bar, circuit breakers, ammeter, and avionics master switch)

## 7. Vacuum and Pitot-Static Systems

The Airspeed Indicator (ASI), Altimeter, and Vertical Speed Indicator (VSI) rely on pitot and static pressure lines, whereas gyroscopic instruments rely on either a vacuum pump or electrical power.

![Fig-01-07: Pitot-Static Pressure Lines and Gyro Supply](Diagram of pitot head, static vent, alternate static source, ASI, Altimeter, and VSI pressure capsules)

## 8. Undercarriage, Brakes and Nosewheel Steering

Training aeroplanes commonly feature a tricycle undercarriage with differential toe brakes mounted on the top of the rudder pedals and a steerable nosewheel linked to the rudder pedal assembly.

![Fig-01-08: Tricycle Undercarriage and Differential Toe Brakes](Illustration of oleo-pneumatic nose strut, main gear spring struts, hydraulic disc brake callipers, and rudder pedal toe brake master cylinders)

## 9. Cockpit Checklists and Standard Operating Procedures

Checklists ensure that vital actions are completed in a logical flow and verified systematically rather than relying on memory alone during high-workload phases.

![Fig-01-09: Cockpit Scan Flow and Checklist Discipline](Cockpit flow pattern diagram tracing floor-to-panel-to-overhead scan sequence used before engine start and prior to take-off)

## 10. Airframe Documents and Technical Log Verification

Before accepting an aircraft for flight, the commander must inspect the Technical Log, Certificate of Airworthiness, Airworthiness Review Certificate, mass and balance schedule, and deferred defect entries.

## 11. Post-Flight Securing and Control Locks

After shutdown, the pilot records flight times in the technical log, fits the external pitot cover and control column gust lock, verifies all switches are OFF, and secures chocks and tie-downs as required.
