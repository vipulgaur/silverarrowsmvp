# Chapter 16 — Aircraft Fuel Systems

# 1\. What an Aircraft Fuel System Must Achieve

A fuel system is more than a place to store fuel. Its complete job is to **carry a usable fuel supply, deliver it continuously to the engine, and support safe operation throughout the flight envelope**. The fuel itself must also be compatible with the engine and remain usable in the environmental conditions likely to be encountered.

An ideal aviation fuel would combine several desirable characteristics:

* **Reliable flow:** It must remain capable of passing through tanks, lines, valves, filters and pumps across the operating temperature and altitude range.
* **Effective combustion:** It should burn as completely and consistently as possible under the engine's intended operating conditions.
* **High energy content:** A large calorific value allows useful energy to be obtained from the fuel carried.
* **Resistance to corrosion:** The liquid should not attack the fuel system's materials.
* **Harmless combustion products:** Burning the fuel should not produce deposits or other products that damage the engine.
* **Reduced fire risk:** Storage and handling hazards should be as low as practicable.
* **Good starting characteristics:** The fuel must allow dependable engine starting.
* **Lubricity:** Fuel-system components that depend on fuel for lubrication need sufficient protection against wear.

These goals can conflict. Achieving every desirable property simultaneously would be excessively expensive, so the fuel specification represents a **practical compromise** between performance, safety, availability and cost.

# 2\. Fuels Used by Piston-Engine Aircraft

## 2.1 Aviation Gasoline — AVGAS

Piston-engine aeroplanes normally burn **aviation gasoline (AVGAS)** rather than turbine kerosene. AVGAS is produced to tightly controlled specifications to make its combustion and handling behaviour suitable for certificated aviation engines. The textbook identifies **DERD 2485** as a gasoline specification.

The grade or performance number relates to the fuel's **anti-knock capability**. An engine operating with a high compression ratio and demanding combustion conditions normally requires a fuel with a correspondingly high anti-knock rating.

### Common grades in the textbook

|Grade|Performance number|Identification colour|Nominal specific gravity|Lead classification|
|-|-|-|-:|-|
|**AVGAS 100LL**|100/130|Blue|0.72|Low lead|
|**AVGAS 100**|100/130|Green|0.72|High lead|
|**AVGAS 115**|115/145|Green|0.72|High lead|

**Important distinction:** **100LL and 100 have the same stated performance number (100/130), but not the same dye colour or lead classification.** The source identifies 100LL as blue and 100 as green. A fuel's colour is therefore a useful identification aid, not a substitute for reading its grade marking.

## 2.2 Motor Gasoline — MOGAS

**MOGAS** means motor gasoline. It may be approved for particular combinations of airframe and engine, but that permission is conditional: ordinary road fuel is **not automatically acceptable** for any aeroplane.

The textbook links permitted use to **CAP 747 GC2** and directs readers to **CAA Safety Sense Leaflet 4a**. It also identifies two important limitations:

* The **lower octane rating** must be considered against the engine's requirements.
* The fuel's **greater volatility** increases the likelihood of **vapour lock** and **carburettor icing**.

Vapour lock interrupts a liquid-fuel supply when fuel vapour forms where the system needs liquid. Carburettor icing is a separate induction-system problem: both are important when evaluating the suitability of a more volatile fuel.

# 3\. Fuels Used by Gas-Turbine Aircraft

Turbine engines generally use kerosene-based fuels. The textbook distinguishes the commonly used **AVTUR** grades from a more volatile, wide-cut **AVTAG** fuel.

## 3.1 AVTUR — Aviation Turbine Fuel

### Jet A-1

* **Fuel family:** Kerosene-type turbine fuel.
* **Nominal specific gravity:** **0.80 at 15°C**.
* **Flash point:** **38°C**.
* **Waxing / freezing characteristic quoted in the book:** **−47°C**.

### Jet A

* Similar kerosene-type fuel with **SG 0.80 at 15°C**.
* **Flash point: 38°C**.
* The textbook gives a waxing point of **−40°C**.
* Its availability is described as predominantly **within the United States**.

The essential comparison is that Jet A and Jet A-1 have similar stated density and flash-point characteristics, but **their quoted low-temperature limits differ**.

## 3.2 AVTAG — Aviation Turbine Gasoline

### Jet B (wide-cut fuel)

* A mixture containing both **gasoline-range and kerosene-range fractions**.
* **Nominal SG:** **0.77 at 15°C**.
* **Flash point:** Can be as low as **−20°C**.
* **Waxing point quoted in the source:** **−60°C**.
* A **wider boiling range** than Jet A-1.

Jet B can serve as an alternative to Jet A-1 in suitable aircraft, but its **broader flammable range and greater volatility** introduce additional fire-handling concerns. Consequently, it is not the routine choice for civil transport operations described by the textbook.

### Fuel-property comparison

|Fuel|General type|SG at 15°C|Flash point|Quoted waxing point|Operational distinction|
|-|-|-:|-:|-:|-|
|Jet A-1|Kerosene|0.80|38°C|−47°C|Common civil turbine fuel|
|Jet A|Kerosene|0.80|38°C|−40°C|Mainly associated with US supply|
|Jet B|Wide-cut gasoline/kerosene|0.77|As low as −20°C|−60°C|More volatile; greater flammability hazard|

## 3.3 Appearance of Turbine Fuel

Unlike the coloured AVGAS grades listed above, **turbine fuel is not normally artificially dyed for grade identification**. Its natural appearance may be **clear/colourless through pale straw-yellow**.

## 3.4 What Cloudiness in a Sample Can Mean

A sample that looks milky, hazy or cloudy needs attention. The textbook provides a useful observation:

* **Entrained air:** The cloudiness tends to **clear upwards quickly**, toward the surface.
* **Water contamination:** The cloudy layer or droplets tend to **settle downward more slowly**.

A cloudy sample is commonly associated with **water in the fuel**. The direction and rate at which the haze clears are aids to interpretation, not permission to ignore a suspect sample.

# 4\. Fuel Additives and Their Functions

Aviation turbine fuel can receive additives during refining or at the aerodrome. Each addresses a particular weakness that may be important in aircraft operation.

## 4.1 FSII — Fuel System Icing Inhibitor

Fuel nearly always contains some water. At altitude, fuel cools, and its ability to retain water in solution reduces. Water can then separate as droplets, which may become **ice crystals** and obstruct filters or passages.

An **FSII** is intended to limit these icing problems. The textbook also describes a **microbiological/fungal suppressant** associated with FSII treatment:

* A fungus named **Cladosporium resinae** (rendered in the source as *Cladasporium Resinae*) can develop in turbine fuel when water is present.
* Growth may form **long green filaments**, potentially obstructing fuel-system items.
* The organisms' by-products can be **corrosive**, notably to fuel-tank sealants.
* Proper inhibitor treatment helps counter both icing and microbiological contamination.

## 4.2 HITEC — Lubricity Additive

**HITEC** is identified as a lubricity agent. By improving the fuel's lubricating ability it helps reduce wear in equipment such as **fuel pumps** and the **fuel control unit**.

## 4.3 Static-Dissipater Additives

When fuel moves rapidly through pipes and hoses, especially in **high-flow refuelling and defuelling operations**, electrical charge may accumulate. A static dissipater helps reduce the associated electrostatic hazard; it does not remove the need for normal bonding and fuelling precautions.

## 4.4 Corrosion Inhibitors

These additives limit corrosion of **ferrous components**, including the steel used in fuel pipelines and storage installations. Some may also improve the fuel's lubricity.

## 4.5 Metal Deactivators

Metals such as **copper** can accelerate fuel oxidation by catalytic action. A metal deactivator reduces this undesirable influence.

### Additive revision table

|Additive|Principal purpose|
|-|-|
|FSII|Restricts icing; the described formulation also addresses microbiological growth|
|HITEC|Improves lubricity and reduces component wear|
|Static dissipater|Reduces risks related to electrostatic charge|
|Corrosion inhibitor|Protects vulnerable metallic storage/handling components|
|Metal deactivator|Suppresses metal-catalysed fuel oxidation|

# 5\. Water Contamination and Low-Temperature Problems

## 5.1 Why Water Is Present

Small amounts of water can enter or remain in fuel because of conditions during **manufacture, storage, handling and transfer**. Tank atmosphere provides another source of moisture after the fuel is on board. The problem is therefore controlled rather than assumed to be completely absent.

### Draining settled water

Water is denser than aviation fuel. If the fuel is allowed to settle after replenishment, free water tends to collect **at the bottom of the tank**, where suitably located drains allow it to be removed.

### Heating turbine fuel

Fuel heating prevents **ice crystals from blocking the fuel filter**. The book describes two heat sources:

1. A heat exchanger using **hot compressor delivery air** to raise the fuel temperature and remove ice formed in cold, high-altitude conditions.
2. A **fuel-cooled oil cooler**, which transfers energy from hot engine oil to the fuel. This **heats the fuel while cooling the oil**.

### Minimising moisture-laden tank atmosphere

Filling a tank reduces the volume of moist air above the fuel and can therefore reduce water ingress. Filling every tank to capacity, however, has two drawbacks:

* **Thermal expansion:** Warmer conditions the next day can increase fuel volume enough for fuel to escape through the vent system.
* **Mass and performance:** The aircraft may be too heavy to carry its intended traffic load, making **defuelling** necessary.

## 5.2 Fuel Waxing

At very low temperatures, heavier hydrocarbon fractions may precipitate as **paraffin-like wax crystals**. These can restrict **filters** and disturb operation of the **fuel-control equipment**.

Two measures are identified:

* Keep heavy-hydrocarbon concentrations low during fuel manufacture.
* Use a fuel heater in the engine fuel system.

## 5.3 Fuel Boiling and Vapour Lock at Altitude

The boiling point depends on ambient **pressure**. As altitude increases, the pressure acting on fuel becomes lower, so fuel may boil and form vapour more readily. Vapour in a liquid-fuel line can block delivery to the engine, potentially causing flameout or stoppage.

**Tank-mounted booster pumps** address this by producing a **positive-pressure feed** to the engine rather than requiring the engine-driven pump to draw fuel through the entire low-pressure supply line.

## 5.4 Specific Gravity and Temperature

**Specific gravity (SG)** generally decreases when fuel temperature rises, because the same mass occupies a larger volume. The reverse occurs as fuel becomes colder.

For airline operations, it is fuel **mass**, not simply volume, that matters. Modern quantity systems can account for SG changes and keep the displayed mass consistent even when temperature alters the fuel's volume.

The textbook also notes that a **maximum governor** on certain gas-turbine installations can respond to changes in fuel SG, so changing to fuel of significantly different SG may call for an adjustment in accordance with the approved aircraft instructions.

**Relationship:** For a specified quantity of fuel, **mass = density × volume**. A rise in volume accompanied by a corresponding fall in density need not change mass.

# 6\. How Fuel Is Stored in an Aircraft

Fuel storage arrangements fall into three broad construction types.

## 6.1 Integral Tanks

An **integral tank** uses part of the aircraft's existing structure as the fuel container. Sealed areas may include:

* Wing structures.
* The wing **centre-section torsion box**.
* The **horizontal stabiliser**, on aircraft designed with such storage.

This arrangement offers a large capacity with **little extra tank weight**, because the structural shell already exists for other reasons. It is characteristic of large modern transport aircraft.

## 6.2 Rigid Tanks

A rigid tank is a separate sealed container, usually metal but sometimes made from composites. It must be supported and secured within or on the aircraft, so it introduces **additional mass and mounting requirements**.

Rigid tanks are especially common in **light aeroplanes**. Some installations carry them externally—for example, as **wing-tip tanks**.

## 6.3 Flexible Tanks / Bladders

A flexible tank is a sealed bag made from **rubberised fabric**. It depends on supporting aircraft structure to maintain its position and shape. The textbook particularly associates such tanks with **military applications**, where a properly designed flexible tank may have **self-sealing properties after combat damage**.

## 6.4 Tank Baffles and Installed Components

Fuel has substantial inertia. During acceleration, deceleration, sideslip or manoeuvring, a largely unrestricted mass of fuel can move rapidly across a tank and impose unwanted forces. **Baffles** break up that motion and reduce **surge/slosh**.

Some tanks also incorporate **one-way baffle check valves** that permit fuel movement **inboard** while restricting movement **outward toward the tips**. Fuel tanks commonly include:

* Vent connections and filler caps.
* Water drains and engine-feed lines.
* Fuel quantity gauging equipment.
* In larger designs: booster pumps, filters, pressure-refuelling valves and high-/low-level sensors.

**Do not confuse baffles with feeder-box/collector-tank functions:** baffles moderate bulk fuel movement; a collector tank preserves a usable local supply around its booster pumps.

# 7\. Basic Fuel Supply Systems in Light Aircraft

The complete airframe fuel installation has to deliver **more fuel than the engine can consume during its most demanding flight condition**, ensuring that the engine supply never becomes marginal merely because demand increases.

On many light aircraft, tanks are **rigid** and installed within the wings; replenishment takes place by **overwing filling** through filler openings in the tank tops.

## 7.1 Gravity-Feed Arrangement

A **high-wing** aeroplane may place the tanks sufficiently far above the carburettor for **gravity alone** to produce the required pressure at the **carburettor float chamber**. Such installations do not depend on a primary engine-feed booster pump for normal fuel movement.

The tank installation still requires selectors or shut-off provisions, gauging, ventilation, filtration, priming and contamination drains as appropriate.

# 8\. Additional Equipment on Multi-Engine Fuel Systems

Airliners operate at greater altitude, carry greater quantities of fuel, and must supply multiple engines under normal and abnormal conditions. Their fuel networks therefore require controls not found in the simplest light-aircraft layouts.

Large aircraft commonly use **integral wing tanks**, frequently with a **centre tank** in the wing centre-section torque box. Some aircraft also store fuel in the **fin or horizontal stabiliser**, both for increased capacity and possible **centre-of-gravity management**.

## 8.1 Tank Venting, Ram Air and Surge Tanks

The pressure above the liquid fuel must be able to equalise with surrounding air. Vent valves and vent piping provide this path. In certain installations ram air can help maintain tank pressure in flight, improving fuel-feed conditions and limiting the tendency for fuel to boil at altitude.

A **vent/surge tank** can catch fuel that enters the vent network and allow it to be returned to the main tanks. The textbook quotes a vent-space allowance of **2% of fuel-tank capacity under CS-23 and CS-25**.

## 8.2 Filters and Screens

Screens in tanks and filters along the system capture debris before it reaches booster pumps and downstream equipment.

## 8.3 Tank Booster Pumps

On the high-altitude aircraft described, each main tank normally houses **two electrically driven booster pumps**, typically **centrifugal pumps powered by AC induction motors**. They produce a **relatively high flow at approximately 20–40 psi**.

Their main roles are to provide adequate positive fuel pressure and prevent the **engine-driven pump from cavitating**. If both pumps in a main tank become unavailable, the aircraft's **Minimum Equipment List (MEL)** may impose a lower permitted operating altitude to protect fuel-feed reliability.

## 8.4 Collector Tank / Feeder Box

The booster pumps sit in a smaller **collector tank or feeder box** that retains fuel around their inlets despite aircraft attitude changes. The textbook gives **about 500 kg** as a typical retained quantity.

Keeping the pump submerged reduces loss of feed and cavitation. Some designs allow pump replacement without draining the complete wing tank.

## 8.5 Cross-Feed and Shut-Off Valves

The **cross-feed system** allows engines to receive fuel from a source other than their normal associated tank, including the possibility of feeding **both engines from one side**. Dedicated **shut-off valves** isolate fuel paths after a malfunction or emergency.

## 8.6 High- and Low-Level Sensors

* **High-level sensors:** Command the refuelling valve closed at the required tank level, preventing overfill during automatic refuelling.
* **Low-level sensors:** Protect the fuel reserve during dumping by controlling a minimum remaining quantity.

## 8.7 Drains, Baffles and Pressure Protection

* A low-point **drain** lets accumulated free water be sampled or released.
* **Baffles** limit rapid fuel transfer within the tank during manoeuvres.
* An **overpressure relief valve**, where installed, protects the fuel-tank structure if normal vent or pressure controls fail.

## 8.8 Outboard Fuel as Structural Relief

Certain wing tanks have distinct **inner** and **outer** sections. The design may retain fuel in the outer sections until the inner fuel has decreased to a selected quantity. Fuel mass retained outboard opposes part of the upward aerodynamic wing-bending load and can also contribute to reduced flutter susceptibility as described in the textbook.

# 9\. Twin-Engine Aircraft Fuel Management

## 9.1 Normal Tank-Use Sequence

The usual logic presented is to burn **centre-tank fuel first after take-off**, then rely on the wing tanks. Preserving fuel in the wings for longer helps reduce structural wing-bending stresses. When centre-tank booster pumps can no longer remove its remaining fuel, a **centre-tank scavenge system** can transfer the residual fuel into **No. 1 tank**.

## 9.2 Abnormal Feeding and Pump Failures

Cross-feed allows either side to support both engines. Some tanks also have **suction-feed valves**, permitting fuel delivery by gravity and/or engine-driven pump suction if the tank's two booster pumps have failed, subject to the aircraft's limitations.

## 9.3 Control and Indication

A representative twin-engine cockpit fuel panel includes:

* Individual **pump selector switches**.
* **Low-pressure lights** that may indicate pump failure or an exhausted tank supply.
* **Cross-feed switch and valve-position indication**.
* Tank-temperature sensing, in the example located in **No. 1 tank**.
* Indicators for a **low-pressure engine-filter bypass**, which can open when the filter becomes obstructed; the illustrated filter is downstream of the fuel heater.

## 9.4 Engine and APU Isolation

The **engine fuel shut-off valve** is closed using that engine's **fire handle** in the system described. Certain designs also close it using a normal engine fuel-control switch during start or shutdown.

The **APU** normally draws from **No. 1 tank** when no booster pumps are selected, using a bypass feed. With a suitable booster pump running, APU fuel may be supplied from other tanks. The APU's fuel shut-off valve commonly operates as part of its automatic start/stop logic.

## 9.5 Correcting a Fuel Imbalance

For the representative design, suppose No. 1 tank contains more fuel than No. 2. To reduce the difference:

1. **Open cross-feed** so both engines can be supplied from the higher-quantity side.
2. **Switch off the booster pumps in the lower-quantity tank** in accordance with the design's intended fuel-balance procedure.
3. Allow both engines to consume fuel from the tank with **more** fuel.
4. Once the desired balance returns, **reactivate the pumps** that were switched off.
5. **Close cross-feed** and return to normal **No. 1 tank → No. 1 engine / No. 2 tank → No. 2 engine** feeding.

This is an explanation of the textbook example, **not a universal cockpit checklist**. Individual aircraft procedures can differ.

## 9.6 Unusable Fuel

A pickup is not normally placed at the absolute bottom of the tank, where water or sediment may accumulate. As a result, some fuel cannot be reliably delivered to the engine and is defined as **unusable fuel**. Its certified quantity is given in the **aircraft manual**, not assumed from the amount visually remaining.

## 9.7 ETOPS Twin-Engine Arrangement

The more advanced installation shown in Figure 16.4 has these distinctive features:

* A **spar / low-pressure (LP) valve** stops fuel at the tank-system outlet.
* A separate **engine firewall / high-pressure (HP) shut-off valve** stops the supply close to the engine.
* A geometrically divided centre tank still functions as **one operational centre tank**.
* Two linked **cross-feed valves** provide redundancy and normally operate in coordination.
* The APU has an **automatic DC-powered fuel pump** that runs if the APU is required when main booster pumps are not operating.
* Following **dual-engine failure**, the DC pump can automatically run and an APU isolation valve opens to supply the **left engine**, assisting a rapid restart.

# 10\. Multi-Engine Fuel Systems and Jettison

A four-engine transport aircraft needs a network that can move fuel among several tanks and multiple engine-feed manifolds. The source illustrates an older layout including **stabiliser fuel storage**, transfer galleries, a centre tank and a **fuel-jettison system**.

## 10.1 Stabiliser Tanks and Protected Lines

A stabiliser tank may feed fuel **into a transfer gallery or a centre tank**. If pipes run through the **pressurised rear fuselage**, the source describes using **double-skinned lines**, reducing the chance of escaping fuel vapour entering the passenger compartment after a pipe leak.

## 10.2 Why Fuel May Need to Be Dumped

On some aircraft the **maximum landing mass** is substantially below **maximum take-off mass**. An emergency soon after departure may therefore require a reduction in mass before landing. Jettison can also be relevant to meeting the applicable **CS-25 climb and discontinued-approach performance criteria** referred to in the textbook.

Dumping usually directs fuel through pumps and a **dump/jettison master valve**, with outlets positioned well **outboard, near the trailing edge**, to prevent discharged fuel entering the airframe or its systems.

## 10.3 Controls and Automatic Termination

Fuel jettison may be managed by the flight crew or flight engineer. In installations with automatic quantity selection, the pilot can select the **amount to discharge** or the **amount that must remain**. The system then terminates dumping when the selected level is reached.

Critical safeguards prevent the crew inadvertently exhausting the entire usable supply. The chapter quotes a CS-25 residual-fuel requirement: after jettison, enough fuel must remain for the aircraft to **climb to 10,000 ft** and then fly **45 minutes at maximum-range cruising speed**.

## 10.4 Manual and Automatic Management

Older aircraft may require extensive monitoring and valve selection by a **flight engineer**. More recent two-pilot aircraft, with the **Boeing 747-400** given as an example, can automate much of this work using a **fuel-management computer**.

The preferred sequence is commonly to use **stabiliser and centre-tank fuel before main-wing fuel**, either by transfers or direct selective feed. Outer-wing fuel is retained where practicable for the structural reasons previously discussed.

# 11\. Fuel Quantity Measurement

Fuel gauges may sense **volume** or, more usefully for transport aircraft, provide a **mass/weight-related indication**. These are not interchangeable when fuel temperature and density change.

## 11.1 Float and Variable-Resistance Systems

A float rises and falls with the liquid level and changes electrical resistance. This is a relatively simple arrangement found mainly on **small aircraft**.

Its limitations are important:

* The indicated quantity may be distorted by **aircraft attitude or manoeuvring**.
* It primarily follows **fuel level/volume**, without automatically compensating for **density changes**.

## 11.2 Capacitance-Type Measuring Systems

A capacitive fuel probe uses **two conducting electrodes**, supplied with **alternating current**, separated by material that behaves as a **dielectric**.

The response depends on four factors:

1. Applied AC voltage.
2. AC frequency.
3. Effective electrode dimensions.
4. **Dielectric constant** of the material between the electrodes.

In a gauging probe the first three can remain fixed. The fuel/air mix occupying the gap changes as the tank level changes and therefore changes its effective dielectric properties. That change can be measured electrically and used in the quantity calculation.

### Temperature compensation

Because the system can respond to **fuel density**, it can be calibrated to display **mass** even when temperature changes cause the fuel to expand or contract. For example, if the same fuel mass expands by 10%, a correctly compensated **mass** display should still indicate **the same mass**.

### Attitude compensation

Modern installations may place **many capacitor probes** at different points in the tank and combine their signals, including parallel-connected sensing elements. This lets the system average variations in local fuel height caused by pitch or bank and avoid the errors associated with a single float.

# 12\. Gauge Failures and Manual Quantity Checks

## 12.1 Fail-Safe Capacitance Indication

A well-designed electronic gauging system should not leave the crew believing it has **more fuel than is actually aboard** after an electrical failure. The chapter describes a **fail-safe circuit** that gradually drives the pointer **toward empty**.

A related test switch may deliberately move the pointer toward the empty end. Releasing the switch should restore the original indication if the system is functioning correctly.

## 12.2 Dipstick

A traditional **dipstick** is inserted from the **top of the tank** and gives a physical fuel-level reading. Its weakness is the access required: standing or walking on **high, potentially slippery wing surfaces** can itself be hazardous.

## 12.3 Dripstick

A **dripstick** is a calibrated hollow tube installed through a fuel-tight opening on the **underside** of a tank. The operator draws it down until fuel begins to drip because the tube opening has entered the fuel. The tube's calibration then indicates the volume.

It avoids the need to climb above the tank but can expose the person conducting the check to dripping fuel.

## 12.4 Dropstick / Magnetic Level Indicator (MLI)

An **MLI** uses a graduated rod within a **sealed internal tube**. A floating magnet inside the tank follows the fuel surface, while a second magnet is associated with the measuring rod. As the rod is extended, the interacting magnetic fields identify the liquid level without releasing the fuel through an open tube.

The **length of rod protruding below the wing** provides the reading. A calibration table in the aircraft manual, together with the fuel's density, is used to convert the reading into the **mass** aboard.

### Comparison

|Method|Primary principle|Strength|Limitation|
|-|-|-|-|
|Float/resistance|Float changes resistance with level|Simple|Attitude and density errors|
|Capacitance|Fuel/air dielectric changes sensed electrically|Mass compensation and multi-probe attitude averaging|Requires functioning electronics|
|Dipstick|Physical depth reading from above|Straightforward backup|Hazardous upper-wing access|
|Dripstick|Fuel emerges through underside calibrated tube|No top-of-wing access|Fuel can drip on operator|
|MLI/dropstick|Magnetic float locates rod without an open fuel stream|Cleaner manual measurement|Needs aircraft calibration and density|

# 14\. Aircraft Refuelling: General Principles

## 14.1 Fuelling Safety Zone

The textbook defines a protective **refuelling zone extending at least 6 m (20 ft)** radially from aircraft **fuel filling and venting points** and from refuelling equipment.

Within that area:

* **Smoking is prohibited.**
* If an APU exhaust passes into the zone and APU operation is required, **start the APU before opening filler caps or connecting equipment**.
* If the APU stops during fuelling, **do not restart it** until fuelling has stopped and the ignition risk from vapour has passed.
* Position **ground power units (GPUs)** as far outside the zone as practicable and **do not make/break GPU connections during fuelling**.
* Provide readily accessible **fire extinguishers**.

## 14.2 Overwing and Pressure Refuelling

**Light aircraft** commonly use **overwing refuelling**, with the delivered volume read in **litres or gallons** on the supply vehicle.

Large transports frequently use **underwing pressure refuelling** from a hydrant dispenser or bowser through **refuel/defuel couplings**. At an external panel the operator can select valves and quantities; many systems allow **preselection** of the required tank quantity. Once that level is reached, the associated valve **closes automatically**, reducing the risk of overfilling.

## 14.3 Bonding Before Filling

The aircraft and refuelling equipment must be **electrically bonded using approved dedicated connections**. A conductive hose alone is **not** to be treated as sufficient proof of bonding.

For **overwing filling**, the nozzle is bonded to the airframe **before opening the filler cap**. Conductive ancillary devices—such as metal funnels, filters and containers—must also be bonded; the textbook warns **against plastic funnels or pipes**.

For **underwing pressure filling**, proper **metal-to-metal contact** at the aircraft connector/nozzle provides bonding as described in the source, so a separate hose-end cable is generally unnecessary for that connection.

## 14.4 Refuelling Order and Centre of Gravity

Fuel added in a particular order can temporarily or finally shift the **CG**, especially if selected tanks are only partly filled or **tail/fin tanks** are fitted. The approved aircraft manual determines the correct sequence for that type.

# 16\. Refuelling with Passengers Aboard — Textbook EU-OPS 1.305

The source describes the historical **EU-OPS 1.305** framework intended to allow a shorter turnaround while maintaining emergency evacuation arrangements. These are **textbook-era provisions**, not a substitute for current operator-approved policy or aviation authority rules.

## 16.1 Situations Where Passengers Must Not Remain Aboard

Under the requirements quoted in the chapter:

* Refuelling an aircraft with **fewer than 20 seats** while passengers are aboard is not permitted.
* Passengers must disembark when using **wide-cut fuels**, such as **Jet B**.
* Passengers must disembark when **AVGAS** is involved.

## 16.2 People, Cabin Preparation and Emergency Readiness

When passengers are permitted to remain aboard:

* Designate a **qualified person at a specified location** who can coordinate communication, firefighting/fire protection actions and the initiation or supervision of an evacuation.
* Warn **crew, ground staff and passengers** before refuelling or defuelling.
* Switch **seat-belt signs OFF** and tell passengers to **unfasten belts**.
* Switch **NO SMOKING signs ON**.
* Keep enough **interior lights ON** to identify emergency exits.
* Ensure an adequate complement of qualified personnel is prepared for **immediate evacuation**.
* If fuel vapour is detected within the aircraft—or another relevant hazard develops—**stop fuelling immediately**.
* Keep the area under emergency exits and **slide-deployment zones clear**.
* Ensure that a **safe and rapid evacuation** remains possible, preferably through **two main doors** at opposite ends; where only one main door exists, a main door plus an emergency exit may be used as specified.
* Permit cleaning, catering and other work only when it does not introduce a new hazard or **obstruct exit routes**.

## 16.3 Wide-Body Aircraft with Automatically Inflating Escape Slides

If a **loading bridge** is connected, the textbook says separate passenger stairs are not additionally required, but **one rear door (left or right)** must be continuously attended and ready for emergency use. Where arming involves a manual **girt-bar** attachment, that slide must remain correctly engaged during fuelling.

If **no loading bridge** is available, put a set of passenger steps at the **normal open passenger entrance**.

## 16.4 Aircraft Without Automatically Inflating Slides

* **Loading bridge present:** Position a set of stairs at **another open passenger door**, preferably at the aircraft's other end.
* **No loading bridge:** Provide stairs at **two open principal passenger doors**, ideally one forward and one aft.
* **Integral stairs** may each count as an exit means when deployed.

# 18\. Worked Concept Checks

## 18.1 Does warmer fuel necessarily increase indicated mass?

An aircraft carries a fixed amount of fuel. Its volume increases on warming while density decreases. Because the **mass is unchanged**, a correctly density-compensated **mass gauging system** should retain the original indicated quantity.

## 18.2 Which tank supplies the engines during a planned fuel-balance correction?

With one wing tank carrying **more fuel** than the other, the textbook's twin-engine arrangement temporarily uses cross-feed so that **both engines consume fuel from the fuller tank**. When balance is restored, the normal tank-to-engine feed arrangement resumes.

## 18.3 What does a blocked fuel filter change?

A clogged filter increases the pressure loss across it and may open a **filter bypass**. Where the source's system provides monitoring, the crew receives a **filter-bypass indication**. The system is not necessarily showing that the tank is empty.

## 18.4 Why protect a minimum quantity during fuel dumping?

Reducing landing mass must not remove the ability to continue safe flight. The chapter cites enough retained fuel for **climb to 10,000 ft and 45 minutes at maximum-range cruising speed** as the prescribed protection.

# 21\. Additional Diagnostic Self-Test

**1. Which fuel property helps protect lubricated pumps and metering parts?**  
**Answer:** Lubricity.

**2. What is the distinction between the performance number and colour of AVGAS 100 versus 100LL?**  
**Answer:** Both are stated as 100/130; the former is green/high lead and the latter blue/low lead.

**3. Which turbine fuel is most volatile in the textbook comparison?**  
**Answer:** Jet B, a wide-cut blend.

**4. Which fuel is quoted with a −47°C waxing point?**  
**Answer:** Jet A-1.

**5. Why may cloudy fuel be suspect?**  
**Answer:** It can contain suspended or separated water.

**6. Which additive suppresses wear in fuel control equipment?**  
**Answer:** A lubricity additive such as HITEC.

**7. Name the two main causes of low-temperature fuel-system restriction discussed.**  
**Answer:** Water freezing to ice and heavier hydrocarbons depositing as wax crystals.

**8. How does a fuel-cooled oil cooler affect both fluids?**  
**Answer:** Engine oil gives heat to the fuel, cooling the oil and warming the fuel.

**9. Why might full tanks overflow on a hotter day?**  
**Answer:** Fuel expands with temperature and may be expelled through the vent system.

**10. Why do high-altitude aircraft need positive fuel-feed pressure?**  
**Answer:** To reduce boiling/vapour-lock and cavitation risks in engine fuel feed.

**11. What is an integral fuel tank?**  
**Answer:** A sealed part of the wing or other structural volume used directly for fuel storage.

**12. What is a fuel-tank baffle designed to moderate?**  
**Answer:** Fuel surge or slosh under acceleration and aircraft manoeuvres.

**13. What makes gravity feed feasible on some high-wing light aeroplanes?**  
**Answer:** A useful height difference between the tank and carburettor.

**14. Give the quoted typical booster-pump operating pressure.**  
**Answer:** Approximately 20–40 psi.

**15. What quantity is given as typical for a collector/feeder tank?**  
**Answer:** About 500 kg.

**16. Why may outboard-wing fuel be retained for longer?**  
**Answer:** Its mass helps counter wing-bending loads; the source also associates it with flutter relief.

**17. What is the source's normal twin-engine centre-tank use priority?**  
**Answer:** Centre fuel is burned before wing fuel after take-off.

**18. What protects a tank from automatic refuelling overflow?**  
**Answer:** A high-level sensor commanding the refuelling valve closed.

**19. Why do several capacitance probes improve the quantity indication?**  
**Answer:** Their combined measurements reduce errors from changes in aircraft attitude.

**20. Which cockpit example is used for electronic fuel-system presentation?**  
**Answer:** Airbus ECAM; Boeing EICAS is mentioned as a comparable system.

**21. What does MLI mean?**  
**Answer:** Magnetic Level Indicator.

**22. Give the minimum refuelling-zone radius stated in the chapter.**  
**Answer:** At least 6 m (20 ft) around fuelling/venting points and equipment.

**23. When may the bonding wire be removed after overwing filling?**  
**Answer:** Once the filler caps have been refitted.

**24. What must be available for passenger-occupied refuelling to be defensible under the cited rules?**  
**Answer:** Effective evacuation provision, suitable trained personnel, clear emergency exits and the other quoted precautions.

**25. How much fuel must remain after jettison according to the chapter's CS-25 statement?**  
**Answer:** Enough to climb to 10,000 ft and continue for 45 minutes at maximum-range cruise speed.
