# Chapter 4 — Aircraft Wheels

## 1\. Introduction

An aircraft's **wheels and tyres** perform several important functions while the aircraft is on the ground:

* **Support:** They carry the aircraft while it is standing or moving on the ground.
* **Mobility:** They allow the aircraft to roll during **taxiing, take-off, and landing**.
* **Shock absorption:** The air-filled (**pneumatic**) tyres help cushion impacts caused by uneven surfaces and by occasional less-than-ideal landings.
* **Braking:** The **main wheels**, and the **nose wheels on some aircraft**, accommodate brake units. These brakes help control ground movement and reduce the aircraft's speed after touchdown.

The wheel assembly and its tyre work together, but they serve different purposes. The wheel provides the mounting structure and, where fitted, accommodates braking components; the pneumatic tyre provides the rolling contact and contributes to cushioning.

## 2\. Aircraft Wheels: Principal Designs

Aircraft wheels are made so that tyres can be removed and replaced without unnecessary difficulty. The textbook identifies **two principal construction arrangements**:

1. **Loose-flange and detachable-flange wheels**, in which a flange can be removed from a wheel body.
2. **Divided wheels (split hubs)**, built from two wheel halves that are joined together.

## 3\. Loose and Detachable Flange Wheel

In this wheel arrangement, **one flange is permanently part of the main wheel body**, while the opposite flange is a separate component. The removable flange is machined to fit over the wheel rim.

The two versions differ in **how the removable flange is held in position**:

* **Loose-flange wheel:** A **locking device on the rim** retains the separate flange.
* **Detachable-flange wheel:** **Nuts and bolts** fasten the separate flange directly to the wheel body.

A detachable flange does not necessarily consist of one solid ring. It may be made from **one, two, or three pieces**, with the pieces bolted together when more than one is used.

**Key distinction:** *Both designs have a removable flange; the retaining method—rim locking device versus bolts and nuts—is what separates the types.*

## 4\. The Divided Wheel (Split Hub)

A **divided wheel** is manufactured as **two matching wheel halves**. Once the halves are aligned, bolts pass through them and hold them together.

The textbook describes two locking methods for these bolts:

* Bolts may be used with **stiff nuts** to resist loosening.
* If one wheel half has **threaded (tapped) holes**, a **locking plate** is used to secure each bolt.

In the specific assembly pictured in **Figure 4.2**, the halves are secured with **bolts, Nyloc nuts, and washers**.

### 4.1 Split Wheels Used With Tubeless Tyres

The divided wheel shown in Figure 4.2 is designed to take a **tubeless tyre**. A seal is installed at the junction between the two wheel halves. It has two purposes:

1. **Prevent air leakage** by making the joint airtight.
2. **Prevent abrasion** where the two wheel halves meet.

### 4.2 Split Wheels Used With Conventional Tubed Tyres

The same general wheel arrangement can also be used with a **conventional tyre containing an inner tube**. In that case, the wheel's own inflation valve is **removed** so that the tube's valve can pass through the wheel rim instead.

**Important distinction:** With a tubeless assembly, air is contained by the tyre-to-wheel assembly and its seals; with a conventional tubed tyre, the tube supplies the inflation valve.

## 5\. Prevention of Creep

### 5.1 What Is Tyre Creep?

**Creep** means unwanted **movement or rotation of the tyre around the wheel rim** while the assembly is in use. The tyre should stay correctly seated relative to the wheel, but operating forces can cause it to slip.

Excessive creep is dangerous because it can **tear out the tyre's inflation valve** and result in a **tyre burst**. The textbook refers to further discussion in the following chapter on aircraft tyres.

Correct **tyre inflation pressure** helps reduce the chance of creep. Wheel design can also incorporate methods that either **resist the movement** or **make it easy to detect**.

### 5.2 Knurled Flange

The **inside face of the wheel flange** is given a milled or roughened (**knurled**) finish. When the tyre is inflated, pressure against the sidewalls presses the **tyre beads** against this prepared flange surface. The increased mechanical grip helps prevent the tyre from rotating around the rim.

### 5.3 Tapered Bead Seat

In a **tapered bead-seat** design, the rim is shaped so that the diameter **near the flange is greater than the diameter at the centre**. As the tyre inflates, side pressure urges the tyre beads outward against the tapered seating surfaces, helping them grip the wheel firmly.

### 5.4 Creep Marks

**Creep marks** do not prevent slipping; they provide a straightforward **visual indication** of it. One white reference line is painted on the **wheel** and another matching line on the **tyre**. When the two marks no longer line up, it shows that the tyre has moved relative to the rim.

|Method|Purpose|How it works|
|-|-|-|
|Correct inflation pressure|Reduce the likelihood of creep|Keeps the tyre correctly supported and seated|
|Knurled flange|Resist creep|Roughened inner flange face grips the tyre bead under inflation pressure|
|Tapered bead seat|Resist creep|Inflation pushes the bead outward onto a wider-diameter seating region|
|Creep marks|Detect creep|Matching white marks on tyre and wheel become misaligned if slipping occurs|

## 6\. Wheel Material

Aircraft wheels are produced by **casting or forging**, followed by **machining and grinding** until they have the required shape and finish.

The two materials identified in the textbook are:

* **Aluminium alloy**.
* **Magnesium alloy**, identified as **Electron** in the text.

Because corrosion protection is important, suitable surface treatment is applied after the initial machining stage:

1. **Aluminium alloy wheels:** Treated by **anodizing**.
2. **Magnesium alloy wheels:** Given a **chromate treatment**.
3. **Final protective finish:** Each wheel is painted using **cellulose paint or epoxy-resin paint**.

These processes form part of the wheel's manufacturing and finishing sequence, rather than changing the basic split or flange construction type.

## 7\. Wheels for Tubeless Tyres

A tubeless wheel resembles a wheel intended for a tyre with an inner tube, but its **surface finish and sealing arrangements** receive extra attention.

According to the textbook, tubeless wheel surfaces have a **finer finish** and the material is **impregnated with Bakelite to seal it**. **O-ring seals** are fitted between separate wheel components to reduce the possibility of air leakage through their joints.

### 7.1 Position of the Inflation Valve

On a wheel intended for a **tubeless tyre**, the inflation valve is **part of the wheel itself**. This is significant when considering creep:

* On a **tubed assembly**, excessive tyre creep can damage or pull out the **tube's valve**, potentially causing a burst.
* On a **tubeless wheel**, the valve is **fixed to the wheel**, so creep cannot pull that valve out in the same way.
* **However, tyre creep can still damage the tyre**, even if the valve is not affected.

**Remember:** Tubeless construction changes the valve arrangement and the need for airtight wheel joints; it does **not** make the tyre immune to creep damage.

## 8\. Fusible Plugs

### 8.1 Why Fusible Plugs Are Fitted

Severe braking can produce substantial heat in the combined **wheel, tyre and brake assembly**. If that heat becomes excessive, the tyre may blow out suddenly, with potentially catastrophic consequences for the aircraft.

Some **tubeless aircraft wheels** therefore use **fusible plugs** as a protective measure.

### 8.2 How a Fusible Plug Works

A fusible plug is fitted into the **wheel hub** and held in place by a **fusible alloy**. The alloy is selected to **melt at a specified high temperature**.

The operation is as follows:

1. **Very heavy braking** causes the wheel/brake/tyre assembly to become excessively hot.
2. Heat reaches the **fusible alloy** securing the plug.
3. When that alloy **melts**, it no longer holds the plug firmly in position.
4. **Tyre air pressure pushes the plug out**.
5. The released opening lets air escape, producing **controlled tyre deflation** rather than allowing a dangerous pressure build-up and sudden blowout.

A fusible plug is shown alongside the split wheel in **Figure 4.2**, where the small cutaway plug assembly is displayed separately to the left of the wheel.

### 8.3 Plug Temperature Ratings and Identification

The textbook identifies **three colour-coded fusible-plug temperature ratings**:

|Fusible-plug colour|Temperature|
|-|-:|
|**Red**|**155 °C**|
|**Green**|**177 °C**|
|**Amber**|**199 °C**|

The colours allow the different temperature-rated plugs to be identified readily.

**Essential principle:** Fusible plugs are designed to **release air when excessive wheel heat melts their retaining alloy**. They are intended to help avoid an abrupt, potentially catastrophic tyre failure.

## 10\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions

### Question 1 — What are the main functions of an aircraft's wheels and tyres?

**Answer:** To support the aircraft on the ground, allow movement during taxiing, take-off and landing, cushion shocks using pneumatic tyres, and accommodate braking systems in the main wheels and some nose wheels.

### Question 2 — Which wheels normally house brake units?

**Answer:** The **main wheels**, with the **nose wheels on some aircraft** also carrying brake units.

### Question 3 — What are the two principal wheel construction categories identified in the chapter?

**Answer:** **Loose/detachable-flange wheels** and **divided (split-hub) wheels**.

### Question 4 — What is the essential difference between loose and detachable flanges?

**Answer:** A **loose flange** is retained by a locking device on the rim; a **detachable flange** is attached to the wheel body with nuts and bolts.

### Question 5 — How many pieces may form a detachable flange?

**Answer:** **One, two, or three pieces**; multi-piece flanges are bolted together.

### Question 6 — How are the two halves of a divided wheel secured?

**Answer:** They are aligned and bolted together, using stiff nuts or locking plates with tapped holes as applicable. **Figure 4.2** specifically depicts bolts, **Nyloc nuts and washers**.

### Question 7 — What are the two functions of the seal at a tubeless divided-wheel joint?

**Answer:** It **prevents abrasion between the halves** and creates an **airtight joint**.

### Question 8 — What change is required to the wheel inflation valve when a divided wheel is fitted with a conventional inner-tube tyre?

**Answer:** Remove the **wheel inflation valve**, allowing the **inner tube's valve** to pass through the wheel rim.

### Question 9 — What is tyre creep?

**Answer:** Unwanted **slippage/rotation of the tyre relative to the wheel rim** during service.

### Question 10 — Why is excessive creep dangerous on a tubed assembly?

**Answer:** It can **tear out the inflation valve**, resulting in a **tyre burst**.

### Question 11 — Name two wheel-design features that resist creep.

**Answer:** A **knurled flange** and a **tapered bead seat**.

### Question 12 — What do creep marks indicate?

**Answer:** **Relative movement** between tyre and wheel, shown when their matching **white lines become misaligned**.

### Question 13 — Which two alloys are mentioned as aircraft-wheel materials?

**Answer:** **Aluminium alloy** and **magnesium alloy (Electron)**.

### Question 14 — Which corrosion-protection treatment is associated with each alloy?

**Answer:** **Anodizing** for aluminium alloy and **chromate treatment** for magnesium alloy; a final **cellulose or epoxy-resin paint** finish is also applied.

### Question 15 — What sealing details distinguish tubeless wheels?

**Answer:** A **finer finish**, **Bakelite impregnation** as stated in the textbook, and **O-ring seals** between wheel components.

### Question 16 — Why is the wheel-mounted valve on a tubeless assembly less vulnerable to tyre creep?

**Answer:** It is **part of the wheel**, rather than a valve attached to a tube moving with the tyre. Creep may still damage the tyre.

### Question 17 — What condition are fusible plugs intended to protect against?

**Answer:** A **sudden tyre blowout** caused by excessive heat generated during very heavy braking.

### Question 18 — How does a fusible plug cause the tyre to deflate?

**Answer:** Excessive heat **melts the fusible alloy** holding the plug; **tyre pressure ejects the plug** and air escapes in a controlled manner.

### Question 19 — Give the three fusible-plug colour and temperature combinations.

**Answer:** **Red—155 °C; Green—177 °C; Amber—199 °C.**

### Question 20 — Does a tubeless wheel eliminate the risk of tyre damage from creep?

**Answer:** **No.** The wheel-mounted valve is not torn out by creep in the same way as a tube valve, but the **tyre itself may still be damaged**.

## 11\. Quick Revision: Essential Facts

* **Wheels and tyres:** Support, mobility, shock cushioning and braking accommodation.
* **Wheel designs:** Removable flange types versus two-piece divided wheels.
* **Loose flange:** Secured by **rim locking device**.
* **Detachable flange:** Secured by **nuts and bolts**; may have **1, 2 or 3 pieces**.
* **Split hub:** Two matched halves connected with bolts; **Figure 4.2** uses **Nyloc nuts and washers**.
* **Tubeless split-wheel joint:** Seal prevents **air leakage and abrasion**.
* **Creep:** Relative movement of tyre on wheel; **correct pressure**, **knurled flanges**, **tapered bead seats** help reduce it, while **white creep marks** reveal it.
* **Wheel alloys:** **Aluminium** (anodized) and **magnesium/Electron** (chromate treated); **cellulose or epoxy paint** finishes.
* **Tubeless wheels:** **Fine finish, Bakelite impregnation, O-rings**, and a wheel-mounted inflation valve.
* **Fusible plugs:** Temperature-triggered controlled deflation during severe braking heat.
* **Colour codes:** **Red 155 °C · Green 177 °C · Amber 199 °C**.
