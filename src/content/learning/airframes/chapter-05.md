# Chapter 5: Aircraft Tyres



# 1\. Tyres Introduction

## 1.1 Purpose of Aircraft Tyres

Aircraft landing wheels use **pneumatic tyres**, meaning that the tyre assembly supports loads through pressurised gas. Depending on its construction, a pneumatic aircraft tyre may be **tubed** (with a separate inner tube) or **tubeless** (with the gas sealed directly inside the tyre-and-wheel assembly).

Inner tubes are more commonly associated with **light aircraft and older aircraft designs**. Aircraft tyres are generally inflated with **nitrogen**. The gas and the tyre structure work together to perform several essential functions:

* **Support the aircraft's weight:** The pressure within the tyre helps carry the load imposed on each wheel.
* **Absorb landing and ground shocks:** A pressurised tyre cushions the aircraft as it encounters runway irregularities and landing loads.
* **Preserve tyre shape:** The tyre cover contains the inflation pressure and maintains the required external profile.
* **Protect an inner tube, when fitted:** The surrounding cover restrains the tube and shields it from damage.
* **Transmit braking forces:** Contact between the tyre tread and the ground allows braking effort to act on the aircraft.
* **Provide a replaceable wearing surface:** The tread is the part that makes repeated contact with the runway or taxiway.

The performance of the tyre depends on **both correct construction and correct inflation pressure**. A tyre that looks structurally sound may still be unsafe if it is under-inflated, over-inflated, damaged or operated beyond its rating.

# 2\. Tyre Covers

## 2.1 Casing and Reinforcing Cords

The **tyre cover** is the outer structural assembly. It includes a rubber casing strengthened by successive layers, or **plies**, of reinforcing cords. The textbook identifies **cotton, rayon and nylon** as materials used for these cords.

The reinforcing cords within a ply are **parallel rather than woven together**. A thin rubber layer separates neighbouring plies. This rubber separation matters because a tyre continually flexes in service: without it, cords in adjacent layers could rub against or cut into one another.

### 2.2 Bias (Cross-Ply) and Radial Construction

The direction of the cords distinguishes two main types of tyre-cover construction:

* **Bias (cross-ply) construction:** Plies are laid in pairs, and the cords of adjacent plies are arranged at approximately **90° to one another**. The crossing arrangement creates a reinforced tyre casing.
* **Radial construction:** The principal casing cords run **from bead to bead**, at approximately **90° to the tyre's centreline**.

The two layouts are shown side by side conceptually by the different exposed ply orientations in **Figure 5.1**. The distinction is in the **direction and arrangement of reinforcement**, not merely the external appearance of the tread.

## 2.3 Breaker Strips

Near the outside of the casing, but beneath the tread, the construction incorporates **two narrow breaker plies** set within relatively thick rubber layers. These are called **breaker strips**.

Their purpose is to **spread impact loads and absorb shocks** before those loads can cause concentrated concussion damage to the casing underneath. They therefore protect the tyre's load-carrying layers during repeated runway contact.

## 2.4 Bead Construction

The tyre must remain securely seated on the wheel rim despite inflation pressure, side loads and repeated flexing. To achieve this, the casing plies are wrapped around **steel-wire coils that are effectively inextensible**. The resulting reinforced edge, formed with ply overlaps, is the **bead**.

The bead assembly is built up as follows:

1. The bead contains coils of steel wire which resist stretching.
2. The individual wires are **copper plated** to assist in forming a reliable bond.
3. The wires are held together firmly by **rubber bonding**, forming a rigid coil.
4. Strips of fabric are wrapped around the coil for further reinforcement.
5. **Apex strips**, made of rubber, and **rubberised fabric filler strips** are added around the bead region.
6. The apex and filler materials make the bead stiffer, avoid abrupt changes of cross-sectional shape and offer an increased area for bonding.
7. Finally, **chafer strips of rubberised fabric** cover the outside of the bead, protecting the bead region where it contacts the wheel.

The complete assembly combines the relatively flexible body of the tyre with a firm, secure attachment to the wheel rim.

## 2.5 Ply Rating Versus Actual Number of Plies

**Ply rating (PR) is a measure of tyre strength; it is not necessarily the literal count of reinforcing plies.**

The textbook gives an example:

|Tyre size|Stated ply rating|Actual number of plies|
|-|-:|-:|
|**49 × 17**|**32 PR**|**18 plies**|

This distinction is important when reading tyre markings: a **32-ply-rating** tyre is *not* automatically made from 32 separate plies. Its rating indicates the strength category specified by the manufacturer.

# 3\. The Regions of the Tyre

## 3.1 Standard Tyre Regions

For inspection and technical description, the tyre cover is divided into named regions. Moving from the outer running surface towards the part fixed to the wheel, these are:

* **Crown:** The highest part of the curved tyre cross-section and its principal running region.
* **Shoulder:** The transition between the crown and the sidewall.
* **Sidewall:** The side of the tyre between the shoulder and the bead area.
* **Bead:** The lower reinforced edge of the tyre which locates and seals or seats against the wheel rim.

The word **tread** refers to the rubber running surface in the **crown and shoulder region**. The term still applies when this rubber is smooth and plain rather than moulded with visible blocks or grooves.

## 3.2 Tread Patterns

### Ribbed Tread

The most common tread described in the chapter is the **ribbed pattern**. It contains **circumferential grooves** running around the tyre. These grooves help **move water away from the contact region**, reducing the likelihood of **aquaplaning (hydroplaning)**.

They also help improve **traction and grip** between the tyre tread and the runway.

### Diamond Tread

The **diamond tread** is referred to in the textbook as an **all-weather pattern**. It is **less frequently seen** than the ribbed pattern.

## 3.3 Chine on Nose-Wheel Tyres

Some **nose-wheel tyres** have a raised, shaped feature called a **chine** moulded into the **shoulder** of the tyre. This is especially relevant to aircraft with **engines installed on the rear fuselage**.

As the nose wheel moves through surface water, the chine **deflects the spray away from the engine intakes**. Its purpose is to reduce the risk of **engine flameout caused by ingesting water**.

For a **single-wheel nose-gear installation**, the tyre has a chine on **both sides** so that water is deflected away on either side of the tyre.

# 4\. Inner Tubes

## 4.1 How an Inner Tube Is Manufactured

An inner tube is made from rubber formed into a continuous hollow length. The manufacturing process described in the chapter follows these stages:

1. An **extrusion machine** pushes a hot rubber compound through a **circular die**.
2. The result is a continuous length of rubber tubing.
3. The required length is cut off.
4. Its ends are joined by **butt welding**.
5. The **inflation valve** is fitted.
6. The tube is placed in a mould, inflated and **vulcanised** to create the final shape and dimensions.

The tube's construction must suit the particular wheel and braking arrangement.

## 4.2 Heat Protection at the Base of the Tube

Some aircraft brakes create substantial heat during braking. Where that heat can reach the inner tube, a conventional tube base might be damaged.

Accordingly, inner tubes may be produced with one of three base types:

* **Standard base**.
* **Thickened base**.
* **Cord-reinforced base**.

The choice depends on the **wheel design and the kind of brake unit fitted**. When replacing an inner tube, **the replacement must be of the same type as the original**; a tube with a different base construction is not an automatic substitute.

# 5\. The Inflation Valve

## 5.1 Attachment of the Valve

The inner tube is inflated through a valve whose **stem is attached to a rubber base by direct vulcanisation**. This rubber base is itself vulcanised into the tube.

Because of this construction, **renewal of the inflation valve is not permitted** on the described tube assembly.

## 5.2 Schrader Valve Core and Cap

Each inflation valve incorporates a **Schrader-type valve core**, which works as a **non-return valve** to admit inflation gas and resist its escape.

However, the valve core **must not be regarded as a completely airtight seal**. A **valve cap must always be fitted** because it:

* Provides an additional safeguard against loss of inflation gas.
* Stops dirt and other contamination from entering the valve.

The chapter also distinguishes the spring materials used in valve cores:

|Valve-core design|Spring material|
|-|-|
|Older design|**Brass**|
|Modern design|**Stainless steel**|

# 6\. Tubeless Tyres

## 6.1 Construction and Sealing

A tubeless aircraft tyre has much of the same basic cover structure as a tyre designed to accept an inner tube. Its principal difference is an **additional rubber lining vulcanised to the inner tyre surface and to the underside of the beads**.

This internal lining provides the **gas-retaining barrier**. The airtight connection to the wheel relies on a **wedging action** between the **underside of the tyre bead** and the **tapered wheel-rim surface** against which the bead rests.

The inflation valve remains a conventional style of valve, but on a tubeless installation it is **mounted in the wheel rim** and fitted with a **rubber gasket** to prevent gas leakage.

## 6.2 Advantages of a Tubeless Tyre

The textbook identifies five advantages relative to a conventional tubed tyre:

1. **Inflation pressure is retained for longer.** The gas-retaining rubber liner is not stretched as an inner tube would be, helping maintain pressure.
2. **A small puncture need not cause an immediate large loss of pressure.** When a nail or similar sharp object penetrates the tyre, the relatively unstretched liner tends to grip that object and restrict escape of nitrogen.
3. **Improved resistance to impact and rough handling.** The thicker casing and inner liner help spread stresses, reducing concentrated damage.
4. **Weight saving.** Omitting the inner tube provides an overall saving of approximately **7.5%**.
5. **No tube-valve tearing due to tyre creep.** As the valve is attached to the wheel rim rather than a movable tube, the particular failure mode of a creeped tube tearing away from its valve is removed.

These features do not mean tubeless tyres are immune to leakage or damage. The condition of the **bead and sealing surfaces** remains essential.

# 7\. Tyre Pressures

## 7.1 Why Aircraft Tyre Pressures Differ

There is no single suitable inflation pressure for every aircraft tyre. Requirements differ with:

* **Landing speed**.
* **Load carried by the wheel**.
* **The surface onto which the aircraft lands**.
* **Landing-gear construction**.
* **Tyre size and internal construction**.

The textbook groups tyre pressures into **four general categories**.

## 7.2 Four Inflation-Pressure Categories

|Pressure category|Operating pressure|Typical surface/application|
|-|-|-|
|**Low pressure**|**25–35 psi (1.73–2.42 bar)**|Grass surfaces|
|**Medium pressure**|**35–70 psi (2.42–4.83 bar)**|Grass or moderately firm surfaces lacking a consolidated base|
|**High pressure**|**70–90 psi (4.83–6.21 bar)**|Concrete runways|
|**Extra-high pressure**|**Above 90 psi**; the chapter notes examples as high as **350 psi**, or approximately **6.21–24.2 bar**|Concrete runways|

**Study distinction:** These are categories presented by the textbook, not a replacement for the **specific tyre manufacturer's inflation instructions**.

# 8\. Tyre Markings

Aircraft tyre sidewalls carry information needed to identify the tyre and confirm that its approved operating limits and construction are appropriate.

## 8.1 Conductive-Tyre Markings

A tyre marked **“ECTA”** or **“Conducting”** contains additional **carbon in its rubber compound**, making the rubber electrically conductive.

This enables the tyre to provide **electrical contact between the aircraft and the ground**, serving a grounding/earthing function.

## 8.2 Size Markings

The sidewall identifies the tyre's dimensions, including:

1. **Outside diameter**, in inches or millimetres.
2. **Nominal width**, in inches or millimetres.
3. **Inside diameter**, in inches.

These identify the overall size and wheel-fit dimensions of the tyre.

## 8.3 Ply-Rating Markings

The manufacturer's **ply rating**, the index of the tyre's structural strength, is also printed or moulded onto the sidewall.

Two ways of displaying the same kind of rating are:

* **16PR**.
* **16 PLY RATING**.

Remember that **ply rating is not necessarily the actual number of fabric plies**.

## 8.4 Speed Rating and Operating Limitations

The **speed rating** indicates the tyre's **maximum approved ground speed in miles per hour**, determined by the speed for which the tyre has been tested and accepted.

This rating is **embossed on the sidewall**. Its application is considered together with **pressure altitude, ambient temperature and wind component** so that the **maximum take-off mass (MTOM)** consistent with the tyres' limits can be determined.

## 8.5 Green or Grey Dots — Awl Vents

**Green or grey dots** indicate positions of the tyre's **awl vents**.

Awl vents are provided so that gas pressure cannot become trapped **between the casing plies**. Trapped inter-ply pressure could disrupt the tyre carcass when the aircraft operates in the **low ambient pressures experienced at high altitude**.

## 8.6 Red Dot or Triangle — Lightest Point

A **red dot or triangle** on the sidewall marks the **lightest part of the tyre**.

When the tyre is installed, positioning this mark **opposite the inflation valve** helps balance the **complete wheel-and-tyre assembly**.

## 8.7 DRR / REINFORCED TREAD Marking

The code **“DRR”**, printed in the tyre's code panel, and **“REINFORCED TREAD”**, printed on the sidewall, identify a tyre whose tread includes an additional **woven fabric reinforcement layer**.

This fabric **may become visible as the tyre undergoes normal wear**. It must **not be mistaken for the load-bearing casing cords** underneath.

# 9\. Tyre Contamination

Rubber can be damaged or degraded by environmental exposure and by contact with unsuitable liquids. The chapter identifies the following hazards:

* Excessive **heat**.
* **Dampness**.
* Strong, direct **sunlight**.
* **Oil**.
* **Fuel**.
* **Glycol**.
* **Hydraulic fluid**.

When an aircraft will remain parked for an extended period, **oilskin protective covers** should be placed over the tyres.

The tyres should also be protected when the aircraft's **oil, fuel, cooling or hydraulic systems** are being drained, filled or replenished.

If any of these liquids are accidentally spilled or allowed to drip onto a tyre, they must be **wiped off immediately** rather than left in contact with the rubber.

# 10\. Creep (Slippage)

## 10.1 What Tyre Creep Means

**Tyre creep** is the movement of the tyre around the wheel rim after installation.

A **small amount of initial movement** may occur when a tyre is newly fitted and is regarded as normal while the assembly settles. Once the tyre has settled, this movement **should stop**.

Continued or excessive movement in service is a fault rather than normal bedding-in.

## 10.2 Why Creep Is Dangerous

For a tyre fitted with an **inner tube**, excessive rotation of the cover around the rim can **pull or tear the inflation valve from the tube**. This can lead to a rapid pressure loss or tyre burst.

Creep is generally **less troublesome on a tubeless installation**, provided the **bead is not damaged** and any reduction in tyre pressure is **within approved limits**.

## 10.3 Inflation Pressure and Creep

Keeping tyres correctly inflated helps prevent movement between the tyre bead and the rim. To support correct servicing, manufacturers specify a **rated inflation pressure**.

The rated inflation pressure applies to a **cold tyre with no load acting on it**—in other words, a tyre **not fitted to an aircraft**.

If the same cold tyre is fitted to an aircraft and supports aircraft weight, the tyre cover deforms and the pressure rises by approximately **4%**. Therefore, the textbook directs the reader to **add 4% to the rated inflation pressure** when comparing it with the pressure of a **cold tyre fitted to the aircraft**.

During **taxi, take-off and landing**, the tyre heats up, potentially producing a **further pressure increase of up to 10%**.

### Pressure-reference summary

|Condition|Effect on inflation pressure|
|-|-|
|Cold tyre not carrying aircraft load|Manufacturer's **rated inflation pressure** is the reference|
|Cold tyre installed and supporting the aircraft|Approximately **4% higher** because of tyre deformation|
|Tyre heated during operation|A **further rise of up to 10%** may occur|

**Important:** Do not use a hot tyre pressure as though it were directly comparable with the manufacturer's cold, unloaded rating.

# 11\. Correct Tyre Pressures

Maintaining correct inflation is essential for tyre safety, service life and wheel security.

The textbook specifies inflation using **nitrogen or another inert gas**, allowing a maximum **oxygen content of 5%**.

Incorrect pressure creates different hazards:

* **Under-inflation:** The tyre can move, or **creep**, around the rim, with the associated risks described above.
* **Over-inflation:** Excess pressure can lead to other forms of tyre failure and influences the pattern of tread wear.

The textbook estimates that **90% of tyre failures are associated with incorrect inflation-gas pressure**. This is presented as the book's estimate and emphasizes the importance of regular pressure management.

On some modern aircraft, the crew can view **tyre pressure indications on an electronic systems-monitoring display**.

# 12\. Aquaplaning

## 12.1 How Aquaplaning Occurs

**Aquaplaning**, also called **hydroplaning**, occurs when water builds into a **wedge between the tread and the runway**. If this wedge separates the tyre from the surface, the tyre loses the normal ground contact needed to develop friction and braking force.

The chapter gives the approximate **aquaplaning speed** as a function of tyre inflation pressure.

## 12.2 Aquaplaning-Speed Formulae

When the pressure is expressed in **psi**, the formula is:

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{AQ}} = 9\\sqrt{P\_{\\mathrm{psi}}},\\mathrm{kt}
$$

When the pressure is expressed in **bar** (or approximately in **kg/cm²**, as stated by the textbook), the alternative formula is:

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{AQ}} = 34\\sqrt{P\_{\\mathrm{bar}}},\\mathrm{kt}
$$

where:

* $V\_{\\mathrm{AQ}}$ is the approximate **aquaplaning speed in knots** (nautical miles per hour).
* $P\_{\\mathrm{psi}}$ is the inflation pressure **in pounds per square inch**.
* $P\_{\\mathrm{bar}}$ is the inflation pressure **in bar** for the second expression.

The two constants correspond to **different pressure units**; use the constant that matches the given pressure.

### Worked practice example — 100 psi

For a tyre inflated to **100 psi**:

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{AQ}} = 9\\sqrt{100} = 9\\times10 = \\boxed{90,\\mathrm{kt}}
$$

This is a practice calculation using the formula provided in the chapter, not an additional operational tyre limit.

## 12.3 Influence of Tread Depth

Aquaplaning becomes **more likely as the tread grooves wear down**. Reduced tread depth makes it harder for water to escape from beneath the tyre.

Consequently, the **remaining tread depth must be inspected accurately**. During aquaplaning, the **coefficient of dynamic friction** may fall to an extremely small value—**typically approaching zero**, as described in the textbook. This corresponds to a substantial reduction in available braking and directional grip.

# 13\. MAT Limits

## 13.1 Mass, Altitude and Temperature Considerations

When take-off performance is calculated, the required take-off distance and obstacle clearance may lead to the use of an **increased $V\_2$ speed**.

Even if this produces a suitable take-off performance result, the aircraft must **not exceed the maximum speed rating of its fitted tyres**.

The textbook notes that compliance with the **mass, altitude and temperature (MAT) limits** may therefore require a **reduction in aircraft mass**. In other words, the permissible take-off weight may be constrained by tyre speed capability even when other performance calculations appear satisfactory.

# 14\. Tyre Damage

## 14.1 General Inspection

During servicing, tyre covers need to be checked for visible signs of deterioration and damage. The specified inspection concerns include:

* **Cuts** or splits.
* **Bulges**.
* **Embedded stones, metal or glass**.
* **Excessive or abnormal wear**.
* **Excessive creep**.
* **Localised sponginess** or softness in the casing.

Some defects require tyre repair, removal or replacement. The book gives specific criteria for several important conditions.

## 14.2 Cuts

A **cut deep enough to reach the casing cords** makes the tyre **unserviceable** in the condition in which it is found. The textbook requires the damage to be **repaired** before the tyre can return to service.

## 14.3 Bulges

A bulge can indicate **partial breakdown of the casing**.

Where examination confirms that the fabric/casing has fractured, the tyre cover must be **renewed**, rather than treated as a harmless external shape change.

## 14.4 Embedded Foreign Bodies

Foreign material such as **glass, stones or metal** may lodge in the tread or casing.

The chapter states that embedded objects **must not be pulled out by the person finding them**. Instead, the condition must be **reported to maintenance personnel**. The affected cuts are then assessed using a **blunt probing tool** to determine their depth, and the decision to repair or renew the cover depends on the degree of damage.

## 14.5 Wear Limits

The allowable amount of tread wear depends on the type of tread:

* **Patterned tread tyres:** Do not continue using the tyre when wear reaches the **base of the marker grooves or marker tie bars over 25% of the tyre circumference**.
* **Plain tread tyres:** Do not continue using the tyre when wear reaches the **casing fabric**.

The textbook illustrates the marker-groove and tie-bar arrangement in **Figure 5.3**, reproduced as a prompt under the final tyre-wear section below.

## 14.6 Creep Limits

The allowable movement is related to the tyre's **outside diameter**:

|Tyre outside diameter|Maximum permitted movement around the wheel|
|-|-|
|**Up to and including 24 in**|**1 in**|
|**Over 24 in**|**1½ in**|

If the measured movement **exceeds the applicable limit**:

1. **Remove the tyre from the wheel.**
2. Check the **inner tube for tearing at the inflation valve**.
3. Inspect the **valve stem for deformation**.
4. If the tube is serviceable, the tyre may be refitted and the **creep marks reapplied**.

These limits and checks are significant because excessive movement can damage the valve connection on a tubed assembly.

# 15\. Tread Separation and Tyre Burst

A tyre may **burst**, or the part carrying the tread may **separate from the rest of the tyre**.

Either event can affect more than the damaged wheel:

* The damaged wheel provides a **smaller ground-contact area (footprint)**.
* The **remaining tyres carry additional load**.
* The aircraft's **braking effectiveness is reduced**.
* Detached rubber or structural fragments become a **foreign-object-damage (FOD) hazard**.
* Debris can damage **brake hydraulic lines**.
* Pieces of tyre or other debris may be **ingested into an engine**.

A tyre failure therefore poses risks to the aircraft's braking and landing-gear systems, as well as to other nearby components.

# 16\. Reduction of Tyre Wear

## 16.1 Taxi Speed

As airports become larger, aircraft often travel longer distances while taxiing. More time spent rolling on paved surfaces means more **tyre wear** and greater exposure to **surface-related damage**.

To limit this wear, the chapter recommends a taxi speed **not exceeding 25 mph (40 km/h)**.

## 16.2 Pressure and Wear Pattern

Tyre inflation has a characteristic relationship with where tread wears most heavily:

* **Over-inflation:** Produces excessive wear in the **crown (central tread) region**.
* **Under-inflation:** Produces excessive wear at the **shoulders (outer tread regions)**.

Observing *where* the tyre is wearing can therefore help identify the effects of unsuitable inflation pressure.

# 18\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions

The following are **new study questions based on the chapter's concepts**, rather than an original printed question bank (this chapter does not provide one).

### 1\. What is the difference between a tubed and a tubeless aircraft tyre?

**Answer:** A tubed tyre contains a separate inner tube to hold inflation gas; a tubeless tyre uses a vulcanised internal lining and a seal between bead and wheel rim to retain the gas.

### 2\. Which gas is normally used for aircraft tyre inflation in the chapter?

**Answer:** **Nitrogen**.

### 3\. Why are the reinforcing cords of adjacent plies separated by rubber?

**Answer:** The rubber film prevents the cords from rubbing against and cutting one another as the tyre flexes.

### 4\. How do the ply directions differ between cross-ply and radial construction?

**Answer:** Adjacent cross-plies have cords approximately **90° apart**; in a radial casing the cords travel **bead to bead at about 90° to the tyre centreline**.

### 5\. What do breaker strips protect?

**Answer:** They distribute shocks and reduce concussion damage to the casing beneath the tread.

### 6\. What secures the tyre casing to the wheel rim?

**Answer:** The **bead**, reinforced by inextensible steel-wire coils around which the plies are wrapped.

### 7\. Does 32 PR mean a tyre has 32 reinforcing plies?

**Answer:** No. **Ply rating indicates strength**, not necessarily the number of actual plies. The example in the book is **32 PR with 18 plies**.

### 8\. What are the four named regions of a tyre?

**Answer:** **Crown, shoulder, sidewall and bead**.

### 9\. Why are ribbed grooves useful on wet runways?

**Answer:** They help displace water, improving tread/runway contact and reducing aquaplaning risk.

### 10\. What is the purpose of a chine on a nose-wheel tyre?

**Answer:** It diverts water spray away from engine intakes, reducing the risk of water-ingestion flameout.

### 11\. What forms can the base of an inner tube take?

**Answer:** **Standard, thickened or cord-reinforced**, depending on wheel and brake design.

### 12\. Why must the valve cap remain fitted even when the Schrader core is installed?

**Answer:** The core is not a perfect seal; the cap provides additional sealing protection and keeps dirt out.

### 13\. What component makes the inside of a tubeless tyre gas-tight?

**Answer:** A **vulcanised inner rubber lining** extending beneath the bead regions, together with the bead-to-rim seal.

### 14\. Approximately what weight saving is stated for removing the inner tube?

**Answer:** About **7.5%**.

### 15\. What is the textbook's pressure range for a high-pressure aircraft tyre?

**Answer:** **70–90 psi (4.83–6.21 bar)**.

### 16\. What does “ECTA” or “Conducting” indicate?

**Answer:** Extra carbon is added to the rubber to allow electrical grounding through the tyre.

### 17\. What does a green or grey sidewall dot identify?

**Answer:** An **awl vent**, which prevents trapped pressure building between casing plies.

### 18\. What does a red dot or triangle on the tyre mark?

**Answer:** The **lightest point**, positioned opposite the valve to help balance the assembled wheel.

### 19\. What do “DRR” and “REINFORCED TREAD” indicate?

**Answer:** A **fabric reinforcement layer within the tread**, which should not be mistaken for casing cords if it becomes visible during ordinary wear.

### 20\. Is a little tyre creep immediately after installation always abnormal?

**Answer:** No. Some initial settling movement can be normal, but continuing movement after the tyre beds in should not occur.

### 21\. Why is excessive creep especially serious with an inner tube?

**Answer:** It can pull or tear the **inflation valve** from the tube, causing rapid deflation or a burst.

### 22\. What pressure increase is expected when a cold, unloaded tyre is installed and carries aircraft load?

**Answer:** Approximately **4%** above the manufacturer's cold, unloaded rated pressure.

### 23\. How much further can tyre pressure rise as it heats during operation?

**Answer:** Up to a **further 10%**, according to the chapter.

### 24\. What maximum oxygen content is permitted for the inflation gas specified in the chapter?

**Answer:** **5%**.

### 25\. At what approximate speed could a tyre at 81 psi aquaplane, using the textbook formula?

**Answer:**

$$
V\_{\\mathrm{AQ}} = 9\\sqrt{81} = 9\\times9 = \\boxed{81,\\mathrm{kt}}
$$

### 26\. Why is aquaplaning more likely when a tyre is worn?

**Answer:** Shallow grooves drain less water from the tread/runway interface, making separation by a water wedge more likely.

### 27\. How can tyre speed rating affect allowable take-off mass?

**Answer:** A required higher take-off speed, including increased $V\_2$, may conflict with tyre speed limits; reducing aircraft mass may be necessary to remain within MAT and tyre limits.

### 28\. What is the required action if a tyre cut reaches the casing cords?

**Answer:** The tyre is **unserviceable** and requires repair before use.

### 29\. Should embedded metal or glass be removed during a routine visual inspection?

**Answer:** **No.** Report the foreign body to maintenance; its damage is assessed by authorised personnel.

### 30\. When is a patterned-tread tyre worn beyond the criterion given in the chapter?

**Answer:** When tread is down to the base of marker grooves or tie bars over **25% of the tyre circumference**.

### 31\. What is the maximum allowed tyre creep for a tyre with a 22-inch outside diameter?

**Answer:** **1 in**, since the tyre is in the **up to 24 in** category.

### 32\. What is the maximum allowed tyre creep for a tyre with a 30-inch outside diameter?

**Answer:** **1½ in**, since its outside diameter is **over 24 in**.

### 33\. What additional hazards can result from tyre burst or tread separation?

**Answer:** Smaller footprint, more load on remaining tyres, reduced braking, debris/FOD, possible brake hydraulic-line damage and possible engine ingestion.

### 34\. What taxi speed limit is recommended in the chapter to reduce tyre wear?

**Answer:** **25 mph (40 km/h)**.

### 35\. Which tread wear pattern is associated with excess tyre pressure, and which with insufficient pressure?

**Answer:** **Over-inflation wears the crown**; **under-inflation wears the shoulders**.
