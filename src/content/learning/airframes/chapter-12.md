# Chapter 12: Ice and Rain Protection

## 1\. Why Ice and Frost Are Dangerous

### 1.1 Changes to airflow and wing performance

A wing is designed around a particular surface shape and smoothness. **Ice changes both**, while even a thin layer of frost can substantially roughen a surface that otherwise looks nearly unchanged. The airflow close to the wing—the **boundary layer**—is especially sensitive to this roughness. As it loses energy, it separates more readily from the surface.

The consequences of contamination include:

* **Higher drag:** rough ice or frost increases surface resistance; severe ice can also distort the wing profile and alter local pressure distribution.
* **Lower maximum lift coefficient, (C\_{L\\max}):** the boundary layer can separate at an angle of attack lower than would be possible on a clean wing.
* **Higher stall speed:** the aeroplane must fly faster to generate the required lift when the maximum usable lift coefficient falls.
* **Greater power requirement:** increased drag must be overcome in flight.
* **Additional weight:** accumulated ice itself adds mass, though aerodynamic degradation can be more serious than the added weight.

The wing leading edge is particularly critical. A thick ridge there reshapes the airflow as it first meets the aerofoil. Ice may generate sharp changes in curvature and pressure; some deposits introduce severe surface roughness and friction. The combined effect is reduced lift capability and a substantial drag penalty.

### 1.2 Forms of atmospheric ice

The chapter distinguishes three commonly encountered deposits:

|Ice type|Recognition / significance|
|-|-|
|**Hoar frost**|A surface deposit that can introduce substantial roughness despite a relatively small change in the wing's overall outline.|
|**Rime ice**|An atmospheric ice deposit that can accumulate where supercooled moisture encounters cold surfaces.|
|**Clear or glaze ice**|Another form of atmospheric ice whose accumulation can significantly modify aerofoil contours.|

For this chapter, the key shared hazard is not the appearance of the deposit but its influence on the boundary layer, maximum lift, drag, and aircraft handling.

### 1.3 Why frost is easily underestimated

A hard frost layer on top of a wing need not produce an obvious new aerofoil profile. Nonetheless, its rough surface drains energy from the boundary layer, increases skin-friction drag, and promotes earlier separation. The reduction in (C\_{L\\max}) can therefore surprise a pilot who expects only large, visible blocks of ice to affect the stall.

Frost generally does not increase drag as dramatically as severe, bulky ice. Its effect on lift, however, can still be operationally decisive.

### 1.4 Consequences during landing

A contaminated aircraft approaching to land may have **less maximum lift and a higher stall speed** than expected. A higher suitable landing speed can be necessary, with possible further difficulty if braking performance on the runway is poor. The chapter emphasizes preventing ice accumulation in flight rather than accepting it as a normal landing condition.

### 1.5 The take-off hazard

**The source's central rule is that ice or frost must not remain on the wing before take-off.** A heavy, hard frost deposit on the upper surface can raise stall speed by roughly **5–10%** through its effect on maximum lift.

Take-off speed is commonly **5–25% above stall speed** in the textbook's example. In that range, take-off lift coefficient is described as approximately **90–65% of (C\_{L\\max})**. A significant drop in maximum lift can remove the assumed safety margin. The aeroplane might fail to lift off at its planned speed or might become airborne with too little margin to tolerate a gust, turbulence, or a turn.

An important trap is that **initial take-off acceleration can appear normal**. Added drag from surface contamination may not noticeably affect the early ground roll. The danger becomes apparent late in the take-off, near rotation or during the initial climb, when the wing's available lift is essential.

## 2\. How Airframe Ice Forms in Flight

### 2.1 Supercooled liquid water

In-flight icing is principally associated with **supercooled water droplets**: liquid moisture at a temperature below the normal freezing point. When these droplets strike exposed forward-facing aircraft parts, they may freeze and form a layer of ice. The change in surface contours or roughness can then interfere with aerodynamic performance.

Small projecting items can collect ice at a **high interception (catch) efficiency** relative to their dimensions. Consequently, even a modest deposit may represent a large proportionate change to the shape of a thin probe or aerial.

### 2.2 The surface's heat balance

The rate and appearance of ice depend partly on the temperature of the impacted surface. The chapter identifies five relevant heat-transfer effects:

|Effect|Influence on surface energy|
|-|-|
|Kinetic heating from airflow|**Adds** heat|
|Kinetic heating from arriving droplets|**Adds** heat|
|Heat released when water freezes (latent heat of fusion)|**Adds** heat|
|Evaporation|**Removes** heat|
|Convection|**Removes** heat|

The final skin temperature follows the balance among these effects, not simply the surrounding air temperature.

### 2.3 Three possible surface-temperature conditions

* **Below 0°C:** the chapter treats droplets striking the surface as freezing.
* **Exactly 0°C:** the surface is at the threshold where the heat balance may support partial freezing.
* **Above 0°C:** impinging liquid droplets are not expected to freeze onto that surface.

For a particular atmospheric state and altitude, there may be a **considerable range of aircraft speeds** for which aerodynamic heating and cooling together leave the skin at about 0°C. At one end of that speed range the incoming droplets freeze; at the other, none freeze. It follows that the number of droplets striking the aircraft is **not automatically equal to the rate at which ice accumulates**.

Whether a particular speed produces a non-icing surface depends on **available liquid-water content, temperature, and altitude**. In severe conditions, the speed needed to avoid icing by this heating effect may approach the highest speeds attainable by subsonic aircraft, so it is not a practical universal escape method.

At around **12,000 m (40,000 ft)** and above, the textbook describes the droplets as generally existing in frozen crystal form rather than as adherent supercooled liquid water. This is a description of the chapter's icing model, not an assurance that all high-altitude ice-crystal encounters are harmless.

## 3\. Protection Standards and Basic Methods

### 3.1 Protection before dispatch and during flight

An aircraft must not be dispatched carrying frost, snow, or ice contamination that compromises the required clean condition. The textbook cites **CS-OPS** requirements for public-transport aircraft flying when the available forecasts suggest that icing may be encountered. It also discusses baseline structural, control, and engine-function provisions intended to provide reasonable tolerance to an inadvertent short exposure to icing.

The design and protection considerations include stability, control balance, possible **freezing or jamming of controls**, and continued satisfactory **engine operation**.

### 3.2 Anti-icing versus de-icing

|Strategy|When it acts|Typical method|
|-|-|-|
|**Anti-icing**|Before ice is allowed to build up|Continuous heating or protective fluid|
|**De-icing**|After some ice has accumulated|Intermittent heat, fluid, or mechanical removal|

The main technologies introduced are **inflatable pneumatic boots**, **electrical / hot-oil / hot-air thermal protection**, and **freezing-point-depressant (FPD) fluids**. Sensors and warning systems complement the physical protection equipment by advising the flight crew that icing exists or may develop.

## 4\. Ice Detection and Warning Equipment

### 4.1 Two detection principles and three broad equipment groups

**Accretion detectors** respond to the presence or growth of actual ice on a sensing object. **Inferential detectors** instead identify the combination of environmental conditions from which ice would be expected.

The chapter places instruments into three broad families:

1. Ice-detector heads—accretion sensing.
2. Mechanical ice detectors—accretion sensing.
3. Element-type sensing units—inferential sensing.

### 4.2 Teddington visual detector

This is a small **aerofoil-profile mast** projecting into the airstream where the pilot can see it. A lamp allows inspection in darkness, and an internal electric heater removes ice once observation is complete. With the heater not operating, any ice forming on the mast provides a direct visible indication of icing.

The Smiths probe is a **hollow tube** anchored to the aircraft. It has **four forward/leading-edge openings and two rear/trailing-edge openings**. ### 4.4 Napier rotary detector

A continuously running electric motor turns a **serrated shaft** near a fixed cutter. Its mounting geometry places the shaft across the airflow and the cutter on the sheltered side. The blade-to-shaft gap is **less than 0.002 in**.

Without ice, the rotor turns with little resistance. When ice coats the shaft, the fixed blade scrapes it away and motor torque rises. The motor's flexible mounting permits a slight reaction movement that actuates a **microswitch**. This can illuminate an ice warning or start an automatic protection sequence; the warning persists while the cutter continues meeting ice.

### 4.5 Rosemount vibrating-rod detector

A short cylindrical probe is vibrated axially at approximately **35 kHz**. Ice adds mass and reduces its natural resonance frequency. Once the sensed frequency falls to a set value, the system issues a warning and energizes a heater within the probe. The heater operates for **six seconds**, after which the sensing cycle begins again. The recurrence rate of this sequence can also indicate how rapidly ice is collecting.

### 4.6 Sangamo Weston moisture/temperature detector

This design does not wait for ice to stick to a probe. Instead it looks for **free water together with freezing ambient conditions**.

Its sensing and control elements work together:

* **Moisture-sensing head:** two heated metal-resistance bulbs stand in the slipstream. The upstream bulb receives droplets; it shields the rear bulb, keeping the latter comparatively dry. Evaporation cools the wet bulb more quickly, creating a measurable temperature difference.
* **Moisture-detector controller:** processes the wet-versus-dry temperature difference. When the difference reaches its threshold, it can command a warning or initiate a selected protection cycle—provided the temperature interlock allows it.
* **Thermal switch:** senses ambient temperature. Above freezing it prevents a moisture signal from being treated as an icing warning. Below freezing it permits the warning circuit to operate.

This is why the Sangamo Weston device is termed **inferential** rather than an ice-accretion sensor.

### 4.7 Beta-particle detector

Two short probes extend perpendicular to the **forward fuselage**: the front one emits beta particles, and the second measures how many arrive. Ice deposited over the emitter absorbs part of the radiation and therefore reduces the count received downstream.

A detector threshold associated with approximately **0.4 mm of ice** operates a relay. A typical indication is an **ECAM message with a single chime**; a test command can reproduce the same indications.

### 4.8 Ice-observation lamps and indications

Aircraft may have one spotlight on each side of the fuselage aimed at the **wing leading edges**. These permit the crew to look for ice after dark; in some types they are the only night-time visual ice-check aid.

The chapter stresses that pilots must continually assess **moisture and temperature**. It gives a broad engine-protection example of an **indicated outside air temperature (IOAT) of +10°C or below together with visible moisture**. Airframe systems are generally selected once ice is indicated—on wing edges, aerials, wipers, or a dedicated sensor. Typical indications include an **amber caution**, sometimes with automatic system initiation when preselected to **AUTO**. The particular pneumatic-boot installation discussed requires a prescribed ice thickness before operation; the aircraft-specific procedure is authoritative.

### 4.9 Equipment that may need protection

Protected locations vary widely. The textbook includes **engine inlets, inlet guide vanes (IGVs), supporting struts/webs, oil-cooler openings, fuel filters, ram-air intakes for generator or engine-bay ventilation, wing and tail leading edges, slats, propellers, aerials, waste-water outlet horns, large fences and fairing bullets, pitot heads and other instrument probes, and flight-deck windows**.

## 5\. Pneumatic Mechanical De-icing

### 5.1 General arrangement

Certain piston-engine aircraft and twin turboprops use **inflatable leading-edge boots** to remove ice mechanically. The exact valves, air source, and sequence depend on the aircraft.

### 5.2 Boot construction and installation

A boot, sometimes called an **overshoe**, is formed from layers of natural rubber and rubberised fabric. Flattened, closed-end inflatable tubes are vulcanised between these layers. In one arrangement the tubes run **spanwise** along the wing or tailplane; in another they extend **chordwise**. Flexible hoses join the tubes to the distribution-valve pipework and are retained by hose clips.

The outer rubber carries a **conductive coating** to drain static electricity. Depending on type, the assembly is secured to the leading edge using **screw fasteners/rivnuts** or by **cementing** it directly in place.

### 5.3 Where inflation pressure and vacuum come from

The inflating air may be supplied by the **pressure side of an engine-driven vacuum pump**, a **high-pressure storage vessel**, or (on certain turboprops) an **engine-compressor tapping**.

Once inflation ends, or when the equipment is switched off, the boots must lie flat. This is accomplished by **vacuum** from the pump or from a **Venturi ejector nozzle** where compressor air is the source.

Air can be routed by three general arrangements:

1. Shuttle valves directed by a separate solenoid valve.
2. Individual solenoid-controlled valves feeding each boot.
3. A motor-driven distribution valve.

### 5.4 Crew controls and indications

A simple installation provides a **master ON/OFF switch**, indication of **pressure and vacuum** (by gauges or lamps), and a means of selecting the operating cycle. The equipment alternates pressure and suction, generally under **electronic timing control**. The correct timings and switching details are particular to the aircraft and must be taken from its approved manuals.

### 5.5 Inflation–deflation cycle

1. Pressure enters the boot tubes and they expand, loosening the bond between ice and the rubber surface.
2. The loosened ice separates from the leading edge.
3. Automatic valves vent the pressure to atmosphere.
4. Vacuum pulls the tubes completely flat again.
5. The system repeats this process as long as the selected operating cycle continues.

With the system **OFF**, vacuum normally remains applied so that the boots lie smoothly against the leading edges and do not introduce avoidable drag.

## 6\. Thermal Wing and Engine Ice Protection

### 6.1 Continuous heating and intermittent heating

**Hot-air protection** on modern transport aircraft commonly uses **engine bleed air** and generally works as anti-icing. Depending on heat level and duration, other thermal installations may be used either to prevent ice formation or periodically remove ice.

The chapter also observes that **some large jet transports do not have dedicated tailplane ice protection**, having been certified with the effect of ice on the tailplane taken into account.

### 6.2 Heated double-skin leading edges

In a typical air-heated structure, a second inner skin is fitted behind the **wing leading edge** (including applicable slats, but **not the leading-edge flaps** in the described arrangement) and behind relevant tailplane sections. The narrow channel between inner and outer skins carries heated air. Heat transfers through the outer skin to prevent accretion or loosen existing ice.

Spent air is discharged through **surface outlets** or, in certain designs, outlets near the **wing/tail tips**. Valves such as **shutters and butterfly valves** regulate temperature and flow.

### 6.3 Why engine inlets demand special protection

Ice can block or change the shape of the **air inlet**, **nose bullet/fairing**, and **inlet guide vanes**. Restricted airflow lowers engine efficiency and thrust; broken-off deposits may enter the compressor and damage blades. The two approaches described are **hot compressor-bleed air** and **electrical resistance heaters**, sometimes combined. Electrical systems are common in the turboprop examples because they can also protect the propeller installation.

### 6.4 Sources of heated air

**Compressor bleed:** Air is extracted from an engine compressor stage. It may first pass through a **mixing chamber with cooler air**, then through the protected ductwork. In some aircraft, **safety shut-off valves** help maintain acceptable supply pressure and mass flow within the capacity of ducts and structure.

**Exhaust heat exchanger:** In certain turboprops, exhaust gas passes around the tubing used for incoming outside air. The outside air becomes warm without mixing with the exhaust. An **exhaust-side thermostatic flap** can regulate the energy supplied.

**Combustion heater:** Ram air passes around a sealed combustion chamber enclosed by a cylindrical jacket. A **fuel–air mixture burns inside the chamber**, and its wall transfers heat to the ram air. The combustion air comes from a **separate intake**, assisted by a **blower**.

### 6.5 Regulating heating temperature

Temperature control must prevent both underheating and structural overheating.

* **Bleed-air installations:** sensors in leading-edge ducts feed electrical signals to air-control valves. These valves adjust hot-air flow to hold a specified temperature range. Typical cockpit or maintenance indications come from resistance sensors, temperature switches, and **overheat lights**. Some designs inhibit normal electrically powered valve action on the ground through **landing-gear relays**, with a held **TEST** selection permitting checks.
* **Heat-exchanger installations:** adjustable airflow or exhaust flaps regulate how much hot and cold air passes the exchanger. A typical **electric actuator** makes small movements commanded by an outlet-duct temperature sensor; other types use thermal switches that close a valve upon reaching a limit. Monitoring may again include resistance temperature sensing and overheat annunciations.
* **Combustion heaters:** cyclic thermal switches in the outlet duct stop fuel delivery automatically on reaching the specified maximum temperature.

### 6.6 Typical engine hot-air circulation

Hot compressor air enters ductwork serving the **intake nose cowl**, **inlet guide vanes**, and sometimes the **nose bullet**. Having passed through these regions, it is discharged to ambient air or back into the engine inlet. Crew switches normally operate **electrically controlled regulating valves**. The particular simple system described does **not normally incorporate a dedicated air-temperature control system**.

### 6.7 Electrically heated intake structures

An alternative bonds heating circuits to the inlet structure. The element may consist of **resistance wire** or **sprayed metal**, usually supplied by **three-phase AC**. The textbook's turboprop example uses a shaped heater overshoe around the **intake-cowl leading edge** and the **oil-cooler intake**.

Different portions can be wired for different duties:

* **Continuously energized zones** prevent ice forming.
* **Intermittently energized zones** periodically melt and release ice.

Elements are enclosed between **resin-impregnated glass cloth** or sometimes rubber layers. Their outside surfaces resist damage from **rain erosion, oil, and grease**. A cyclic switch feeds intermittent circuits and, in the illustrated system, also coordinates **propeller blade heating**.

## 7\. Fluid-Based Wing and Tail Anti-icing

### 7.1 Freezing-point-depressant supply

A fluid system continuously presents a thin film of **freezing-point-depressant (FPD)** liquid at the aerofoil leading edge. The local airstream spreads it rearward over the surface, making deposited water less likely to freeze and remain attached.

Fluid follows this route:

**Storage tank → integral filter → pump → multiple delivery outlets → leading-edge distributors → surface film.**

A representative pump has a single inlet, several outlets, and one main casing incorporating its **pump body, filter chamber and gear housing**. Working fluid occupies the pump and filter and provides lubrication. A **pressure-relief / flow-reduction device** protects the equipment against an abnormally high pressure, such as might result from a blocked line.

### 7.2 Strip and panel distributors

**Panel distributors** cover more leading-edge area and are generally efficient and economical. However, they are unsuitable for complex **double-curvature** surfaces, such as certain fins, which require **strip distributors**.

The panel assembly includes a **porous outer surface**, a **microporous sheet**, and a **backplate**. The outer panel extends beyond the microporous insert so that the assembly can be screwed over, or recessed into, the leading edge. A **metering-tube connector** passes through and is bolted to the backplate.

## 8\. Windscreen and Rain Protection

### 8.1 Available methods

Flight-deck windows may use **fluid spray**, **electrical heating**, and supplied **cabin air for demisting**. Heating may be built into the windscreen or provided by a small separate heated-glass panel ahead of it. Some aircraft also have powered **wipers**, **washing sprays**, and a **rain-repellent liquid**.

### 8.2 Windscreen wipers

The two pilots usually have **independent two-speed units**. The drive can be electrical or hydraulic; some installations include a **park** function. Operating wipers on a **dry window** can cause damage and should be avoided.

### 8.3 Windscreen washer equipment

A washer system sends cleaning fluid onto the glass while the wipers operate. In the example arrangement, a **single washer-button** commands fluid for both windscreens. The reservoir contains about **one gallon**, may be mounted in an **underfloor equipment bay**, and is equipped with a **sight gauge** for checking replenishment. A pump feeds **four spray nozzles**, while cockpit-operated distribution/control valves can select which window receives fluid.

### 8.4 Rain-repellent spray

The chapter's example uses **four valve/timer nozzles—two per windscreen**—and a distribution manifold carrying fluid from a **screw-in disposable aerosol container**. A **low-fluid float indication** and a **pressure gauge with green/red sectors** indicate usability. A visible refill float or pressure in the red area signals an exhausted container.

The crew activates repellent through the relevant wiper panel. The container valve opens for about **one-third of a second per button actuation**, regardless of how long the button remains held. A single spray lasts approximately **2–5 minutes**, depending on airspeed and rain rate, and a full container provides roughly **75 applications**.

**Important:** The chapter cautions that repellent on a **dry windscreen reduces visibility**, and simultaneous operation of repellent and the wipers should be avoided in the installation described.

### 8.6 Electrically heated laminated windscreen

An electrically heated windscreen helps prevent **ice and mist**, while keeping the glass warmer also supports **impact resistance** at low temperature. Heat comes from a **thin-film resistance element** supplied with aircraft AC. The amount of energy needed depends on the area being heated and the outside conditions.

Laminated temperature regulation is important because excessive heat can permanently harm the **vinyl interlayer**, producing bubbles or discoloration. A typical design embeds **two temperature-sensitive wire-grid elements** whose resistances change with glass temperature. One is for normal control; the other independently senses overheat. Lamps and, in some types, magnetic indicators show **NORMAL**, **OFF**, or **OVERHEAT** states.

### 8.7 Normal cycling, high power, and overheat

Under standard operation, the controller energizes the film. Once the target temperature is reached, a change in the sensing resistance causes heating to be **isolated or reduced**. As glass cools, heating resumes. This creates a repeating temperature-control cycle.

If normal control fails and the glass becomes too hot, a separate **overheat circuit** cuts supply and turns on a warning. Certain systems permit automatic resumption once the glass cools sufficiently; others incorporate **lockout**, in which heating remains disabled until the control is switched **OFF** and then **ON** again.

For more demanding icing, the system may select **HIGH** power, receiving a higher-voltage tap from the **autotransformer** while retaining temperature regulation. Some installations reduce available window-heating voltage during ground testing by using **landing-gear shock-strut microswitches**.

## 9\. Propeller Ice Protection

### 9.1 Why propeller ice matters

An ice-coated propeller blade no longer has its intended aerofoil section. This can reduce thrust-producing efficiency and produce **unequal blade loading**, leading to imbalance and potentially destructive vibration. The chapter discusses two main approaches: **fluid anti-icing** and **electrical thermal de-icing**.

### 9.2 Fluid propeller anti-icing

A fluid film acts as a **freezing-point depressant** after mixing with water on the blade. Liquid arrives at a **slinger ring** positioned behind the propeller hub from a tank and delivery pipe.

Two distribution arrangements are described:

* **With blade overshoes:** fluid enters a trough at the boot root and moves outward in **longitudinal grooves** under centrifugal force.
* **Without overshoes:** pipes carry fluid from the slinger ring to blade roots; centrifugal action then spreads it across the blades.

Supply may be by an **independent electrically driven pump**, potentially with adjustable speed through a **rheostat**, or by **air pressure**. Check valves may limit fluid loss when the pump stops. A typical pressure is **10 psi**. Air-driven installations normally use a **relief valve** in the pressure line and a **flow-control valve** to meter the fluid.

### 9.3 Electrical propeller heating

For thermal de-icing, heating elements are bonded around the **blade leading edges**. On turboprop examples, **woven or sprayed conductors** may also heat the forward surface of the **spinner**. Depending on the aircraft, the supply can be **DC or AC** and is delivered in a prescribed pattern by a **cyclic timer**. On turboprops, propeller heating is often coordinated with the **engine inlet anti-icing/de-icing circuitry**.

### 9.4 Heating overshoe construction

One common build interweaves heating resistance wire with **glass fibres**, making a glass-cloth base enclosed between rubber sheets. A **wire-gauze protective layer** lies beneath the outer rubber covering. The completed assembly follows the blade leading-edge shape and is **cemented** to the blade. On some propellers the blade is recessed so the attached overshoe lies **flush** with the surrounding surface.

### 9.5 Supplying power to a rotating blade

Electrical current reaches the rotating elements through **cables, slip rings and carbon-type brushes in a brush housing**. Slip rings are normally behind the hub or at the **starter ring gear**, while the brush unit is fixed to the **engine front casing**; some designs reverse their arrangement. The cable installation must permit the full specified range of **propeller pitch movement**.

### 9.6 Cyclic heat control

Continuous propeller heating would consume considerable power. A cyclic system allows a thin layer of ice to form temporarily, then briefly heats the blade before that layer becomes large enough to affect performance appreciably. The existing ice also acts as an insulating layer; melting at the **ice–blade interface** releases the main deposit, which is then removed by **centrifugal force and airflow**.

## 10\. Other Aircraft Components Requiring Heat

Beyond the main wing, inlet, window and propeller systems, aircraft may need targeted electrical or other heating for:

* **Pitot heads and probes**
* **Alpha (angle-of-attack) probes**
* **Q-feel probes**
* **P1 probes**
* **Waste-water drain horns**
* **Total-air-temperature probes/heads**
* **Aerials**
* **Water pipes**, including fitted **in-line heaters**

These components often have narrow profiles or small passageways and may become unusable or inaccurate if ice obstructs them.

## 12\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions and Answers

**1. Why can a small amount of frost be dangerous even when the wing's basic contour looks unchanged?**  
Its roughness reduces boundary-layer energy, promotes earlier separation, and lowers the maximum lift available.

**2. What three forms of ice are distinguished in the chapter?**  
Hoar frost, rime ice, and clear/glaze ice.

**3. What happens to drag, maximum lift coefficient and stall speed with substantial wing icing?**  
Drag rises, (C\_{L\\max}) falls, and stall speed rises.

**4. How much may heavy hard frost raise stall speed in the textbook example?**  
Approximately **5–10%**.

**5. Why might early take-off acceleration fail to reveal an icing problem?**  
The initial drag increase can be relatively small, while the loss of maximum lift becomes critical near lift-off and initial climb.

**6. What typically causes airframe ice in flight?**  
Supercooled liquid droplets freezing after striking exposed surfaces.

**7. Name three effects that add heat in the chapter's icing-energy balance.**  
Kinetic airflow heating, heating due to droplet impact, and latent heat released by freezing.

**8. Which two processes remove heat from the affected surface?**  
Evaporation and convection.

**9. Why are droplet interception rate and actual ice growth not always the same?**  
Some droplets may not freeze, depending on the heat balance and surface temperature.

**10. Distinguish anti-icing from de-icing.**  
Anti-icing attempts to prevent accumulation; de-icing removes deposits already formed.

**11. Which detector in this chapter senses icing conditions rather than requiring ice accumulation?**  
The Sangamo Weston detector.

**12. What are the Smiths detector's forward and rear openings?**  
Four leading-edge openings and two trailing-edge openings.

**13. What indicates ice to a Napier rotary detector?**  
Higher torque as its cutter removes accreted ice, moving the motor mounting and operating a microswitch.

**14. At approximately what frequency does the Rosemount sensor vibrate?**  
About **35 kHz**.

**15. How long does the Rosemount detector's example probe-heater cycle operate after a warning?**  
**Six seconds**.

**16. What measured ice thickness is associated with the beta-particle detector threshold?**  
Approximately **0.4 mm**.

**17. What environmental combination is used in the chapter's broad engine-ice-protection example?**  
IOAT **+10°C or below** together with **visible moisture**, subject to the relevant aircraft procedure.

**18. What is the purpose of applying vacuum to deflated de-icer boots?**  
To keep the boots flat against the leading edge and limit drag.

**19. What are the three typical pneumatic-boot groups?**  
Wing outboard, wing inboard, and fin/tailplane.

**20. What is the active de-icing cycle time in the illustrated boot system?**  
**34 seconds**, with varying intervals between cycles.

**21. Give the example delays between boot cycles for light and heavy ice.**  
**206 seconds** in light icing and **26 seconds** in heavy icing.

**22. Name the three heated-air supply methods described.**  
Compressor bleed, exhaust heat exchanger, and combustion heating.

**23. Why is ice entering a turbine-engine compressor hazardous?**  
It can obstruct inlet airflow and, after shedding, damage compressor components.

**24. Why are some heater-mat sections powered continuously while others are timed?**  
Continuous zones prevent ice; cyclic zones periodically release accumulated ice while managing electrical demand.

**25. What are the example FAST and SLOW heater-mat cycles?**  
FAST: **2 minutes** at **−6°C to +10°C**. SLOW: **6 minutes** below **−6°C**.

**26. What is the function of a freezing-point-depressant fluid system?**  
To feed protective liquid onto exposed surfaces, lowering the freezing tendency of water on those surfaces.

**27. Why are strip distributors preferred on some fin surfaces?**  
They can suit double-curvature areas where a large panel distributor is impractical.

**28. What are the typical washer-reservoir capacity and total spray-nozzle count in the example?**  
Approximately **one gallon** and **four nozzles**.

**29. For about how long does one rain-repellent activation open the valve?**  
Approximately **one-third of a second**.

**30. Roughly how many applications does a charged example repellent container contain?**  
About **75**.

**31. Why does a heated laminated windscreen need overheat protection?**  
Excessive temperature can permanently damage or discolour the vinyl interlayer.

**32. What is the function of an autotransformer in the example windscreen heater?**  
It provides selectable supply-voltage taps, including a higher-power setting for severe icing.

**33. What transfers anti-icing liquid onto rotating propeller blades?**  
A slinger ring and centrifugal distribution, sometimes aided by grooved rubber overshoes.

**34. How does electrical power pass from the stationary aircraft to spinning blade heaters?**  
Through cable connections, slip rings, and stationary brush assemblies.

**35. Why is electrical propeller de-icing applied cyclically?**  
To reduce sustained power consumption while releasing small amounts of ice before they disrupt blade performance.
