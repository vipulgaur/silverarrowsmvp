# 1\. Introduction: Why Aircraft Need Landing Gear

**Landing gear (undercarriage)** gives the aircraft the support and control it needs during ground operations and landing. Its three principal functions are:

1. **Ground manoeuvring:** Allow the aircraft to taxi and be moved around the aerodrome.
2. **Supporting the airframe:** Hold the aircraft at a practical height, providing clearance for **propellers, flaps, and other low-mounted parts**, while also making loading and unloading possible.
3. **Landing-energy absorption and deceleration:** Absorb the kinetic energy associated with touchdown and provide the means to control the aircraft's slowing-down after landing.

The gear must consequently perform several jobs that do not exist during normal airborne flight. It must carry aircraft weight, withstand dynamic touchdown loads, remain controllable on the ground, and transfer these forces safely into the supporting airframe.

# 2\. Landing Gear Design

In flight, landing gear provides no useful aerodynamic function. It becomes **additional mass** and, when exposed to airflow, also adds drag. In principle, an entirely ground-based support arrangement would avoid carrying that extra equipment in the air. Although ground equipment might replace certain supporting or manoeuvring functions, the textbook explains that a satisfactory replacement has not been found for the **energy-absorbing function at landing**.

Undercarriage designers therefore try to achieve two competing aims:

* Provide sufficient strength and shock absorption for take-off, landing, taxiing, and ground handling.
* Reduce **gear weight** and, for retractable designs, the **volume occupied when stowed**.

The large amount of engineering effort devoted to the undercarriage reflects this compromise.

# 3\. Landing Gear Types — Fixed or Retractable

The basic choice is whether the undercarriage remains exposed during flight or can be moved into the airframe.

|Feature|Fixed landing gear|Retractable landing gear|
|-|-|-|
|Arrangement|Stays in its normal position during flight|Stows in a wing or fuselage compartment|
|Typical suitability|Slower, lighter aircraft; some larger types where simplicity matters most|Faster aircraft and most modern transport aircraft|
|Main benefit|Straightforward construction, reduced maintenance, lower initial cost|Lower drag and improved flight performance|
|Principal compromise|Exposed landing gear reduces aerodynamic performance|More weight, more complicated systems, and additional maintenance|

For an aircraft flying relatively slowly, the aerodynamic penalty of a fixed installation may be an acceptable trade for its simplicity. As operating speeds increase, the **drag penalty becomes more significant**, making retraction increasingly worthwhile.

# 4\. Fixed Landing Gear

The chapter identifies **three common shock-absorbing arrangements** used in fixed undercarriages:

1. Spring-steel legs.
2. Rubber-cord shock absorbers.
3. Oleo-pneumatic struts.

Other arrangements also exist, including **rubber working in compression, coil springs, and liquid-spring struts**.

## 4.1 Spring-Steel Legs

A spring-steel leg is normally found at a **main-gear position**. Its resilient structural member may be made from a **tube** or a **tapered strip of spring steel**. The top end is bolted to the fuselage, whereas the bottom end carries the axle to which the **wheel and brake assembly** are attached.

When a landing or ground load is applied, the steel leg flexes and acts as the spring element of the undercarriage. Its construction avoids the complexity of a separate fluid-and-gas shock absorber.

## 4.2 Rubber-Cord Shock Absorbers

With rubber-cord suspension, the gear normally uses **tubular struts** arranged so that touchdown forces stretch or load **several turns of elastic rubber cord**. The rubber is typically formed into loops or a **grommet-like** arrangement.

The key point is that the aircraft's shock load is carried by the rubber element through the geometry of the undercarriage struts.

## 4.3 Oleo-Pneumatic Struts

An **oleo-pneumatic strut** absorbs shocks using **hydraulic fluid and compressed gas**. The source notes that these struts appear on some fixed main gears and on most fixed nose gears, although designs vary.

### Aerodynamic spats and grass operations

An oleo-type fixed undercarriage may be fitted with **spats**, which are streamlined fairings intended to reduce the drag created by the exposed landing-gear assembly. Spats also create a drawback on grass airfields: **mud may accumulate inside them** during a take-off or landing.

This accumulation increases the aircraft's weight and can adversely affect take-off performance. The textbook specifies that, if mud has been collected, the spats must be **removed, cleaned, and refitted before the following take-off**.

# 5\. Construction of Oleo-Pneumatic Struts

Figure 3.1 represents a relatively simple **nosewheel oleo strut** incorporating steering-related fittings. The structure includes:

* An **outer cylinder**, attached firmly to the aircraft by **two mounting brackets**.
* An **inner cylinder** and piston assembly housed inside it.
* A cavity partly filled with **hydraulic fluid** and charged with compressed **air or nitrogen**.
* **Torque links**, also known as **scissor links**, connecting the moving inner assembly to the steering collar and restricting unwanted relative movement.
* **Steering-collar arms** linked to the rudder pedals through **spring struts**.
* A **shimmy damper** connected to the steering collar.

The inner cylinder can move vertically within the outer housing and, in this steering arrangement, rotate as required. The torque-link connection and steering components govern those movements, while the spring struts accommodate wheel travel without passing all ground shocks into the rudder-control system.

# 6\. Oleo-Pneumatic Strut Operation

The absorber combines two distinct effects: **compressed gas acts as the spring**, while **restricted liquid movement provides damping**.

## 6.1 Aircraft Standing on Its Wheels

On the ground, the aircraft's static weight is opposed by gas pressure inside the strut. The inner cylinder settles at approximately the **middle of its operating stroke** in the example described. In other words, there is still movement available both for compression and for extension.

## 6.2 Compression During Touchdown

When a wheel encounters an upward landing force:

1. The strut **shortens** and the inner cylinder moves upward.
2. Hydraulic fluid is driven through the narrow passage between the **piston orifice** and the **metering rod**.
3. Because that passage restricts flow, the inner cylinder cannot move upward instantly; its travel is **damped**.
4. Compression also reduces the internal gas volume, causing **gas pressure to rise**.
5. Gas pressure increases until it can balance the applied upward force.

Thus, the strut dissipates the severity of a shock through resistance to oil flow while the gas compresses to provide its elastic response.

## 6.3 Extension After the Shock

As the upward load begins to disappear, the compressed gas attempts to expand. It drives the inner cylinder **back outward**. The oil must again pass through a restricted opening, limiting the **speed of extension** and preventing an uncontrolled rebound.

## 6.4 Taxiing and Steering

Small bumps during taxiing are cushioned by the gas and moderated by restricted liquid flow. For the steerable nose arrangement, movement of the rudder pedals rotates the steering assembly and wheel; the **spring struts** allow the wheel to move up and down without transmitting the full vertical shock into the rudder pedals and controls.

## 6.5 Indications of Gas Leakage

A strut losing its gas charge may sit **lower than its normal extension**. Unequal lengths of exposed **fescalized metal** on the two main-gear struts can also indicate abnormal servicing or leakage. The source uses *fescalized metal* for the bright, hard-coated, shiny external strut surface.

**Key distinction:** A strut that is low because of gas leakage does not have the same static extension as a correctly charged strut.

# 7\. Retractable Landing Gear

Most modern transport aeroplanes, along with an increasing share of light aircraft, use retractable undercarriages to reduce drag and improve airborne performance.

**Hydraulic power** is the usual method of retraction, but **pneumatic** and **electric** systems are also used. Some mechanisms apply power only to raise the landing gear, relying on **gravity and slipstream** to help lower it.

Retractable systems also require equipment beyond the basic gear legs:

* **Up and down locks** to secure each undercarriage in both extreme positions.
* **Position indicators** that tell the crew where the landing gear is and whether it has locked.
* An **alternative extension method** for use after failure of the normal power source.
* Protection against **accidental retraction on the ground**.
* A warning arrangement that helps prevent an approach or landing with the gear retracted or unlocked.
* **Wheel-well doors** that close off the compartments for better aerodynamics.

# 8\. Design and Construction of Retractable Gear

There is no universal arrangement for the size, location or geometry of undercarriage units. These decisions are made during aircraft design and reflect the aircraft's particular operational and structural requirements.

## 8.1 Tricycle and Tailwheel Layouts

The source distinguishes two broad configurations:

* **Tricycle undercarriage:** Two principal main-gear units and a nosewheel.
* **Tailwheel undercarriage (taildragger):** Main gear supported by a tailwheel arrangement rather than a nosewheel.

In the usual **tricycle layout**, the main units lie **just aft of the centre of gravity (C of G)**. They carry **up to approximately 90% of the aircraft's weight** and absorb the **initial landing shock**. The nose unit helps keep the aircraft approximately level and, on most types, serves as a steering mechanism.

The chapter contrasts tricycle gear with a taildragger: the tricycle arrangement avoids the particular risk of pitching onto the nose while taxiing in a strong tailwind and has **less tendency to ground-loop**.

# 9\. Factors Affecting Design and Construction

The main variables identified by the textbook are:

1. **Aircraft dimensions:** Larger aircraft require a different supporting arrangement from smaller types.
2. **Aircraft weight:** Gear legs, wheels and attachments must carry the intended loads.
3. **Aircraft role:** Cargo, passenger and other operational requirements influence the arrangement.
4. **High-wing or low-wing configuration:** Wing placement affects leg length and possible attachment points.
5. **Performance requirements:** Aerodynamic drag and the need for retraction affect the choice.
6. **Airframe construction and stowage space:** The structure must provide suitable mounting and room to house the gear after retraction.

## 9.1 Other Factors: Multi-Role Aircraft

Cost pressures and the desire to accommodate both **freight and passengers** have influenced aircraft layouts. For some multi-role designs, a **high wing** allows the freight floor to remain near the ground, improving the practical handling of cargo.

However, a high wing may be **as much as 20 ft above ground level**, making a long, wing-mounted main undercarriage impractical to build with sufficient strength. Accordingly, certain aircraft locate the main gear in or on the **fuselage**, rather than stretching long gear legs down from the wing.

# 11\. Fuselage Mounted Landing Gear

A fuselage-supported main undercarriage performs essentially the same roles as wing-mounted gear, but its architecture creates some distinct issues:

* If the layout cannot use an appropriate **geometric lock**, another method must retain the unit in both **UP** and **DOWN** positions.
* According to wheel arrangement, **individual wheels** may need their **own shock absorber** and sometimes an individual steering motor.
* The position of the gear can make it **more accessible in flight**, potentially allowing **manual emergency lowering**.

The specific arrangement depends on how the aircraft structure supports, retracts and restrains the gear.

# 12\. Loads Sustained by the Landing Gear

Landing gear endures different kinds of force throughout its service life. All these loads are transmitted through the gear attachments into the surrounding airframe; therefore, **the mounting structure must be strong enough as well as the gear itself**.

|Load|Situation identified by the textbook|
|-|-|
|**Compression**|Static weight on the ground and extra forces at touchdown|
|**Rearward bending**|Forces acting toward the rear during ground operation|
|**Side load**|Crosswind take-offs, crosswind landings, and taxi turns|
|**Forward load**|Pushing the aircraft backwards during pushback|
|**Torsion**|Twisting forces associated with ground manoeuvring|

A landing-gear unit must therefore be considered as a **load-bearing system in several directions**, not merely as a vertical support.

# 13\. Nose Undercarriage

A typical nose landing-gear assembly is **lighter than a main unit**, because it supports a smaller portion of aircraft weight and is commonly subject mainly to **direct compressive loading**. Nevertheless, towing equipment connects to this unit, so it must also resist substantial **shear forces**.

The nose-gear design must accommodate five requirements:

1. **Castoring** — freedom to turn as necessary.
2. **Self-centring** — alignment with the aircraft's fore-and-aft direction.
3. **Steering** — commanded turns for ground handling.
4. **Anti-shimmy** — control of unwanted oscillations.
5. **Shear strength** — ability to withstand side-related loads, including towing forces.

## 13.1 Castoring

**Castoring** is the nosewheel's ability to **turn to either side** in response to forces such as **differential braking** or the aerodynamic effect of the **rudder**.

The wheel must be able to pivot while the gear simultaneously carries compression and shear loads. Combining this freedom of rotation with structural load carrying creates a particular challenge for the **bearing arrangement**.

# 14\. Self-Centring

A retracting nose undercarriage must reach the **fore-and-aft centre position before it enters the wheel well**. If the nosewheel remains turned, it may not fit inside the limited stowage space; retraction pressure could then force it against the structure, producing severe damage.

The source describes two methods of automatic centring:

* A **spring-loaded cam**.
* A **hydraulic dashpot** or hydraulic centring arrangement.

**Reason for centring:** The requirement comes from **stowage clearance and protection against structural interference**, not simply from steering convenience.

# 15\. Nose Wheel Steering

Ground steering gives the pilot controlled movement of the aircraft around taxiways and other manoeuvring areas. Older arrangements could rely on **differential braking**; powered steering has become common on large commercial aeroplanes.

Hydraulic nosewheel steering allows an aircraft to taxi with its engines near **minimum required thrust**, an advantage for large jet aircraft because it saves fuel. The source also identifies **more accurate directional control**, **reduced tyre and brake wear**, and **less noise** as benefits.

## 15.1 Free Castoring for Towing

For situations such as towing, the hydraulic steering circuit needs to permit the wheel to turn freely. A **bypass** connects the steering-jack sides so fluid can move from one side to the other. When powered steering is selected, hydraulic pressure **closes the bypass**, making normal steering control possible.

## 15.2 Pilot Steering Inputs

According to the aircraft, steering commands may come from:

* A dedicated **steering handwheel/tiller**.
* The **rudder pedals**.

The system described also incorporates a **self-centring jack** and a **shimmy damper**.

# 16\. Power Steering Systems

Light aircraft can use a direct **mechanical connection** between the rudder pedals and the nosewheel. Larger aircraft typically need powered steering, using **electric, pneumatic or, most often, hydraulic** energy to rotate the nose assembly.

A typical **hydraulic power-steering** arrangement has:

1. A cockpit **steering wheel or tiller**.
2. A **control valve** that directs fluid according to the pilot's command.
3. **Steering cylinders/jacks** that move the nose-gear mechanism.
4. A **mechanical follow-up or feedback linkage** that holds the gear at the selected steering angle.
5. A **hydraulic supply**, normally powered by **engine-driven pumps**.

# 17\. Nose Wheel Steering Operation

The normal pressure supply for the system described comes from the landing-gear **DOWN line**. A **hydraulic accumulator** provides a limited emergency pressure source. A **change-over valve** ensures that steering is available only when the nose landing gear is in its **down** position.

## 17.1 Normal Steering

When the pilot turns the steering control:

1. Mechanical linkage moves the **steering control valve** in the direction and amount commanded.
2. Pressure is routed to the appropriate **steering jacks**.
3. The jacks **extend or retract**, turning the nose shock-strut assembly inside its housing.
4. A **follow-up linkage** responds as the nosewheel turns and progressively brings the control valve back toward its centred position.
5. If the pilot releases the steering wheel, **centring springs** restore the valve to neutral; the wheel is then free to **castor**.

## 17.2 Self-Centring During Retraction

Inside each steering jack is an **inner cylinder** connected to the landing-gear **UP line**. When UP is selected, pressure reaches these inner cylinders, making the steering jacks extend **equally** and straighten the nosewheel.

Only after this centring action does pressure act on the **nose retraction jack**. At the same time, the bypass valve supplies a return route for fluid from the steering jacks.

This sequencing avoids retracting the gear while the wheel is still steered to one side.

## 17.3 Castoring in Neutral and During Towing

With the steering control valve in **neutral**, fluid may transfer between the steering jacks. This allows the wheel to swivel during towing or return toward centre after a commanded turn. When the wheel is moved by towing forces, the **follow-up linkage** can transmit that motion back toward the cockpit steering wheel.

Some installations provide **quick-release pins** so the steering jacks can be disconnected for the large steering angles needed during ground servicing.

## 17.4 Steering Damping

**Pipeline restrictors** situated between the control valve and the steering jacks moderate hydraulic flow and therefore damp the steering movement, rather than allowing abrupt travel.

# 18\. Nose Wheel Shimmy

**Shimmy** is an unwanted, rapid, approximately **sinusoidal vibration or oscillation** of the nose undercarriage. The flexibility of the tyre sidewalls contributes to this unstable movement.

Severe shimmy, especially at speed, can send vibrations through the entire aircraft and become dangerous.

### Factors that increase shimmy tendency

* **Worn or broken torque links**.
* **Worn wheel bearings**.
* **Unequal tyre pressures**.

### Methods described to reduce shimmy

1. Arrange a **hydraulic lock** across the steering-jack piston.
2. Install a **hydraulic damper**.
3. Use strong **self-centring springs**.
4. Fit **double nosewheels**.
5. Use **twin-contact wheels**.

The purpose of all these arrangements is to restrain or damp the unwanted oscillatory motion.

# 19\. Undercarriage Configuration

As aircraft became heavier and larger, the load imposed on each wheel also increased. The chapter defines **wheel loading** as the **static force on each wheel when the aircraft is at its take-off weight**. Since most aircraft weight rests on the **main landing gear**, main-wheel loading is particularly important.

Wheel loading influences the strength of runway surface needed. An aircraft with excessively concentrated wheel loads may damage a **low-strength runway**, which makes gear configuration directly relevant to the aircraft's intended role.

## 19.1 Spreading Load Across More Wheels

Strengthening long transport-aircraft runways can be prohibitively costly. Instead of requiring a very large, single wheel with a high-pressure tyre, a transport aircraft can distribute its weight over **several smaller wheels with lower-pressure tyres**.

The examples in the textbook include the **Boeing 747, Boeing 777, and Airbus A340**, with total landing-gear wheel counts quoted in the range **10–18**. Larger aircraft may have **more than two main legs**, with wheels arranged on **wing and body gear**, so that the total load is spread across more runway contact points.

A designer also has to consider where the wheel assemblies will go when **retracted**, not just how effectively they support the aircraft on the runway.

## 19.2 Advantages of Multiple Wheels

|Advantage|Explanation|
|-|-|
|**Potential weight reduction**|More wheels can allow each individual wheel to be smaller and lighter; the source notes that a direct single-wheel comparison is difficult because a single unit would be impractical on very large types.|
|**Easier individual servicing**|Although the complete assembly becomes more complicated, individual wheel and brake components are smaller, closer to ground level, and generally easier to replace.|
|**Greater reserve after a tyre burst**|Other wheels remain available to support the load if one tyre fails.|
|**Stowage flexibility**|Multiple smaller wheels may be easier to house in the available spaces, although the gear must still be specially designed around the aircraft's structural layout.|

A **thin wing** leaves little internal space. The designer may need **folding or swivelling bogies**, which add cost and make routine servicing more difficult. Placing the gear within the fuselage can simplify the stowage arrangement and permit **essentially vertical raising and lowering**.

## 19.3 Main Disadvantage: Turning and Tyre Scrub

The large footprint of a multi-wheel bogie can make the whole assembly **crab or resist the turn**. This can require a **larger turning radius** and complicate manoeuvring on the ground.

During a tight turn, tyres tend to **scrub across the surface** rather than roll perfectly in line with the direction of travel. The smaller the turn radius, the greater the associated tread forces. Severe scrubbing may **tear or split the tread**, potentially exposing the underlying **casing fabric**.

The textbook accordingly recommends **wide turning circles where possible**, avoiding unnecessary tight turns, and moving **straight ahead for a short distance before stopping** to reduce the effects of tyre scrub.

# 20\. Landing Gear Operation on Contaminated Runways

On a slush-covered runway—where **water, wet snow and ice** may be present—contamination can be picked up by landing-gear parts during the take-off run. If this slush later **freezes inside the wheel well during climb or cruise**, the undercarriage may be unable to lower at the destination.

The edition describes a possible precaution when departure in those conditions is unavoidable: cycling the gear **UP → DOWN → UP** shortly after take-off so movement and shock may shake off deposits.

**Operational caution:** This is the textbook's explanation of a risk and a suggested response, **not a universal instruction**. In an actual aircraft, contaminated-runway and landing-gear operation must follow that model's approved procedures.

# 21\. A Hydraulic Gear Retraction System

Hydraulically actuated landing gear commonly receives pressure from **engine-driven hydraulic pumps**, with an **alternative source or system** available if normal pumping power is lost.

Some light aeroplanes use a more compact **power pack**, housing a reservoir and **gear/flap selector valves**. It may include an **electric pump**, or it may receive power from an engine-driven pump. In the arrangement discussed, hydraulic power raises the landing gear while **gravity (free fall)** and **spring-strut assistance** allow it to lower.

## 21.1 How the Illustrated System Retracts

### Stage 1 — Selector UP / initial movement

When the landing-gear selector is placed in **UP**:

1. Pressure is connected to the **UP line**.
2. The **DOWN line** is connected to a return path.

4. At the same time, pressure reaches the **NLG jack**, retracting the nose gear.

6. The sequence system coordinates release of the **MLG downlock** and actuation of the **MLG jack**. In this geometry, the main jack **extends to raise the main undercarriage**.

# 22\. A Pneumatic Retraction System

A pneumatically powered undercarriage follows a similar broad raise/lower logic, but it uses **compressed air** rather than hydraulic liquid. Its crucial difference is that **air returning from a line is discharged into the atmosphere through the selector valve**, rather than returned as fluid to a hydraulic reservoir.

## 22.1 Air Supply

**Engine-driven air pumps** charge a main **storage cylinder**. Pressure is supplied from that storage cylinder through a **pressure-reducing valve** to the landing-gear selector.

## 22.2 Retraction: Selector UP

With **UP** selected, the selector sends pneumatic pressure into the **UP pipelines**, operating the retraction rams. The **DOWN line** is simultaneously opened to atmosphere so air in that line can escape.

## 22.3 Extension: Selector DOWN

Selecting **DOWN** sends air via a **second pressure-reducing valve** to the **DOWN lines**, **uplock rams**, and **retraction rams** as required for extension.

The source emphasises that extension occurs at a **lower pneumatic pressure**. This serves the same protective purpose as a **hydraulic flow restrictor**: it prevents the landing gear from extending so violently that its structure or attachments are damaged.

## 22.4 Damping of Pneumatic Retraction Rams

Despite their air-powered actuation, the rams are often damped using **oil or grease**. The **hollow piston rod** contains the viscous material. As the ram moves in either direction, it drives this material through a narrow gap between the rod's inner wall and a **fixed damper piston**, slowing the movement.

## 22.5 Uplocks, Downlocks and Doors

* **Downlocking** can be obtained by an **over-centre geometry** of the drag strut at the end of the ram's stroke.
* **Uplocks** can use **spring-ram-operated locks**.
* During **retraction**, the first movement of the ram releases the **downlock**.
* During **extension**, pneumatic pressure acting in the spring ram releases the **uplock**.
* The **undercarriage doors** in this example are mechanically moved by linkage to the **shock-absorber housing**.

Seeing wheels below the aircraft does not necessarily prove that they are **mechanically locked down**. The crew therefore needs a positive indication of the position and locking condition of each gear assembly.

## 24.1 Electrical Indication

Typical systems use **microswitches** on the **uplocks and downlocks**. As a lock engages or disengages, the relevant switch changes electrical state. These signals operate the **landing-gear indicators** on the flight deck.

# 25\. Gear Safety Features

Because a failure to extend, lock or retain the undercarriage is critical, landing-gear systems include protective interlocks and warning arrangements to reduce the risk of incorrect operation.

# 26\. Nose Wheel Centring

Before retracting, the nosewheel must be aligned **straight fore and aft** to avoid colliding with the wheel well or nearby structural components. Hydraulic centring is one method, as covered in the steering-system discussion; automatic arrangements ensure the wheel is properly aligned as part of the retraction sequence.

# 27\. Gear Selector Lock

A **selector lock** prevents a pilot from accidentally commanding gear retraction while the aircraft is **standing on its wheels**.

In the arrangement described, a **spring-loaded plunger** physically holds the selector in its **DOWN** position. A **solenoid** can withdraw the plunger, but electrical power to that solenoid depends on a switch fitted to the **shock-absorber strut** and connected to the aircraft's **air/ground logic**.

The source sequence is:

1. **Aircraft weight on wheels:** The shock strut is compressed and the controlling switch is **open**; the solenoid cannot release the lever.
2. **After take-off:** The strut extends and the switch contacts **close**.
3. The completed electrical circuit energises the solenoid, releasing the selector lock so that **UP** can be selected.

An **override** is available for authorised emergency or maintenance use. It may be a separate **gated electrical switch** or a mechanical method of bypassing the locking plunger.

# 28\. Ground Locks

**Ground locks** (often called **landing-gear locking pins**) prevent the gear from collapsing while an aircraft is parked and **unpowered**. They may take the form of **pins** or **metal sleeves** positioned so that the undercarriage cannot move while they remain installed.

These locks normally carry **high-visibility warning flags**, intended to make a remaining installed lock obvious before flight.

The textbook emphasises that, before departure, the locks should be:

* **Removed** from the undercarriage.
* **Stowed on board** as required by that installation's procedures.
* Positively reported to the flight crew as **removed and safely stowed**.

# 29\. Warning Devices

The purpose of a gear-warning device is to prevent landing with the undercarriage **retracted or not correctly locked**.

In the system described, a **warning horn** is connected to a **throttle-operated switch**. When one or more throttles are reduced below approximately **one-third open**, as might occur on approach, the horn sounds if the landing gear is **not down and locked**.

## 29.1 Horn Isolation and Airspeed Switches

An **isolation switch** may silence the horn during certain training exercises or maintenance procedures. It carries a risk: it might be left isolated inadvertently.

The chapter regards an **airspeed-controlled switch** as advantageous because it automatically suppresses the warning in suitable conditions without requiring the crew to remember to reset a manually selected horn isolation. An airspeed switch can also keep the horn quiet during an **initial descent from high altitude**.

## 29.2 GPWS — Ground Proximity Warning System

The source states that the relevant **GPWS condition below 500 ft** is inhibited only when the **landing gear is down and locked** and the **flaps are in their landing position**. This reference is to the configuration-warning logic discussed in the textbook, not a universal description of every GPWS function on every aircraft.

For a fuller treatment, the chapter refers readers to **Warnings and Recording, Book 5**.

# 30\. Landing Gear Operating Speeds

Two similarly named speed limits govern different landing-gear conditions.

## 30.1 V\_LO — Landing Gear Operating Speed

**V\_LO** is the **maximum permitted airspeed for operating the landing gear**. It must not be exceeded while the gear is **extending or retracting**.

## 30.2 V\_LE — Landing Gear Extended Speed

**V\_LE** is the **maximum permitted airspeed with the landing gear extended**.

|Speed|What it limits|When it is relevant|
|-|-|-|
|**V\_LO**|Gear **movement**|During extension or retraction|
|**V\_LE**|Flight with gear **already extended**|Whenever flying with gear down|

### Why the speeds are different

A fully extended or fully retracted undercarriage is normally **locked in position**, enabling it to resist aerodynamic loading more effectively. While the unit is travelling between those positions, it is not secured by an end-position lock and the **retraction/extension mechanism** must carry the loads. Certain designs also swivel or fold the gear while stowing it, potentially creating unusual aerodynamic effects at high speed.

The source points out that aircraft normally slow further as landing approaches, so a pilot will seldom need to accelerate toward V\_LO after extension. Nevertheless, if an aircraft must fly a long distance **with the gear down**, such as on a maintenance ferry flight, **V\_LE** remains the applicable gear-extended limit.

# 31\. Emergency Lowering Systems

Landing-gear designs include an alternate way of **lowering and locking the gear** if the usual extension system fails.

## 31.1 Gravity / Free-Fall Extension

On some aircraft the crew can release the **uplocks mechanically or electrically**. The gear then drops under **its own weight**, and **mechanical downlocks** engage to secure it.

When the main power source has failed, the **gear doors may stay open**, because no power remains to retract them after the gear has lowered. On certain aircraft these doors could touch the runway during landing unless contact is gentle. The textbook notes that some doors have **frangible lower portions**, designed to break away in a controlled manner and reduce subsequent replacement difficulties.

## 31.2 Emergency Pressure Extension

Other installations use a secondary pressure supply, sometimes delivered through **different hydraulic lines** to the gear jacks. Sources identified in the text include:

* A **hydraulic accumulator**.
* A **hand-operated pump**.
* A **pneumatic storage cylinder**.
* An **electrically powered pump**.

A **mechanical indication** is provided to confirm that the gear has reached the **down-and-locked** position.

# 32\. Air/Ground Logic System

Aircraft equipment often needs to behave differently when the aircraft is **on its wheels** versus when it is **airborne**. The **air/ground logic system** supplies the necessary status information to those other circuits.

## 32.1 Strut Microswitches

An early approach fits **microswitches** to the main-gear oleo struts. Their operating state changes with strut extension:

* **On the ground:** The aircraft's weight **compresses** the oleo.
* **After take-off:** The suspended wheel and bogie **extend** the oleo.

A change in switch position therefore provides an indication of whether the aircraft is supported by its landing gear.

## 32.2 Proximity Sensors

On more modern systems, **proximity sensing** replaces mechanical microswitches. The source identifies **inductive or capacitive sensing** methods that detect relative oleo extension or compression and thereby determine an air/ground status.

## 32.3 Relays and Redundancy

Whichever sensing method is used, it sends a control signal to a **relay or group of relays**. Those devices switch the affected aircraft circuits **on or off** in response to the detected ground/air state.

Some aircraft place the relevant sensor on **one main-gear strut**; many use sensors on **both main struts** for extra **redundancy**.

# 33\. Chapter 3 — Key Revision Summary

* Landing gear provides **ground manoeuvrability, structural support/clearance, and touchdown energy absorption with deceleration control**.
* **Fixed gear** is simpler and less expensive but causes drag; **retractable gear** reduces drag at the cost of weight and complexity.
* Fixed gear may use **spring steel, rubber cord, or oleo-pneumatic** shock absorption.
* In an oleo strut, **compressed gas supplies spring action** and **restricted hydraulic flow supplies damping**.
* An oleo strut sitting unusually low can indicate **loss of gas pressure**.
* **Tricycle gear** normally locates its principal gear just behind the C of G and may carry **up to about 90%** of the aircraft's weight.
* Landing gear and mountings must resist **compression, bending, side, forward, and torsional loads**.
* Nose gear must support **steering, castoring, centring, shimmy control, and towing loads**.
* **Powered nosewheel steering** commonly uses hydraulic pressure, steering jacks, feedback linkage, and a bypass for castoring/towing.
* **Shimmy** is unstable nosewheel oscillation aggravated by link, bearing, or tyre problems.
* Multiple wheels **spread runway load**, offer redundancy and easier individual component replacement, but can increase **tyre scrub during tight turns**.
* Slush can freeze in the gear bay and obstruct extension; use approved aircraft procedures for contaminated-runway operations.
* In the hydraulic example, **sequence valves coordinate doors, locks, and jacks**, while **one-way restrictors limit movement speed**.
* Pneumatic gear uses **compressed air** and normally exhausts return air **to atmosphere**; controlled pressure and viscous damping moderate movement.
* Electric gear uses a **reversible motor, gearing, screw jack, torque tube, linkages, limit switches, and mechanical locks**.
* Typical indicators distinguish **down/locked**, **in transit**, and **up/locked**, but exact colour conventions vary by type.
* **Selector locks**, **ground locks**, **gear warning horns**, and **air/ground logic** provide different layers of protection.
* **V\_LO** relates to **gear movement**; **V\_LE** relates to **flight with gear extended**.
* Emergency extension can use **gravity release** or an **alternative power source**, together with a means to verify **downlock**.

# 34\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions

These are newly written revision questions based on the concepts covered in the chapter, rather than copied textbook examination questions.

### 1\. What are the three fundamental purposes of landing gear?

**Answer:** Ground manoeuvring; supporting the aircraft at a suitable height with necessary clearances; and absorbing landing energy while enabling controlled deceleration.

### 2\. Why do faster aircraft commonly have retractable landing gear?

**Answer:** Exposed gear creates aerodynamic drag. Stowing it improves performance, although the retraction mechanism adds mass and complexity.

### 3\. Identify the three main fixed-gear shock-absorber arrangements.

**Answer:** Spring-steel legs, rubber-cord systems, and oleo-pneumatic struts.

### 4\. Which component of an oleo-pneumatic strut acts as a spring, and which action provides damping?

**Answer:** Compressed gas supplies the spring effect; controlled flow of hydraulic fluid through a restriction provides damping.

### 5\. Why does the internal gas pressure rise as an oleo strut compresses?

**Answer:** Compression reduces the space occupied by the gas, increasing its pressure.

### 6\. What visible indication can suggest a loss of oleo gas pressure?

**Answer:** The strut may be less extended than it should be; uneven lengths of exposed shiny/fescalized strut surface may also be seen on main-gear units.

### 7\. Why may fixed-gear spats cause problems on grass airfields?

**Answer:** Mud can collect inside them, adding weight and affecting take-off performance.

### 8\. Where is the main gear placed in a conventional tricycle arrangement, and approximately how much weight might it support?

**Answer:** Just aft of the centre of gravity; up to about 90% of the aircraft's weight in the example given.

### 9\. What practical benefit does the source attribute to tricycle gear compared with a taildragger?

**Answer:** Less tendency to ground-loop and no corresponding tendency to tip onto the nose while taxiing in a strong tailwind.

### 10\. Why might a high-wing freight aircraft use fuselage-mounted main landing gear?

**Answer:** The wing may be too high for a reasonably strong, practical gear leg while the cargo floor needs to remain low for loading.

### 11\. Name five kinds of load an undercarriage must withstand.

**Answer:** Compression, rearward bending, side loads, forward loads and torsion.

### 12\. What is meant by *castoring*?

**Answer:** The ability of a nosewheel to turn in response to forces such as differential braking or rudder aerodynamic effects.

### 13\. Why is automatic nosewheel centring necessary for retractable gear?

**Answer:** A turned nosewheel could strike the surrounding structure or fail to fit into its wheel well during retraction.

### 14\. Name two methods used to centre a nosewheel automatically.

**Answer:** A spring-loaded cam and a hydraulic dashpot/centring arrangement.

### 15\. Which hydraulic line normally supplies powered nosewheel steering in the system described?

**Answer:** The landing-gear DOWN line.

### 16\. What limited emergency steering-pressure source is described?

**Answer:** A hydraulic accumulator.

### 17\. Why is a feedback or follow-up mechanism fitted to the steering valve?

**Answer:** It progressively returns the valve toward neutral as the wheel reaches the commanded angle, allowing the steering position to stabilise.

### 18\. What does the steering bypass allow when the nosewheel needs to castor?

**Answer:** Fluid can transfer between the steering-jack sides, allowing the wheel to swivel.

### 19\. What is nosewheel shimmy?

**Answer:** A rapid, unwanted oscillation of the nose undercarriage that can be promoted by tyre sidewall flexibility and component wear.

### 20\. Give three factors that make nosewheel shimmy more likely.

**Answer:** Worn/broken torque links, worn wheel bearings, and unequal tyre pressures.

### 21\. Why do very large aircraft use multiple landing-gear wheels?

**Answer:** To distribute wheel loads across a larger runway contact area and reduce the load carried by each wheel.

### 22\. What major tyre problem can occur during tight turns on multi-wheel bogies?

**Answer:** Tyre scrubbing may tear or split the tread and expose the casing fabric.

### 23\. Why can a slush-contaminated take-off prevent gear extension later?

**Answer:** Slush deposited on the gear may freeze during climb or cruise and obstruct the mechanism in its bay.

### 24\. What is the purpose of a sequence valve in the illustrated hydraulic gear system?

**Answer:** To make the parts operate in a required order, coordinating locks, jacks and gear doors.

### 25\. What is the function of a one-way restrictor in the lowering system?

**Answer:** It limits return-flow rate in one direction and thereby slows and cushions gear or door movement.

### 26\. What is the main difference between pneumatic and hydraulic return flow in the systems shown?

**Answer:** Pneumatic return air is vented to atmosphere through the selector; hydraulic fluid is routed back into the fluid system.

### 27\. How does the electric gear mechanism shown move the undercarriage?

**Answer:** A reversible motor drives a screw jack and torque tube; rods and cables then move the nose drag strut and main-gear sidestays.

### 28\. What is the function of limit switches in electric landing-gear systems?

**Answer:** They cut off electrical power and brake the motor when the gear reaches its locked end position.

### 29\. Why is seeing the landing gear extended not enough to confirm a safe landing configuration?

**Answer:** The gear may be down but not mechanically locked; the pilot needs positive downlock indication.

### 30\. What does a typical red landing-gear indication represent in the described system?

**Answer:** Gear in transit, between its fully locked positions.

### 31\. How does the selector lock prevent retraction on the ground?

**Answer:** A spring-loaded plunger holds the lever in DOWN until an air/ground-controlled solenoid is energised after the strut extends.

### 32\. What is the purpose of landing-gear ground locks and their warning flags?

**Answer:** The pins/sleeves prevent unpowered gear movement or collapse; the flags alert ground and flight crews that the locks must be removed.

### 33\. When does the source's throttle-operated gear-warning horn sound?

**Answer:** When one or more throttles are below approximately one-third open and the gear is not down and locked.

### 34\. Explain the difference between V\_LO and V\_LE.

**Answer:** V\_LO limits speed **while the gear moves**; V\_LE limits speed **with the gear extended**.

### 35\. Give four possible sources of emergency extension pressure.

**Answer:** A hydraulic accumulator, hand pump, pneumatic storage cylinder or electric pump.

### 36\. What is the main function of an air/ground logic system?

**Answer:** It tells dependent aircraft circuits whether the aircraft is supported on its wheels or airborne, so those circuits can switch to the required state.

### 37\. How may a modern aircraft detect air/ground status without an oleo microswitch?

**Answer:** By using proximity devices with inductive or capacitive sensing of strut extension or compression.

### 38\. Why might air/ground sensors be fitted on both main undercarriages?

**Answer:** To add redundancy to the status-detection system.
