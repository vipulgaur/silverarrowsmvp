# Chapter 7: Flight Control Systems



## 1\. General Arrangement of Aircraft Controls

An aircraft uses a combination of primary flying controls and other movable surfaces to change its attitude, maintain control, and meet take-off, landing and in-flight performance requirements. The surfaces are distributed between the tailplane, fin and wings rather than being grouped in a single location.

The principal items shown in the chapter's overall control arrangement are:

* **Elevator:** Mounted on the horizontal stabilizer; provides pitch control.
* **Rudder:** Mounted on the vertical stabilizer; provides yaw control.
* **Inboard and outboard ailerons:** Wing-mounted surfaces associated with roll control.
* **Inboard and outboard spoilers:** Upper-wing surfaces used in the spoiler system.
* **Inboard and outboard flaps:** Trailing-edge devices for high-lift operation.
* **Leading-edge slats:** Leading-edge devices supporting low-speed flight.
* **Horizontal and vertical stabilizers:** Fixed stabilizing surfaces that carry the relevant movable tail controls.

## 2\. Introduction: Methods of Operating Flight Controls

Moving a cockpit control must produce a corresponding movement of the appropriate aircraft control surface. The means of transmitting the pilot's command can be divided into three broad types:

|Method|How the command reaches the surface|Important qualification|
|-|-|-|
|**Mechanical**|Cables, rods, levers and chains physically link the cockpit control to the surface.|The pilot's mechanical input acts directly through the linkage.|
|**Hydraulic**|Hydraulic power supplies the force needed to move the surface.|The valve which regulates hydraulic power can still be positioned through a mechanical connection.|
|**Electrical**|Moving the cockpit control sends an electrical signal associated with the surface command.|The surface itself may still be driven hydraulically after the electrical command has been received.|

These categories describe the **method of command transmission or actuation**; an electrically commanded system does not necessarily use an electric motor to move the aerodynamic surface.

### 2.1 Mechanical Elevator Control System

A light-aircraft elevator may be connected directly to a control column through a run of control cables, pulleys and connecting hardware. The illustration shows how the cockpit input is transmitted to the elevator and how the travel of the system is limited.

**Direction of movement:**

1. Pulling the control column **rearward** raises the elevator.
2. The upward elevator movement produces a **nose-up pitch** response.
3. Pushing the column in the opposite direction causes the reverse elevator and pitch response.

The arrangement contains both cockpit-side and surface-side movement stops, along with means for adjustment and cable guidance.

### 2.2 Aileron and Rudder Responses

* **Roll:** Turning the control wheel **right** raises the right aileron while lowering the left one, so the aircraft rolls **right**.

* The ailerons and rudder can use mechanical arrangements like the elevator's, including cables, push-pull rods and chains.

## 3\. Primary Controls

**Primary flying controls** are the means used to rotate the aircraft about the three axes, thereby altering its attitude and/or flight path. In this chapter, the set includes the **elevator, rudder, ailerons and roll-control spoilers**.

|Aircraft movement|Main control surface(s)|
|-|-|
|Pitch|Elevator|
|Roll|Ailerons, with roll-control spoilers where installed|
|Yaw|Rudder|

### 3.1 Reversible Manual Control Systems

In a manually operated system, movement can be transmitted **both ways** through the mechanism:

* Moving the cockpit control moves the aerodynamic surface.
* An external force on the surface can, in turn, move the cockpit control.

This is known as a **reversible** system. Its significance is that the pilot can directly sense some of the **aerodynamic loading** on the control surface through the cockpit control forces.

### 3.2 Irreversible Fully Powered Controls and Artificial Feel

A fully powered system is **irreversible**: forces acting on the control surface are not able to back-drive the cockpit control through the power mechanism. Consequently, the pilot does not receive the natural control-force feedback found in a manually operated system.

An **artificial-feel system** must therefore provide the required feedback. In the arrangement described, the resistance felt at the cockpit control is intended to increase with:

1. **Greater control deflection**; and
2. **Increasing aircraft speed**.

**Related distinction:** A **manually set trimming tab** is also described as irreversible. After the pilot fixes its position using the trim wheel, aerodynamic forces applied to that tab do not alter the set trim-wheel position.

> \*\*Important exception:\*\* \*\*Power-assisted\*\* controls can preserve natural feel and remain reversible when the external surface loading is sufficiently large. Do not treat every hydraulically assisted installation as equivalent to a fully irreversible powered system.

## 4\. Control System Checks

Flight-control linkages require checks during servicing and after adjustments. Depending on the circumstances, the pilot may need to carry out some of these checks.

The areas identified in the chapter are:

1. Correct **cable tension**.
2. Effective **locking and safety** of relevant mechanisms.
3. Full **range of control movement**, including freedom from obstruction and movement in the **correct sense**.
4. Acceptable **friction** in the system.
5. Absence of unacceptable **backlash**.

Each check addresses a different aspect of integrity: a linkage may have the right travel but still have excessive friction, for example, or be free-moving yet insufficiently secured.

## 5\. Cable Tension

Control cables must be adjusted to the tension specified for the system.

* **Insufficient tension** makes a cable slack and permits excessive movement of the cable run.
* **Excessive tension** places too much resistance into the mechanism, making the cockpit controls stiff.

**Turnbuckles** are used to adjust cable tension, while a **tensiometer** is used to measure it.

### 5.1 Turnbuckle Construction

A typical barrel-type turnbuckle consists of a **central barrel** and a threaded **end fitting at each end**. The cable ends attach to the fittings, and adjustment changes the effective length and tension of the cable assembly.

## 6\. Temperature Compensation

**Ambient temperature changes the tension in a control-cable run** because the cable and supporting airframe do not necessarily expand by the same amount.

In the example used by the textbook:

* The aircraft structure is made primarily of **aluminium alloy**.
* The control cables are made of **steel**.
* As temperature increases, aluminium alloy expands **more than the steel cables**.
* This differential expansion **increases the cable tension**.

Accordingly, the correct target reading for a tension check is the one appropriate to the **ambient temperature**, not simply an unadjusted nominal value.

Some aircraft use a **temperature compensator** in the control linkage to maintain the correct tension automatically as temperature varies.

## 7\. Safety and Locking of Turnbuckles

After setting a cable's tension, the turnbuckle needs **two separate checks**:

1. Verify that the fittings have **enough threaded engagement** to carry the working load (the turnbuckle is *in safety*).
2. **Lock the assembly** so vibration cannot rotate the barrel relative to the fittings and loosen the cable during service.

### 7.1 Checking Thread Engagement

Where an **inspection hole** is present, the end-fitting thread must extend far enough inside the barrel to **completely block** the hole. This is checked using a **hardened pin**: the pin should not pass through an inspection hole that is correctly blocked by thread.

For a turnbuckle **without** inspection holes, the source states that **no more than three threads** on the end fitting should be visible outside the barrel.

### 7.2 Preventing Adjustment From Changing in Service

Once the assembly is correctly tensioned and verified as safe, it must be secured against relative rotation. Otherwise vibration can gradually unwind the barrel and reduce cable tension.

The most common locking technique identified is **locking wire**, although approved **locking clips, locking plates** and other securing methods are also used.

## 8\. Range of Control Movement

Each control surface has an approved amount of movement to either side of its **neutral position**. This ensures adequate control throughout the aircraft's permitted operating conditions.

The permitted travel need **not** be symmetrical. For instance, the textbook notes that the **elevator commonly deflects farther upward than downward**.

A mechanical stop defines the travel limits:

* **Primary stop:** Prevents further movement **at the control surface**.
* **Secondary stop:** Limits movement at the **control column or rudder pedals**.

When the primary stop has reached its limiting position, there should still be a **small clearance at the secondary stop**. Thus the intended surface-side limit, rather than the cockpit-side limit, determines maximum deflection.

## 9\. Control System Friction

**Control-system friction** is the resistance the pilot must overcome to move the controls when the aircraft is stationary.

During flight, the air loads acting on the surfaces add to the forces felt at the controls. If friction within the linkage is unusually high, the expected change in stick force with airspeed can be masked or distorted.

### 9.1 Checking Friction

Attach a **spring balance** to the cockpit control and measure the force while moving it through its **entire travel**.

The source identifies two possible causes of excessive friction:

* **Control cables adjusted too tightly**.
* **Bearings without sufficient lubrication**.

\---

## 10\. Backlash

**Backlash** is the initial free, unproductive movement of a cockpit control when its direction is reversed, before the control linkage begins to respond effectively.

Flight-control systems should be free from backlash. Its presence can indicate **worn components** or **incorrectly fitted components** in the system.

It is distinct from friction: friction resists motion, whereas backlash is movement that initially produces **little or no useful response**.

\---

## 11\. Control Locks

When parked outdoors, an aircraft may encounter strong and gusting winds. Wind-driven control-surface movement can strike the mechanical stops with enough force to cause damage.

**Control locks** restrain the flying controls to protect the linkage and surfaces while parked. They may be:

* **External locks**, attached at a control surface; or
* **Internal locks**, fitted to or acting on cockpit controls.

Some cockpit-installed locking arrangements are interlocked so that **the throttle cannot be opened until the lock has been removed**.

### 11.1 A Locked Surface Does Not Always Mean an Immovable Cockpit Control

There are important exceptions:

* With **servo-operated controls**, the cockpit control may still move even when **external surface locks** are fitted.
* With **spring-tab-assisted controls**, some cockpit movement can also remain possible with the external locks installed, although the control will feel **very stiff**.

Therefore, freedom of cockpit movement alone does not establish that an externally restrained control surface is unlocked.

## 12\. Duplicate Inspection of Controls

A malfunction caused by incorrect maintenance or assembly of a flight-control system can have serious consequences. For that reason, the chapter describes **duplicate inspection**, in which the disturbed system is checked separately by **two qualified people** before further flight.

* The requirement applies when the control system has been **disturbed or adjusted**.
* In some permitted situations, the **second inspector may be a pilot**.
* The detailed scope, required sequence and certification procedure are covered in **Appendix A** later in these notes.

## 13\. Take-off Configuration Warning

A take-off configuration warning system is **armed on the ground** as the **forward thrust levers are advanced for take-off**.

If a relevant item has not been placed in a suitable take-off configuration, an **intermittent warning** can sound. The source identifies the following potential causes:

1. **Stabilizer trim** outside the safe range.
2. **Trailing-edge flaps** not in the take-off position.
3. **Leading-edge high-lift devices** not in their required take-off position.
4. **Speed-brake lever** not fully **DOWN**.
5. One or more **doors not completely locked**.
6. **Flight-control locks** not fully released on aircraft equipped with internal locks.

The intermittent indication stops when the incorrect configuration is corrected.

### 13.1 Landing-Gear Warning Is Different

The text also describes a **steady warning horn** for the landing configuration when **any landing gear is not down and locked**. The gear-warning logic is additionally affected by **flap position, aircraft speed and thrust-lever position**.

**Distinguish the warnings:** The take-off configuration alert described here is **intermittent**, whereas the landing-gear warning in the stated condition is **steady**.

## 14\. High Lift Devices

**High-lift devices** raise the wing's lift coefficient, allowing the aeroplane to produce substantial lift at the **lower speeds** used for take-off and landing. The related effect is a **reduction in stall speed**.

* On **most jet transport aircraft**, high-lift devices are installed on **both the leading and trailing edges**.
* On **many smaller aircraft**, the high-lift arrangement uses **trailing-edge flaps alone**.

The following sections cover the forms and operating arrangements described in this chapter.

Flaps mounted at the rear of the wing can increase **both lift and drag**, but the changes are different for each flap design.

* A **plain flap**, also called a **camber flap**, is particularly common on light aircraft.

### 15.2 Typical Power-Driven Trailing-Edge Flap System

A typical powered transport-aircraft flap system operates as follows:

1. Moving the **cockpit flap selector** sends the request to **two slat/flap computers**.
2. These computers **control, monitor and test** the system's operation.
3. An **electrically controlled hydromechanical power unit** drives the transmission.
4. The transmission moves the flap surfaces through the associated drive train.
5. **Flap position** is shown on the cockpit display.

The arrangement includes safeguards against:

* **Asymmetric** flap movement.
* **Runaway** movement.
* **Uncommanded** movement.
* **Overspeed** conditions.

**Torque-limiting brakes** stop the transmission if excessive torque is detected.

### 15.3 Flap Load Relief System (LRS)

The **load-relief system**, also termed a **load limiter**, reduces flap loading when the aircraft exceeds a predetermined airspeed:

* The flaps are moved automatically toward an **intermediate position** if the relevant speed threshold is exceeded.
* When airspeed later falls beneath the specified limit, the flaps **return automatically to the cockpit-selected position**.

### 15.4 Alternate Flap Operation

After failure of the normal flap-control system, some installations can operate the trailing-edge flaps using:

* An **alternative hydraulic power supply**, or
* An **electric motor** acting on the trailing-edge drive unit or gearbox.

The alternate arrangement then moves the flaps by driving the **same mechanical gear train**.

## 16\. Leading Edge Devices

Leading-edge high-lift equipment may be **slats, Krueger flaps, variable-camber flaps**, or combinations of these.

The source gives the **Boeing 747** as an example of a mixed arrangement, with **Krueger flaps inboard** and **variable-camber flaps outboard**.

### 16.1 Actuation and Control

Leading-edge surfaces may be moved using:

* **Hydraulic power**, or
* **Air-turbine motors**.

Their operation is linked to the **cockpit flap lever**.

Certain installations use a hydraulic motor to turn **screw jacks**, which move the surfaces. These designs require **mechanical locks** to prevent the surfaces from slowly moving or **creeping** after hydraulic power is removed.

### 16.2 Leading-Edge Flaps Compared With Slats

* **Leading-edge flaps** are hinged sections that deploy by rotating **downward from the underside** of the wing's front edge.
* **Slats** consist of forward portions of the wing leading edge which project **ahead of the wing**, creating either a **sealed** or **slotted** leading-edge arrangement, depending on the selected trailing-edge-flap configuration.

### 16.3 Normal Extension Sequence

The leading- and trailing-edge devices operate in a coordinated sequence; the exact sequence can depend on aircraft type.

|Trailing-edge flap position|Typical leading-edge response described|
|-|-|
|**Retracted**|Leading-edge flaps and slats **retracted**.|
|**Intermediate**|Leading-edge flaps **fully deployed** and slats approximately **halfway out** where applicable.|
|**Fully lowered**|Slats move to the **fully extended** position.|
|**Retracting trailing-edge flaps**|The leading-edge sequence is **reversed**.|

### 16.4 Alternate/Standby Actuation

The chapter describes standby operation by:

* A **standby hydraulic supply** for hydraulic installations; or
* An **electrical standby system** for installations normally driven by air-turbine motors.

The alternate arrangement drives the leading-edge devices to **full extension**. Depending on the aircraft, it **may or may not be possible to retract them** with the alternate system.

### 16.5 Autoslat Function

Some aircraft include an **autoslat system**. If the aircraft approaches the **stall angle of attack** while the slats are only at their intermediate position, the system automatically moves them to **full extension**.

### 16.6 Leading- and Trailing-Edge Position Indications

The textbook illustrates both an **electronic display** and a more traditional **analogue display** for showing flap and slat/leading-edge-flap positions.

## 17\. Speed Brakes

A speed-brake system can use **flight spoilers** and **ground spoilers**, with different operating permissions and functions.

### 17.1 Basic System Arrangement

The **speed-brake lever** operates a **spoiler mixer**. The mixer distributes the command to:

* **Flight-spoiler Power Control Units (PCUs)** or their actuators; and
* The **ground-spoiler control valve**.

Hydraulic power moves the surfaces through the PCUs or individual hydraulic actuators.

### 17.2 Flight Spoilers Compared With Ground Spoilers

* **Flight spoilers:** With the lateral controls centred, selecting speed brakes raises the flight spoilers **symmetrically**.
* **Ground spoilers:** These can additionally rise when the speed brakes are applied **on the ground**.

The ground-spoiler hydraulic circuit contains a **shut-off valve downstream of the control valve**. It stays closed until the main landing gear operates the **weight-on switch**, thereby preventing normal ground-spoiler deployment in flight.

The cockpit lever can transmit its demand **mechanically** to the mixer, which routes the appropriate signals toward both types of spoiler.

### 17.3 Automatic Speed-Brake Lever Actuation

Some systems use an **electrical speed-brake lever actuator**. When the lever is **armed**, appropriate touchdown wheel-rotation signals drive it toward **FULL UP**, extending both flight and ground spoilers.

If the engine thrust levers are advanced again during the landing run, the system treats this as an **aborted-landing/go-around demand** and lowers the raised spoiler surfaces.

## 18\. Typical Flight Spoiler System

The example system in the chapter uses **two flight-spoiler panels on each upper wing surface**:

* The **outboard spoiler** on each wing is supplied by **one hydraulic system**.
* The **inboard spoiler** is supplied by a **second hydraulic system**.
* Two **flight-spoiler switches** operate hydraulic-pressure shut-off valves for the relevant spoiler supply arrangements.

This makes it possible to control spoiler hydraulic supply using separate switching paths.

### 18.1 Spoilers as Roll-Control Surfaces

The flight spoilers are moved hydraulically in response to **aileron-control movement**:

1. An aileron input passes into the **spoiler mixer**.
2. The mixer regulates the hydraulic **PCUs** attached to the spoiler panels.
3. The amount of spoiler movement corresponds to the aileron input.
4. On the wing with the **up-going aileron**, the associated flight spoilers **rise**.
5. On the wing with the **down-going aileron**, the relevant flight spoilers **stay down/retracted** for the roll-control input.

### 18.2 Speed-Brake Selector Positions

## 19\. Spoiler Operation: Detailed Control Sequence

The chapter describes how a flight-spoiler system uses the aileron linkage, mechanical feedback and hydraulic actuation to provide roll assistance.

### 19.1 From Pilot Input to Spoiler Movement

1. **Control wheel input:** Wheel rotation sends a roll-demand signal through the **captain's aileron control cables** to the **aileron Power Flying Control Units**.
2. **Aileron movement:** The aileron power units move the ailerons.
3. **Spoiler demand:** As the aileron units move, they also deliver a roll-control input to the **spoiler mixer**.
4. **Valve action:** Rotation of the mixer's output mechanism operates the **spoiler control valves**.
5. **Roll assistance:** The spoilers rise on the **down-going wing**, where the aileron is moving **up**.

### 19.2 Follow-up and Intermediate Positioning

The spoiler actuators supply a **mechanical follow-up** signal as the surfaces move. When the desired deflection has been reached, this follow-up cancels the control-valve input.

As a result, a flight spoiler can remain at a controlled position **anywhere between fully retracted and fully raised**, rather than functioning only as an on/off surface.

### 19.3 Hydraulic Isolation

The hydraulic power supply to the flight spoilers is controlled by **electrically driven valves** in the flight-control hydraulic-power system. Two spoiler switches independently command these valves, allowing the spoiler power supply to be **isolated when necessary**.

### 19.4 Roll Control While Speed Brakes Are Extended

Flight spoilers do not lose their roll-control function merely because speed braking is selected. The system **adds together** the demands for:

* **Speed braking**, and
* **Roll control**.

For example, with the speed brakes already raised and a **left-roll command** applied:

* The spoiler(s) on the **down-going wing** rise **farther**, while
* The spoiler(s) on the **up-going wing** lower relative to the symmetric speed-brake setting.

The resulting command is a **combined response** that accounts for **drag, adverse yaw, roll and airspeed-control requirements**, rather than treating the spoilers as independent devices for each task.

## 20\. Automatic Ground Speed Brake Control Operation

An automatic ground-spoiler/lift-dumping system uses several signals to decide whether deployment should occur after landing. The textbook presents the following inputs and protections.

### 20.1 Armed Lever Position

Selecting **ARMED** on the speed-brake lever prepares the **flight and ground spoilers** for automatic lift dumping.

### 20.2 Anti-skid Wheel Spin-up

The anti-skid system provides electrical information to **wheel-speed relays**. A combination of touchdown **wheel spin-up** signals, passed through **two parallel circuits**, energizes the speed-brake actuator. The actuator drives the speed-brake lever **UP**, deploying the spoilers.

### 20.3 Air/Ground Sensing as an Alternative

If **both anti-skid channels are inoperative** at touchdown, the system can use **air/ground sensing** instead. Compression of the landing-gear strut supplies the signal used to command the spoiler system.

### 20.4 Thrust-Lever Position

Moving the thrust levers back at touchdown provides a further deployment-related input, which can raise the spoiler system through the speed-brake lever.

### 20.5 Thrust Reverser Operation

The **thrust reverser linkage** can mechanically raise the speed-brake lever. It also energizes a **relay** that supplies power to the speed-brake system, allowing the spoilers to rise.

### 20.6 Overspeed Protection

An automatic protection feature is provided to stop deployment when **indicated airspeed (IAS)** is excessively high.

### 20.7 Go-around/Aborted Landing Logic

The example system includes a **go-around capability**. A suitable **wheel spin-up signal after slowing down**, or **opening the thrust levers**, can command the spoilers to **retract**.

> \*\*Exam distinction:\*\* Wheel spin-up can participate in automatic spoiler deployment at touchdown, while the source also identifies a particular post-slowdown spin-up condition within the go-around/retraction logic. Keep the two stated conditions separate rather than assuming every wheel-speed change has the same effect.

# 21\. Appendix A — Civil Aviation Authority: Control Systems

This appendix presents the **historical CAA control-system inspection guidance reproduced in the textbook**. It should be studied as the chapter's stated reference material; specific regulatory references in a 2014 edition should not be assumed to be current without checking up-to-date regulations.

## 21.1 Purpose and Scope of the Guidance (Appendix Paragraph 1)

The guidance concerns inspection of control systems that are:

* **Manually operated**.
* **Power assisted**.
* **Fully power operated**.

It is intended to be used **alongside the approved aircraft drawings and maintenance manuals**, rather than as a replacement for the aircraft-specific documents.

## 21.2 Meaning and Scope of a Control System (Paragraph 2)

For the appendix, a **control system** includes the means by which the aircraft's **flight attitude or propulsive force** is changed.

### 21.2.1 What Counts as the Flight-Control System for Duplicate Inspection (Paragraph 2.1)

The definition covers more than just the three primary movable surfaces. It also includes:

* Main flying-control surfaces.
* **Lift and drag devices**.
* **Trim and feel systems**.
* **Control-lock systems**.
* Associated operating controls and mechanisms.

For **rotorcraft**, the definition also includes the pilot-operated mechanisms controlling **collective pitch, cyclic pitch and yaw**.

The **engine-control system** includes the primary engine controls and associated systems, with the appendix giving examples of:

* Throttle controls.
* Fuel-cock controls.
* Oil-cooler controls.
* Crew-operated mechanisms associated with those controls.

## 21.3 Definition of Duplicate Inspection (Paragraph 2.2)

A **duplicate inspection** of a control system or vital point has **two separately certified stages**:

1. A first inspection carried out and certified by **one qualified person**.
2. A subsequent inspection carried out and certified by a **second qualified person**.

### 21.3.1 Vital Point

A **vital point** is an aircraft location where a **single incorrect assembly** could lead to a catastrophe, potentially including **loss of the aircraft and/or fatalities**. The appendix cites **BCAR Section A, Chapter A5-3** for this term.

### 21.3.2 No Disturbance Between Inspections (Paragraph 2.2.1)

The part or system undergoing duplicate inspection **must not be adjusted or disturbed** after the first check and before the second. The second stage should follow the first **as closely in time as reasonably possible**.

### 21.3.3 Exception for Access and Assembly (Paragraph 2.2.2)

If the arrangement of the components or access limitations makes successive inspections impracticable, the appendix permits the two parts of the inspection to be carried out **simultaneously** in some cases.

### 22.1 Components Concealed During Bench Assembly (Paragraph 3.1)

Certain internal portions of a component may become hidden during **bench assembly**, before installation into the aeroplane. Such portions must be inspected **in duplicate as they are assembled** when the component is being:

* Manufactured.

The **Inspection Record** for the component must contain certification of:

* **Both stages** of duplicate inspection; and
* The results of relevant tests made **during and after final assembly**.

## 23\. Duplicate Inspection of Complete Control Systems (Appendix Paragraph 4)

### 23.1 When Duplicate Inspection Is Required (Paragraph 4.1)

A control system installed in an aircraft must undergo duplicate inspection:

1. **Before the aircraft's first flight following initial assembly**.
2. **Before its first flight after overhaul, replacement, repair, adjustment or modification** of that system.

The two inspection stages are meant to be the **final operations** performed on the completed control system. The work must therefore be finished before the inspection begins, so the inspection can establish system integrity.

If the control system is **disturbed after the duplicate inspection but before flight**, the affected part must **undergo duplicate inspection again** before the aircraft is flown.

### 23.2 Work That Will Be Boxed In or Sealed (Paragraph 4.2)

Some portions of a control system become inaccessible as aircraft assembly progresses because they are **boxed in or sealed**.

Before sealing such a section, its **condition and security** must be checked to the satisfaction of the authorized personnel referenced in Appendix Paragraph 5. The appropriate **Inspection Record** must be endorsed at that stage.

### 23.3 Planning the Inspection Records (Paragraph 4.3)

The records should be organised so that all duplicate inspections required **during early assembly** are clearly flagged. This prevents the unnecessary dismantling that might otherwise be needed to inspect a concealed mechanism later.

### 23.4 Standard of the Inspection (Paragraph 4.4)

Correct flight-control operation is a continuous **airworthiness** requirement. Licensed aircraft engineers and personnel from approved inspection organisations must understand the particular systems they inspect.

The inspection needs to be systematic and verify that every relevant part is:

* **Correctly assembled**.
* **Free to move** throughout its prescribed range.
* Clear of obstructions or **fouling**.
* **Adequately and correctly locked**.
* **Clean**.
* **Properly lubricated**.
* Moving in the **correct sense** in response to the crew's cockpit input.

# 25\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions

These questions are newly worded for revision. They follow the concepts, examples and distinctions in the source chapter.

### Control-system fundamentals

**1. Which three broad methods can transmit and implement a pilot's control-surface command?**  
**Answer:** Mechanical, hydraulic and electrical control arrangements.

**2. Does electrical signalling necessarily mean the surface is moved by an electric motor?**  
**Answer:** No. The command may be electrical while the surface is moved by hydraulic power.

**3. What does pulling the control column backward do in the light-aircraft example?**  
**Answer:** Raises the elevator and produces a nose-up pitch response.

**4. How do the ailerons move for a right-roll cockpit input?**  
**Answer:** The right aileron moves upward and the left aileron downward.

**5. What is the rudder response to the right pedal being pushed forward?**  
**Answer:** The rudder moves right, causing the aircraft to yaw right.

**6. Name the primary control surfaces listed by the chapter.**  
**Answer:** Elevator, rudder, ailerons and roll-control spoilers.

**7. What makes a manually operated primary control reversible?**  
**Answer:** Forces and movement can pass in either direction between the cockpit control and the control surface.

**8. Why must fully powered irreversible systems incorporate artificial feel?**  
**Answer:** Surface loading cannot naturally back-drive the cockpit control, so another mechanism must create an appropriate control-force sensation.

**9. How should artificial cockpit-control force vary?**  
**Answer:** It should increase with control deflection and with aircraft speed, as described in the source.

**10. Are power-assisted controls necessarily irreversible?**  
**Answer:** No. They can retain natural feel and be reversible when loads are sufficiently high.

### Mechanical checks and safety

**11. What happens if cable tension is too low?**  
**Answer:** The cable becomes loose and allows excessive movement.

**12. What happens if cable tension is too high?**  
**Answer:** The controls become excessively stiff.

**13. Which device adjusts cable tension, and which one measures it?**  
**Answer:** A turnbuckle adjusts it; a tensiometer measures it.

**14. Why must control-cable tension be checked with ambient temperature in mind?**  
**Answer:** Steel cable and aluminium-alloy structure expand by different amounts, changing tension.

**15. What is the expected effect of rising temperature on steel cable tension in a normal aluminium-alloy airframe?**  
**Answer:** Cable tension increases because the aluminium structure expands more than the steel cable.

**16. How is adequate thread engagement checked on a turnbuckle with an inspection hole?**  
**Answer:** The fitted thread must block the hole completely, preventing a hardened checking pin from passing through it.

**17. Without an inspection hole, how many threads may remain visible beyond the turnbuckle barrel according to the textbook?**  
**Answer:** No more than **three threads**.

**18. Why must a properly adjusted turnbuckle also be locked?**  
**Answer:** To stop vibration rotating the barrel and reducing the set tension.

**19. Distinguish primary and secondary control stops.**  
**Answer:** Primary stops limit movement at the surface; secondary stops limit movement at the cockpit control or pedals.

**20. What should be present at the secondary stop when the primary stop has reached its limit?**  
**Answer:** A small clearance.

**21. How can friction be checked in a control system?**  
**Answer:** Measure the force with a spring balance as the control is moved through its full travel.

**22. What is backlash?**  
**Answer:** Ineffective free movement on reversing a cockpit control before useful movement resumes in the linkage.

**23. Why are control locks fitted while an aircraft is parked in windy conditions?**  
**Answer:** To prevent wind from driving the surfaces against their stops and causing damage.

**24. Why is cockpit-control movement alone not proof that an external surface lock has been removed?**  
**Answer:** Some servo- and spring-tab-assisted systems still permit cockpit movement with an external lock installed.

### High-lift and spoiler systems

**25. What are high-lift devices intended to achieve at take-off and landing speeds?**  
**Answer:** Raise the maximum usable lift coefficient and reduce stall speed so adequate lift can be produced at lower airspeeds.

**26. Which trailing-edge flap types are highlighted as common on light aircraft and large transports?**  
**Answer:** Plain/camber flaps on light aircraft, and slotted Fowler flaps on large transport aircraft.

**27. According to the comparative figure, which designs show an illustrated 90% increase in maximum lift?**  
**Answer:** Zap flaps and Fowler flaps.

**28. What two major duties are assigned to the slat/flap computers?**  
**Answer:** The two computers control and monitor the flap system, and also perform its tests.

**29. Name four flap movement hazards for which the powered system provides protection.**  
**Answer:** Asymmetry, runaway, uncommanded movement and overspeed.

**30. What does a flap load-relief system do?**  
**Answer:** It retracts the flaps to an intermediate position at excessive speed and returns them to the selected position after speed falls below the limit.

**31. What distinguishes leading-edge flaps from slats?**  
**Answer:** Leading-edge flaps rotate downward from the lower leading edge, while slats move forward and can produce a sealed or slotted leading edge.

**32. What happens to the slats when trailing-edge flaps are moved from intermediate to fully lowered?**  
**Answer:** The slats extend from an intermediate setting to fully extended.

**33. When might an autoslat system extend the slats fully?**  
**Answer:** On approach to stall angle of attack when the slats are not already fully extended.

**34. What stops ground spoilers being commanded normally in flight?**  
**Answer:** The weight-on-operated hydraulic ground-spoiler shut-off valve remains closed while the aircraft is airborne.

**35. What happens to roll spoilers on the wing with the up-going aileron?**  
**Answer:** They extend/raise to assist the aileron roll command.

**36. What is the purpose of spoiler-actuator follow-up?**  
**Answer:** It cancels valve demand when the desired deflection has been reached, allowing intermediate spoiler positions.

**37. Can flight spoilers assist with roll control while speed brakes are already deployed?**  
**Answer:** Yes. Roll and speed-brake demands are combined in the spoiler mixer.

**38. What is the alternate sensing method if both anti-skid channels fail during touchdown?**  
**Answer:** Air/ground sensing via landing-gear strut compression.

**39. What is the purpose of excessive-IAS protection in an automatic speed-brake system?**  
**Answer:** It prevents spoiler deployment at an excessive indicated airspeed.

### Inspection appendix

**40. What is a duplicate inspection?**  
**Answer:** A check and certification by one qualified person followed by a separate check and certification by a second qualified person.

**41. What qualifies as a vital point?**  
**Answer:** A location where one incorrect assembly could lead to catastrophe, including loss of the aircraft or fatalities.

**42. May the inspected control system be changed between the two inspection stages?**  
**Answer:** No. It must not be disturbed or readjusted between them.

**43. What must happen if a control system is altered after its duplicate inspection but before first flight?**  
**Answer:** The affected part must be inspected in duplicate again before flight.

**44. When should portions of a control system that will later be sealed be inspected?**  
**Answer:** Before they are boxed in or sealed, with the result recorded appropriately.

**45. In the appendix's away-from-base minor-adjustment exception, who may carry out the second check?**  
**Answer:** A pilot or flight engineer licensed for the aircraft type, where the stated exception applies.
