# Chapter 13 — Aircraft Oxygen Equipment

## 1\. Why Aircraft Need Supplemental Oxygen

### 1.1 Oxygen and Flight Safety

The body's organs depend on oxygen delivered through the bloodstream. The lungs transfer oxygen from inhaled air into the blood, allowing normal physical and mental functions to continue. If that supply becomes inadequate, a person develops **hypoxia**.

For pilots, the main threat is not limited to eventual unconsciousness. Hypoxia may first interfere with the ability to recognize danger, assess information, remember instructions, and operate aircraft equipment. A crew member can consequently lose the ability to correct the situation while still appearing awake.

The textbook therefore treats early recognition and well-practised oxygen procedures as essential aircrew knowledge. Before operating above approximately **10,000 ft**, a crew member should know the oxygen equipment and the procedures applicable to that aircraft.

### 1.2 Typical Effects of Hypoxia

Symptoms can progress through several forms of impairment:

* **Personality or behaviour:** an unexpected change in attitude, behaviour, or temperament.
* **Judgement:** reduced capacity to make sound decisions.
* **Muscular function:** difficulty coordinating physical movement.
* **Memory:** poorer recall and difficulty remembering actions or information.
* **Sensory ability:** a reduction in the reliability of the senses.
* **Consciousness:** confusion may develop into partial consciousness, unconsciousness, and ultimately death.

The response is based on two priorities: **establish an adequate oxygen supply** and **descend to an altitude at which atmospheric oxygen can meet the body's requirements**. The practical execution must follow the aircraft's approved procedures. For more detailed physiology, the source textbook refers students to its Human Performance and Limitations volume.

### 1.3 Time of Useful Consciousness (TUC)

**Time of Useful Consciousness** is the period after oxygen becomes inadequate during which a person can still recognize the emergency and perform effective corrective actions. It is **not** the total time until unconsciousness. The useful period ends earlier, when mental or physical impairment prevents effective self-help.

|Altitude|Person seated or resting|Person doing moderate activity|
|-|-|-|
|20,000 ft|Approximately 30 minutes|Approximately 5 minutes|
|30,000 ft|Approximately 1–2 minutes|Not specified in the source table|
|35,000 ft|Approximately 30–90 seconds|Not specified in the source table|
|40,000 ft|Approximately 15–20 seconds|Not specified in the source table|

**Interpretation:** A person who is active can have substantially less time than one who is resting. At very high altitudes, the usable interval may be measured in seconds, reinforcing the importance of immediate recognition and rapid access to oxygen equipment.

## 2\. When Oxygen Equipment Is Required

### 2.1 Oxygen in Pressurized Aircraft

The chapter describes supplemental oxygen provisions for loss of cabin pressure and for contamination of flight-deck air. Its principal altitude-based examples are:

* Flight crew oxygen when **cabin pressure altitude is above 13,000 ft**.
* Flight crew oxygen if the aircraft spends **more than 30 minutes between 10,000 and 13,000 ft cabin pressure altitude**.
* Crew oxygen when hazardous fumes or smoke make the flight-deck atmosphere unsuitable for ordinary breathing.
* Passenger oxygen for **all passengers above 15,000 ft cabin pressure altitude**, **30% above 14,000 ft** in the specified band, and **10% above 10,000 ft** under the duration conditions discussed later.

The final percentages and time exceptions must be understood together with the detailed EU-OPS tables in Section 12. They are not universal, unconditional thresholds.

### 2.2 Unpressurized Aircraft

Where unpressurized aircraft operate above **10,000 ft**, the chapter describes the provision of passenger and crew oxygen. A portable supply may be used when no permanently installed equipment is provided, subject to the operating requirements referenced in the book.

### 2.3 Purposes of Installed and Portable Systems

Aircraft may carry several types of oxygen equipment because not everyone needs oxygen for the same reason:

|Equipment|Main users|Purpose|
|-|-|-|
|Crew gaseous oxygen system|Pilots and other flight-deck occupants|Support breathing during cabin-altitude increase or contamination; commonly demand-operated|
|Passenger oxygen installation|Passengers and cabin attendants|Provide emergency oxygen after cabin depressurization; ordinarily continuous-flow|
|Portable first-aid/therapeutic bottle|Persons needing supplemental oxygen|Supply oxygen at selected flow rates in the cabin|
|Portable crew/smoke protection apparatus|Crew moving or working in adverse conditions|Provide breathing protection in a hazardous atmosphere|

The chapter also distinguishes **high-pressure stored oxygen** from oxygen created by a **chemical generator**. High-pressure gas is the normal crew supply; passenger installations may use either method. Some light aircraft use a continuous-flow arrangement for their crew as well.

### 2.4 Storage and General System Protection

For the gaseous installations described in this chapter:

* Oxygen bottles are typically charged to **1,800 psi**.
* The pressure delivered to a mask is reduced considerably from storage pressure.
* A cockpit gauge indicates the supply quantity by reading **cylinder pressure**.
* An overpressure **bursting disc** provides a route to discharge oxygen safely outside the aircraft.
* An external **discharge indicator**, positioned near the oxygen storage area, shows when the protective disc has operated.
* **Bottle shut-off valves** allow a cylinder to be isolated and removed during maintenance.

## 3\. Continuous-Flow Oxygen System

### 3.1 Basic Principle

A continuous-flow arrangement supplies oxygen through its distribution line at an ongoing rate when the appropriate valves are open. Unlike a demand-only regulator, it does not depend on every individual inhalation to command an oxygen pulse.

Its basic path is:

1. Oxygen leaves the charged cylinder through its shut-off valve.
2. It passes through the line valve and associated supply equipment.
3. A **pressure-reducing valve (PRV)** brings the high cylinder pressure down to approximately **80–100 psi**.
4. Oxygen reaches the individual mask outlets.
5. At the outlet, a **calibrated restriction/orifice** produces the additional pressure reduction appropriate for continuous breathing supply.

Passenger masks may connect through permanent plug-in outlets or through automatic drop-down units. In a drop-down installation the equipment becomes available following an appropriate cabin-pressure event; the passenger pulls the mask into use to establish flow.

### 3.3 Regulators, Gauges and Flow Indication

A continuous-flow regulator can be either **manually adjustable** or **automatically controlled**. The source associates hand adjustment with some crew arrangements and automatic control with passenger installations.

A manually controlled installation typically includes:

* A **pressure gauge** displaying cylinder pressure in psi.
* A **flow indicator**, marked in relation to cabin altitude.
* A **control knob** used to set the appropriate oxygen flow for the cabin altitude.

An important distinction is that **seeing movement or indication in a flow indicator confirms only that oxygen is passing through the regulator**. It does **not**, by itself, prove that the flow quantity is correct or that enough oxygen reaches the user.

## 4\. Diluter-Demand Crew Oxygen Systems

### 4.1 How Demand Operation Differs From Continuous Flow

On many transport aircraft, crew oxygen uses a separate **diluter-demand** system. Oxygen is supplied when the user inhales, and the regulator mixes it with cabin air in a proportion selected for the prevailing cabin altitude.

Each flight-deck crew position, including an additional/supernumerary position where provided, has a mask connection. The system is **separate from and supplementary to** the passenger oxygen installation.

As altitude increases, cabin air becomes less suitable as a diluent, so the regulator increases the oxygen share. The textbook states that **100% oxygen is being delivered by 32,000 ft cabin altitude** in the normal automatic-mixing mode.

### 4.2 Main Regulator Selections

|Selection|Effect described in the textbook|
|-|-|
|Supply **ON**, mode **NORMAL**|Oxygen is delivered on inhalation and mixed with cabin air; oxygen concentration rises with cabin altitude.|
|**100% O₂**|Stops normal air dilution and supplies undiluted oxygen, irrespective of cabin altitude.|
|**EMERGENCY**|Provides 100% oxygen with positive-pressure delivery to resist entry of smoke and harmful gases; the particular illustrated regulator uses a steady-flow emergency setting.|
|**TEST**|Produces oxygen at elevated positive pressure so mask fit and leakage can be assessed.|

### 4.5 Narrow-Panel Regulator — Normal Operating Cycle

For a typical narrow-panel unit, normal use is established with the **oxygen supply ON**, the oxygen selection at **NORMAL**, and emergency selection **OFF**.

The breathing cycle then operates as follows:

1. Inhalation creates a pressure difference across the **demand diaphragm**.
2. This difference opens the **demand valve** so oxygen enters the breathing line.
3. Air enters the mixing route through the **air inlet valve** at the same time.
4. An **aneroid-controlled air-metering valve** adjusts how much air can mix with oxygen.
5. At relatively low altitude more cabin air is admitted; with increasing altitude the regulator supplies a larger oxygen fraction.

Selecting **100%** shuts off the inlet from the flight deck, preventing its contaminated air from being added to the mask supply. Moving **EMERGENCY** to ON places a mechanical load on the demand diaphragm to produce **positive-pressure oxygen**.

## 5\. EROS — Emergency Regulating Oxygen System

### 5.1 Crew Mask and Regulator Unit

**EROS** equipment combines a breathing mask and oxygen regulator at each flight-crew station. Its purpose is to provide either oxygen mixed with cabin air or undiluted oxygen, without requiring the pilot to assemble separate components during an emergency.

The mask is stored inside a panel box. Certain regulator controls and the oxygen hose remain accessible through openings in the box doors. With the mask stowed and doors shut, a valve inside the box prevents unwanted oxygen flow. The left door's **RESET–TEST lever** holds that valve closed, and the **flow indicator** remains visible whether the box is open or closed.

The mask includes a **pneumatic head harness**. When stored, it is deflated. Once released, it can expand for fast fitting and subsequently contract around different head sizes.

The source cites the JAR-OPS requirement for **quick-donning masks at flight-deck crew stations on aircraft with maximum operating altitudes above 25,000 ft**.

### 5.2 EROS Controls

* **N / 100% PUSH:** The regulator normally permits dilution; pushing the portion marked **100% PUSH** selects undiluted oxygen.
* **EMERGENCY:** Rotating this knob to the emergency position changes the regulator's operation from diluter demand to the described steady oxygen delivery.
* **PRESS TO TEST:** The emergency knob also serves a test function when operated together with the **RESET–TEST** lever.

### 5.3 Quick Donning Sequence

1. Hold the two **red release grips** between thumb and forefinger.
2. Withdrawing the mask in this way inflates the harness so it can pass over the head rapidly.
3. After positioning the mask, release the grips; the harness depressurizes and closes around the head to secure the seal.

The mask provides **radio-telephony (R/T) communication**. Certain installations can also feed ventilation air to smoke goggles, reducing misting during use.

### 5.4 EROS Test

The illustrated check uses the knob marked **PRESS TO TEST** together with the **RESET–TEST** lever. The pilot then verifies that oxygen flow is indicated. This is a description of the textbook's equipment; actual checks must be performed according to the applicable aircraft procedure.

## 6\. Passenger Oxygen Distribution

### 6.1 How Oxygen Masks Are Presented

Passenger oxygen is an **emergency continuous-flow supply**, obtained either from a central store of compressed oxygen or from chemical oxygen generators.

Masks are normally contained in overhead **passenger service units (PSUs)**. Their doors can be released:

* **Automatically** by a cabin-altitude-sensitive arrangement at approximately **14,000 ft cabin altitude**.
* **Manually from the flight deck** at lower cabin altitudes when the crew selects release.

The source describes an **electrical actuator** for chemical-generator installations and a **pneumatic actuator** for gaseous installations.

When a PSU opens, its masks hang partly down in the **half-hung** position. This makes the masks visible and accessible; it does not necessarily mean oxygen is already flowing. **Pulling a mask toward the face** initiates supply:

* In a gaseous arrangement, by opening an associated **check valve**.
* In a chemical arrangement, by actuating an **electrical or percussion-cap firing mechanism**.

## 7\. Chemical Oxygen Generators

### 7.1 How They Produce Oxygen

Chemical oxygen generators are relatively light, self-contained units commonly located with passenger masks and at cabin-crew and lavatory service positions.

The generator contains a formed chemical core made from **sodium chlorate (NaClO₃)** and **iron (Fe)**. Once ignited, a chemical reaction releases oxygen. The core's shape is selected to make a comparatively high oxygen flow available soon after activation.

The textbook represents the overall chemistry schematically as:

`NaClO3 + Fe → NaCl + FeO + O2`

**Chemistry note:** This is the expression printed in the textbook. It is a schematic representation and should not be treated as a balanced chemical equation.

### 7.2 Internal Parts and Operating Limits

A typical generator contains:

* A **chemical charge** from which oxygen is released.
* An ignition or **firing mechanism**.
* **Thermal insulation** surrounding the reaction zone.
* **Filters** that remove contaminants and help cool the outgoing oxygen.
* A **pressure-relief valve**.
* A **case**, manifold and oxygen outlet connections.

The source gives the following values:

|Parameter|Textbook figure|
|-|-:|
|Maximum oxygen temperature at outlet|No more than **10°C above cabin ambient temperature**|
|Pressure-relief limit|**50 psi**|
|Normal delivery pressure|Approximately **10 psi**|
|Minimum emergency descent supply|**15 minutes**|
|Longer-duration generator described|**22 minutes**|
|Potential external surface temperature|**232°C (450°F)**|
|Chemical generator shelf/installed life described|**10 years**|

A critical handling characteristic is that **the reaction cannot be stopped once started**. The generator can become extremely hot, and an **indicator strip or heat-sensitive paint** changes color—usually to black—after use, identifying an expended unit.

## 8\. Portable Oxygen Bottles

### 8.1 First-Aid and Sustaining Oxygen

Portable bottles are installed at accessible locations in the passenger cabin. Their role includes both supplying therapeutic oxygen and sustaining a person who needs a controlled breathing supply. A typical unit described in the chapter has **120 litres of oxygen**, a cylinder charged to approximately **1,800 psi**, and a carrying bag with straps.

The regulator normally provides two selectable flow settings:

|Setting|Flow|Duration from a 120 L supply|
|-|-:|-:|
|**Normal**|**2 L/min**|**60 min**|
|**High**|**4 L/min**|**30 min**|

Calculation check:

* Normal: `120 L ÷ 2 L/min = 60 min`.
* High: `120 L ÷ 4 L/min = 30 min`.

The source also mentions **310-litre** portable bottles, including units with **four-way manifolds** that can provide multiple outlets at medium or high settings.

### 8.2 Portable Equipment for Crew

Crew can use a conventional portable bottle when moving within the cabin during reduced-pressure conditions. Where smoke or another hostile cabin atmosphere is present, **full-face smoke protection equipment** is required instead of relying solely on a simple portable oxygen mask.

Such equipment can consist of a conventional cylinder connected to a full-face mask, or a specialized smoke hood/portable device containing its own generator. The generator-type apparatus described can operate for **15 minutes** once started. The chapter specifically notes that it requires **special training** and is **not intended for passenger use**.

## 9\. Oxygen System Safety Precautions

### 9.1 Oxygen Supports Burning

Although oxygen itself is **not flammable**, it makes other substances burn more intensely. The textbook also describes it as **heavier than air**. This makes oxygen-rich low areas, such as service pits and bilges, an important safety concern during maintenance.

The precautions emphasized are:

1. **No smoking or ignition sources** where oxygen concentration may be elevated; remove combustible materials during recharging operations.
2. **Keep oil and grease away.** Contact with oxygen can create violent chemical reactions and may lead to spontaneous combustion. Tools, gloves and clothing must therefore be uncontaminated.
3. **Avoid moisture contamination.** It may promote corrosion, valve freezing and an abnormal smell when the system is used. Refill only with **approved aviation oxygen**.
4. **Ventilate servicing areas adequately**, especially enclosed or low-lying spaces where oxygen could collect.
5. **Use only approved maintenance lubricants**—the textbook gives **graphite** as an example rather than ordinary grease or oil.
6. **Recognize cylinder colour conventions in the textbook:** American and European bottles are stated as **green**, whereas British cylinders are described as **black with a white neck**.

Flight crews and maintainers must also follow the aircraft-specific safety material in the applicable manuals; general principles do not replace those instructions.

## 10\. Historical EU-OPS Provisions Referenced in the Chapter

**Context:** The next sections paraphrase the EU-OPS rules printed in this edition of the textbook. They document the source's exam syllabus context and may not represent regulations currently in force.

### 10.1 OPS 1.760 — First-Aid Oxygen

For a pressurized aeroplane operating **above 25,000 ft** where carrying cabin crew is required, the historical provision requires undiluted oxygen for passengers who may have a physiological need after depressurization.

Key planning criteria given are:

* Allow at least **3 litres per minute STPD** per person for the prescribed remaining period of flight if cabin altitude is **above 8,000 ft but no more than 15,000 ft** following depressurization.
* Calculate supply for at least **2% of the actual passengers**, but **never fewer than one person**.
* Provide enough dispensing units for the operation, **not fewer than two**, with cabin-crew access. Units may be portable.
* Base the required quantity on **cabin-pressure altitude, flight duration, route and operating procedure**.
* Equipment should be capable of at least **4 L/min STPD** to an individual, with a means of lowering the rate to **not less than 2 L/min STPD**.

Here **STPD** refers to the standardized temperature, pressure and dry-gas reference condition used when stating the oxygen flow.

### 10.2 OPS 1.770 — Supplemental Oxygen for Pressurized Aircraft

Under the cited rule, operation above **10,000 ft pressure altitude** requires equipment capable of storing and dispensing the oxygen quantities specified by the provision.

The amount must be planned using:

* **Cabin pressure altitude** and the length of exposure.
* A hypothetical pressurization failure occurring at the **most demanding point** in the flight from the perspective of oxygen requirement.
* An emergency descent performed according to the aeroplane flight manual to a safe altitude at which flight and landing can continue.

After a pressurization failure, planning ordinarily assumes that **cabin pressure altitude becomes the same as aircraft pressure altitude**. A lower demonstrated maximum cabin altitude could be used only if the relevant authority accepted that no probable failure would lead to cabin altitude equalling the aircraft's pressure altitude.

### 10.3 Appendix 1 to OPS 1.770 — Pressurized Aircraft Supply Table

|Recipient|Period during which oxygen must be available under the cited appendix|
|-|-|
|**All flight-deck seat occupants on flight-deck duty**|All time **above 13,000 ft cabin pressure altitude**, and after the first **30 min** in the **above 10,000 to 13,000 ft** band. ### 10.4 Crew and Passenger Equipment Requirements

The cited OPS 1.770 further explains how people on board are categorized and how oxygen must be accessible:

**Flight deck:**

* Every member of the flight crew on duty must have the supplemental supply specified by the appendix.
* If another flight-deck occupant uses the **crew oxygen source**, the provision treats that person as an on-duty flight-crew occupant for oxygen planning; if that person is not supplied from the crew source, they are treated as a **passenger**.
* Crew members outside this on-duty category are also treated as passengers for these calculations.
* A flight crew mask should be **immediately reachable** from the assigned duty station.
* Above **25,000 ft** operation, the crew masks must be of a **quick-donning type**.

**Cabin:**

* The appendix specifies supplies for the required cabin crew and passengers. Extra cabin staff over the required number and other additional crew count as **passengers** for oxygen-supply purposes.
* Aircraft intended for operation above **25,000 ft** must distribute **spare outlets/masks or portable oxygen units** throughout the cabin so every required cabin-crew member can obtain oxygen immediately, wherever positioned when pressure is lost.
* Above **25,000 ft**, there must be oxygen delivery available immediately to each seated occupant. The total number of masks/outlets must exceed available seats by **at least 10%**, and the extra units must be evenly distributed.
* **Automatically deploying oxygen equipment** is specified for aircraft operating above 25,000 ft; it also applies to certain aircraft at or below 25,000 ft **unable to descend safely to 13,000 ft within four minutes**, where the first individual certificate of airworthiness was issued **on or after 9 November 1998**. The same **10% extra** and even-distribution provisions apply.
* For aircraft **not certificated above 25,000 ft**, the cited rule provides a limited reduction in particular oxygen quantities if every point along the route permits a safe descent to **13,000 ft within four minutes**. The reduction concerns supply for the required cabin crew and at least **10% of passengers** for the specified time in the **10,000–13,000 ft** cabin-altitude band.

### 10.5 OPS 1.775 — Supplemental Oxygen for Non-Pressurized Aircraft

The historical rule requires suitable supplemental oxygen equipment for non-pressurized operations **above 10,000 ft**.

**Appendix 1 to OPS 1.775** provides the following breakdown:

|Recipient|Requirement under the source's non-pressurized table|
|-|-|
|Everyone occupying a flight-deck seat while on flight-deck duty|**All flight time above 10,000 ft pressure altitude**.|
|Required cabin crew|**All flight time above 13,000 ft**, and any period **after the first 30 minutes** above **10,000 ft up to 13,000 ft**.|
|100% of passengers|**All flight time above 13,000 ft**.|
|10% of passengers|Time **after the first 30 minutes** above **10,000 ft up to 13,000 ft**.|

As in the cited regulation, the passenger count covers the people **actually carried**, including children **under two years of age**.

## 11\. Essential Comparisons

### 11.1 Continuous Flow Versus Diluter Demand

|Feature|Continuous flow|Diluter demand|
|-|-|-|
|Most typical use in the chapter|Passenger oxygen; some smaller-aircraft crew arrangements|Transport-aircraft flight-deck crew|
|When oxygen moves through mask supply|Continual regulated supply when operating|On the user's inhalation, normally|
|Cabin-air mixing|Not essential to the illustrated continuous-flow principle|Adjustable mixture determined by regulator and cabin altitude|
|High-altitude behavior|Delivered through regulator/orifice at set rate|Increasing oxygen fraction; source states pure oxygen by about 32,000 ft cabin altitude|
|Emergency smoke protection|Not the defining feature|100% oxygen with positive pressure available on emergency selection|

### 11.2 Gaseous Passenger Supply Versus Chemical Supply

|Feature|Stored gas|Chemical generator|
|-|-|-|
|Source|High-pressure cylinder|Chemical reaction in individual generator|
|Initiating a passenger mask|Pulling mask opens the gas check valve|Pulling mask triggers firing mechanism|
|Supply characteristic|Regulated gas flow|Reaction-based oxygen output following firing|
|Stop after activation|Governed by installed gas valves and controls|**Cannot stop the chemical reaction** once initiated|
|Specific hazard emphasized|High-pressure oxygen and contamination/combustion risk|Very hot generator housing and limited run time|
