# Chapter 8: Flight Controls

## 1\. Purpose of Flight Controls

Aircraft flight controls have **two fundamental jobs**:

1. **Establish and maintain equilibrium.** In a steady, balanced flight condition, the net turning moment about each aircraft axis must be zero. Control surfaces make it possible to obtain that balance over the aircraft's approved combinations of **centre-of-gravity (CG) position, speed, and configuration**.
2. **Change the aircraft's attitude and flight path.** By developing aerodynamic moments, the controls allow the pilot to manoeuvre about the three aircraft axes.

A control surface does not simply “move an aircraft in a direction.” It changes the aerodynamic force acting on a wing, tailplane, or fin. Because that force acts at a distance from an axis, it produces a **turning moment**.

### 1.1 Movement About the Three Axes

|Aircraft axis|Aircraft movement|Principal control surface or system|
|-|-|-|
|**Longitudinal** (nose-to-tail)|**Roll**|Ailerons, roll spoilers, or both|
|**Lateral** (wingtip-to-wingtip)|**Pitch**|Elevators or an all-moving tailplane|
|**Normal/vertical**|**Yaw**|Rudder|

### 1.2 Surfaces With Combined Functions

Some designs use one type of movable surface to perform more than one function:

* **Elevon:** Combines the functions of elevator and aileron. On **tailless aircraft**, it produces both pitch and roll control.
* **Ruddervator:** A control surface on a **V-tail** that contributes to both pitch and yaw control.
* **Stabilator:** An **all-moving horizontal tailplane** that provides both the stability normally associated with a fixed stabilizer and the control normally associated with an elevator.

### 1.3 Three Ways to Develop a Control Moment

A control system can alter the force on an aerofoil in three basic ways:

* **Alter camber.** A downward-deflected movable surface increases the effective camber, normally increasing the aerofoil's lift or aerodynamic force in the relevant operating condition. The principle is used by the **elevator, aileron, and rudder** to produce their respective moments.
* **Alter aerofoil incidence / angle of attack.** Moving the entire aerofoil changes its angle relative to the airflow. For pitch control, this is a characteristic of the **moving tailplane or stabilator**.
* **Spoil the airflow.** Raising a spoiler disturbs airflow over the **upper wing surface**, reducing lift. When this is done on one wing only, the resulting difference in wing lift creates a rolling moment.

## 2\. Spoiling Wing Lift to Obtain Roll

A spoiler is a movable panel installed in the upper surface of the wing. When raised, it interrupts the local airflow and **reduces the lift** generated by that part of the wing.

If the spoiler on **one wing** rises while the opposite wing's spoiler remains down, the wing with reduced lift tends to drop, causing a roll. A spoiler therefore obtains lateral control by **removing lift**, not by adding lift to the other wing.

## 3\. Hinge Moments

A deflected control surface is subjected to an **aerodynamic force**. Because that force usually acts away from the hinge, it tends to turn the control surface about the hinge line.

This turning tendency is the **hinge moment**:

$$
M\_h = F \\times d
$$

where:

* **Mₕ** = hinge moment;
* **F** = aerodynamic force on the control surface;
* **d** = perpendicular distance from the hinge to the control surface's centre of pressure.

A hinge moment can arise from either the **aerofoil's angle of attack** or the **deflection of the control surface**. For the purpose of analysis, the overall hinge moment is treated as the sum of those two effects.

To keep the surface in a chosen position, a pilot operating a mechanically connected control must apply an opposing force through the cockpit controls. Therefore **larger hinge moment generally means larger cockpit control force**.

## 4\. Why Control Surfaces Need Balancing

For a given deflection, the force on an aerodynamic control surface depends on its **size** and on **the square of the airspeed**. Consequently, a large surface on a fast aircraft can develop such a high hinge moment that direct manual operation would demand excessive stick or pedal forces.

The general approaches to reducing the pilot's effort are:

1. **Power assistance or powered controls**, which provide operating force by a separate system; and
2. **Aerodynamic balancing**, which arranges aerodynamic forces so that they help overcome part of the main hinge moment.

The methods in the following sections concern aerodynamic balance. **Mass balancing**, covered later, has a different purpose: protection against flutter.

## 5\. Aerodynamic Balance

Aerodynamic balancing reduces control force by making some aerodynamic loading create a moment **opposite to the main surface's hinge moment**. Several forms are used.

### 5.1 Set-Back Hinge Line

The normal hinge moment depends on the distance between the **hinge line** and the control surface's **centre of pressure**. If the hinge is positioned farther aft, within the control surface, that distance becomes smaller.

As a result:

* The **moment arm** decreases.
* The **hinge moment** decreases for the same applied aerodynamic force.
* The aerodynamic force itself, and therefore the surface's **control effectiveness**, is not inherently reduced by this balancing arrangement.

### 5.2 Horn Balance

A **horn balance** places a portion of the control surface **ahead of the hinge line**. Aerodynamic loading on this forward-projecting part tends to create a turning moment opposite to the moment developed by the main part of the control surface.

The result is a reduced net hinge moment and lighter cockpit control loads **without reducing the useful aerodynamic effectiveness of the main surface**.

### 5.3 Internal Balance

An **internal aerodynamic balance** uses the same counter-moment principle, but the balancing area is housed **inside the wing** rather than exposed externally.

Suppose the main control surface moves **down**:

1. Pressure over the aerofoil becomes **lower**.
2. Pressure below the aerofoil becomes **higher**.
3. Those pressures also act on the internal balancing surface: low pressure above it and high pressure below it.
4. The resulting force creates a hinge moment **opposite** to the main control-surface hinge moment.
5. The opposing moments reduce the overall load that must be resisted by the pilot or operating mechanism.

### 5.4 Balance Tab

A **balance tab** is a small movable surface at the trailing edge of a larger control surface. Unlike a set-back hinge, horn, or internal balance, it assists the control by applying a counteracting force **near the control surface's trailing edge**.

* The tab is mechanically arranged to deflect **opposite** to the main control surface.
* Its aerodynamic force opposes the force contributing to the main surface's hinge moment.
* The net hinge moment is reduced, allowing a lower cockpit operating force.
* Because the tab's force opposes part of the main surface's aerodynamic force, **some control effectiveness is lost**.

### 5.5 Anti-Balance Tab

An **anti-balance tab** is geared to move in **the same direction** as the main control surface. Its aerodynamic action reinforces the force produced by the larger surface.

* **Control effectiveness increases.**
* **Hinge moment increases.**
* **Cockpit stick forces become heavier.**

### 5.7 Servo Tab

The **servo tab** is different from a simple balance tab: it provides the aerodynamic effort **needed to move the main control surface** rather than merely reducing a direct control load.

Operating sequence:

1. The pilot moves the cockpit control.
2. That movement **deflects the servo tab**, not the whole main control surface directly.
3. Airflow acting on the tab produces an aerodynamic force.
4. The tab force turns the main control surface until an **equilibrium position** is reached.

**Ground-check implications:** If the aircraft is stationary and there is no useful airflow, moving the cockpit control can move **the tab only**, without visibly moving the main control surface. Likewise, even when external locks secure the main surface, the cockpit control may still be free to move because it is connected to the servo tab.

## 6\. Mass Balancing and Flutter Prevention

**Mass balance** must not be confused with aerodynamic balance. Its purpose is principally **to prevent control-surface flutter**, not to make the pilot's control forces lighter.

### 6.1 Why Flutter Can Develop

Flutter is an oscillatory motion of a control surface that may arise from **structural bending and twisting while aerodynamic forces act on it**.

If a control surface's **centre of gravity lies behind its hinge line**, the inertia associated with the surface's mass can cause the surface to oscillate about the hinge as the supporting structure flexes. Under particular conditions, the oscillation can become **divergent**—increasing rather than dying away—and may ultimately destroy the structure.

### 6.2 Principle of Mass Balance

A weight is attached to, or arranged within, the control surface **ahead of the hinge line**. It shifts the control-surface CG forward, normally to a location **near or slightly forward of the hinge**, as specified by the aircraft designer.

This reduces the inertial moments about the hinge that contribute to flutter. The required final location is **the manufacturer's prescribed balance point**, not an arbitrary position chosen by the mechanic or pilot.

## 7\. Longitudinal Control (Pitch)

Pitch control is normally provided by:

* **Elevators**, attached to a fixed horizontal stabilizer; or
* An **all-moving horizontal tailplane**, which changes its incidence as a complete surface.

Whatever arrangement is used, pitch control must be sufficiently effective to:

1. Balance the aircraft over the **full permitted speed range**.
2. Work at **every approved CG position**.
3. Remain effective in the **approved aircraft configurations**.
4. Produce an **adequate pitch rate during manoeuvres**.

These requirements cover both **trim/balance** and the ability to **change pitch attitude when commanded**.

## 8\. Lateral Control (Roll)

### 8.1 Aileron Principle

Ailerons control roll by **increasing lift on one wing and reducing lift on the other**. The resulting difference in aerodynamic force across the span produces a rolling moment.

### 8.2 Adverse Aileron Yaw

An aileron-controlled roll changes **induced drag** as well as lift:

* The wing whose lift increases experiences **more induced drag**.
* The wing whose lift decreases experiences **less induced drag**.
* This drag imbalance creates a yawing moment **opposite** to the intended roll.

For example, a command to **roll left** may produce a **yawing tendency to the right**. This unwanted response is called **adverse yaw**.

### 8.3 Methods Used to Reduce Adverse Yaw

**Differential ailerons.** The linkages deflect the **upgoing aileron farther** than the downgoing one. This increases drag associated with the upgoing aileron and reduces the difference in total drag between wings, helping to counter adverse yaw.

**Frise ailerons.** These have an **asymmetrically shaped leading edge**. As the aileron moves up, its leading edge projects below the lower wing surface and generates additional drag. On the opposite side, the leading edge of the downward-moving aileron stays shielded and causes less drag. The drag difference helps counter the yaw created by the lift imbalance.

**Aileron–rudder coupling.** A connection between the aileron and rudder mechanisms automatically deflects the **rudder in the direction required to oppose adverse yaw** when the ailerons are operated.

**Roll-spoiler assistance.** Raising a spoiler on the **descending wing** adds drag to that wing while reducing lift, so a spoiler used with ailerons can also **reduce adverse yaw**.

### 8.4 Inboard Ailerons

An aileron near the **wingtip** acts with a large moment arm, so it can create a strong roll moment. However, that position also produces **large wing-bending and twisting loads**, which may cause reduced aileron effectiveness or even **aileron reversal**.

There are two common structural/control arrangements to reduce the problem:

* Place the ailerons **farther inboard**, where their action creates less severe wing distortion.
* Fit **two sets**: outboard ailerons for **low-speed flight** (smaller aerodynamic loads), and inboard ailerons for **high-speed flight** (larger loads and greater risk of distortion).

For the dual-aileron arrangement described in the textbook, **only inboard ailerons operate with the flaps retracted**.

### 8.5 Flaperons

The wing's trailing edge must accommodate both:

* **Flaps**, which ideally occupy a large span for good take-off and landing performance; and
* **Ailerons**, which ideally also have a large span to provide a strong roll response.

Because trailing-edge space is limited, **flaperons** combine some or all of these functions:

1. Ailerons may droop **symmetrically**, increasing the effective flap area.
2. Starting from that drooped position, they may move **differentially** for roll control.
3. Alternatively, movable trailing-edge sections may be designed from the outset to act as **both flaps and ailerons**.

## 9\. Spoilers as Roll Controls

A **spoiler** is a hinged portion of the upper wing surface that rises into the airflow. It can provide roll control **instead of** or **in addition to** an aileron.

For a roll command, the spoiler is raised on the wing intended to go **down**, whereas the opposite spoiler stays stowed. Because the raised panel destroys some lift instead of increasing it, **spoiler-generated roll inherently involves a net reduction in total lift**.

### 9.1 Advantages of Spoiler Roll Control

Compared with ailerons, the source identifies five key benefits:

1. **No adverse yaw:** A raised spoiler increases drag on the side of the aircraft that is dropping, producing yaw **in the same direction** as roll rather than against it.
2. **Less wing twist:** Aileron forces act heavily near the trailing edge; spoiler lift reduction is spread more across the wing chord, tending to lessen twisting loads.
3. **Useful at transonic speeds:** Spoiler effectiveness is not reduced by **shock-induced flow separation** in the manner discussed for ailerons.
4. **No spoiler flutter:** The textbook identifies spoilers as not being susceptible to flutter.
5. **Preserved trailing-edge space:** The spoiler is on the **upper wing surface**, leaving the trailing edge available for flaps.

### 9.2 Combined Aileron-and-Spoiler Systems

Although a few aircraft use spoilers as their only roll control, it is more common for **ailerons and spoilers to work together**.

Reasons for combining them include:

* **Low speed:** Dynamic pressure is small, so ailerons alone may not achieve the desired roll rate.
* **High speed:** Ailerons may cause excessive wing twist and can lose effectiveness as shock-related separation becomes important.
* **Operating-range requirements:** Spoiler assistance might be required over **part** of the speed range rather than throughout it.

On certain aircraft, roll spoilers are used only at low speeds and are made **inoperative when the flaps are retracted**.

A **mixer unit** interprets the pilot's lateral-control input:

* When the aileron moves **up**, the corresponding spoiler rises to help produce roll.
* When the aileron moves **down**, the corresponding spoiler remains **retracted**.

## 10\. Speed Brakes

**Speed brakes** are devices intended to produce extra drag when the aircraft must **slow down quickly** or **descend at a high rate**.

Typical circumstances described in the chapter include:

* Rapid reduction to **rough-air speed** after turbulence is encountered at high speed.
* A rapid descent to comply with an **air traffic control instruction**.
* A particularly high rate of descent during an **emergency descent**.

### 10.1 Different Types of Speed Brake

An ideal speed brake would increase **drag** without changing **lift** or the aircraft's **pitching moment**. A **fuselage-mounted speed brake** is well suited to this ideal because its drag increase need not directly interfere with wing lift.

However, **wing-mounted spoilers** also produce drag when raised, so they can conveniently serve as speed brakes while retaining their roll-control function.

### 10.4 Ground Spoilers / Lift Dumpers

An aircraft slows down after touchdown because of both **aerodynamic drag** and **wheel-braking drag**.

Wheel-brake effectiveness depends on the force pressing the tyres against the ground. If the wing is still producing lift, that force is reduced. Raising spoilers after landing **removes some of the remaining wing lift**, so:

1. **Weight on the wheels increases**.
2. **Wheel braking becomes more effective**.
3. The deployed spoilers also **increase aerodynamic drag**.
4. These effects help **shorten the landing run**.

Many aircraft have extra spoiler panels specifically for ground use. The textbook describes an **undercarriage-leg switch** that makes the ground-spoiler function inoperative in flight; it changes state as the leg extends after take-off.

## 11\. Directional Control (Yaw)

The **rudder** controls aircraft motion about the normal/vertical axis. It is needed for several different situations:

* Holding direction with **asymmetric engine power**.
* Correcting for **crosswinds on take-off and landing**.
* Countering **adverse yaw**.
* Assisting **spin recovery**.
* Opposing **changes in propeller torque** on single-engine aeroplanes.

### 11.1 Excessive Rudder Deflection at High Speed

With an uncomplicated direct-link control system, pressing a rudder pedal through its entire travel would deflect the rudder through its entire available range.

A high-speed aircraft nevertheless needs two different capabilities:

* **Large rudder deflections at low speed**, when rudder effectiveness is relatively low; and
* **Limited rudder deflection at high speed**, to prevent excessive loads on the tail and supporting structure.

The chapter outlines two principal limiting arrangements.

### 11.2 Rudder Ratio-Changing System

The **pedals retain their full travel** at every speed. As **indicated airspeed (IAS) increases**, the system reduces the amount of actual **rudder deflection** corresponding to a given pedal movement.

**Key distinction:** Pedal range remains unchanged; the **pedal-to-rudder ratio changes**.

### 11.3 Variable-Stop System

Here, rudder deflection stays **directly proportional to pedal movement**, but the system physically limits the **available pedal travel** as IAS rises. Since the pedals cannot move as far, neither can the rudder.

The source states that this reduction **usually begins at about 165 kt IAS**.

**Key distinction:** The effective ratio remains proportional; the **available travel of both pedals and rudder is reduced**.

## 12\. Trimming — Meaning and Purpose

An aircraft is considered **trimmed** when it can maintain the selected attitude and airspeed **without continuous holding force on the cockpit controls**.

Sometimes a primary control surface must remain deflected to balance an aerodynamic moment. If the pilot has to hold that deflection manually, a continuing stick or pedal force is required. A trim system adjusts the balancing arrangement so that the **required holding force falls to zero**.

### 12.1 Reasons for Trimming in Pitch

Pitch trim may have to change because of:

* **Airspeed changes**.
* **Power changes**.
* **Changes in CG position**.

### 12.2 Reasons for Trimming in Yaw

Yaw trim is particularly useful for:

* **Asymmetric power** on multi-engine aircraft.
* **Changing propeller torque**.

### 12.3 Reasons for Trimming in Roll

Roll trimming is less commonly needed but may be necessary when:

* The aircraft has an **asymmetric configuration**; or
* The **CG is displaced sideways** from the aircraft's centreline.

### 12.4 Main Trim Methods

The chapter lists five approaches:

1. **Trim tabs**.
2. **Variable-incidence tailplane**.
3. **Spring-bias system**.
4. **CG adjustment**.
5. **Artificial-feel-unit adjustment**.

## 13\. Trimming Tab

A **trim tab** is a small adjustable flap built into the **trailing edge of a primary control surface**. The pilot sets it using a cockpit **trim wheel or switch**, normally laid out so that the adjustment direction feels natural or instinctive.

To relieve the pilot's holding force, the trim tab moves **opposite to the main control surface's required deflection**. The tab produces its own aerodynamic force, which creates a balancing hinge moment.

The trim adjustment is complete when the **tab's moment balances the main surface's hinge moment**, allowing the main surface to remain in the required position without pilot holding force.

## 14\. Variable-Incidence Tailplane Trim

Changing the angle of the **entire horizontal tailplane** is another means of trimming the aircraft, and it can be used with **manually operated or powered tailplane/elevator systems**.

### 14.1 Manual Elevator Arrangement

In an aircraft with manually operated elevators:

* Aerodynamic load on the **elevator** is transmitted back to the pilot's control column.
* The tailplane's own load is **not felt in the same way** through that control.

To trim, the pilot changes **tailplane incidence** until the combined aerodynamic load from the tailplane and the **free elevator** provides the balancing force needed for equilibrium.

### 14.2 Trim Controls and Runaway Protection

Tailplane incidence may be changed by a **trim wheel** or by a paired **electric trim-switch arrangement**. Paired switches are commonly on the control wheel, with possible additional paired levers on the centre console.

The two controls have different functions:

* One enables the **power** for the trim drive.
* The other selects the **direction of movement**.

**Both must be operated together** to command trimming. This prevents accidental actuation that might otherwise cause unintended continuous longitudinal trim motion, described as **trim runaway**.

### 14.3 Advantages of Variable-Incidence Tailplane Trim

The textbook gives two principal advantages:

1. **Lower drag when trimmed.** The tailplane/elevator combination remains more streamlined than it would with a large sustained elevator deflection.
2. **Full pitch-control range remains available.** When the aircraft is correctly trimmed, the elevator is approximately **neutral**, so a large fraction of its deflection range has not been used merely to hold the trim condition.

These benefits remain relevant to **powered elevator systems**, even though the pilot does not feel aerodynamic elevator forces directly.

### 14.4 Correct Take-Off Stabilizer Setting

The required tailplane trim depends on **CG position**. Recommended stabilizer values are provided in the **aircraft Flight Manual**.

Correct setting **before take-off** is critical:

* An incorrect setting can produce an **excessively rapid pitch-up on rotation**, potentially causing a **tail strike**.
* A setting in the other direction may give **very large rotation stick forces**, potentially **increasing take-off distance requirements**.

## 15\. Spring-Bias Trim

With **spring-bias trim**, an adjustable spring applies the force needed to offset the pilot's normal holding load.

The aircraft can therefore be trimmed **without a separate trim tab**: the spring provides an opposing control force instead of using an aerodynamically deflected tab.

\---

## 16\. CG Adjustment as a Trim Method

If a primary flight control has to remain deflected simply to balance a pitching moment, that deflection creates **additional drag**.

An alternative is to move the aircraft's **centre of gravity nearer the centre of pressure**, reducing the unbalanced pitching moment. Less compensating aerodynamic load is then required and associated drag falls, potentially **improving cruise range**.

The source describes performing this adjustment by transferring **fuel between forward and aft trim tanks**.

This changes the aircraft's mass distribution rather than relying solely on a deflected control surface to counter the pitching moment.

\---

## 17\. Artificial-Feel Trim

A **power-operated control system** usually prevents aerodynamic loads at the flight-control surface from being transmitted directly to the cockpit control.

For acceptable control feel, an **artificial-feel mechanism** is fitted. As the pilot moves the control, the mechanism develops a resisting force so that the control does not feel completely unloaded.

To trim this type of system, the **reference or datum position** of the feel mechanism can be adjusted until it no longer applies a force at the desired control position.

Thus, the cockpit holding force can be eliminated **without relying on natural aerodynamic feedback at the control column**.

\---

## 18\. Mach Trim

As an aircraft approaches **high-subsonic speeds**, the wing's **centre of pressure moves rearward**. That shift creates a tendency toward a strong **nose-down pitching moment**, referred to in the chapter as **tuck-under**.

To counter that change automatically, aircraft may be equipped with a **Mach-trim system**.

### 18.1 Operating Logic

* Mach trim must remain capable of operating **whether the autopilot or another automatic flight-control function is engaged or not**.
* The system detects increases in speed **above a predetermined Mach-number datum**.
* Through a **servo arrangement**, it then produces the corrective action needed to maintain the trimmed flight condition.
* Correction may involve moving the **horizontal stabilizer** or **shifting the aircraft's CG**.

# 22\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions and Answers

**1. What are the two overall purposes of flight controls?**  
To balance the aircraft in steady flight across permitted CG/configuration conditions and to manoeuvre it about its three axes.

**2. Which axis corresponds to roll, and which controls can produce it?**  
The longitudinal axis; ailerons, spoilers, or a combination.

**3. Which controls primarily produce pitch and yaw?**  
Pitch: elevator or moving tailplane. Yaw: rudder.

**4. How do elevons, ruddervators, and stabilators differ?**  
Elevons combine pitch and roll; ruddervators on V-tails combine pitch and yaw; stabilators are all-moving tailplanes providing stability and pitch control.

**5. Name the three basic ways an aerodynamic control can create a turning moment.**  
Change aerofoil camber, change incidence/angle of attack, or spoil the airflow to reduce lift.

**6. What determines hinge moment?**  
The aerodynamic force acting on the control and its perpendicular distance from the hinge, as expressed by `Mₕ = F × d`.

**7. Why are high-speed aircraft more likely to need aerodynamic balancing or powered controls?**  
Because force on a deflected control rises with its size and approximately the square of speed, potentially creating excessive hinge moments and cockpit loads.

**8. Does moving the hinge line aft reduce aerodynamic force itself?**  
No. It mainly shortens the moment arm, reducing hinge moment without inherently removing control effectiveness.

**9. How does a horn balance reduce pilot effort?**  
Its section ahead of the hinge develops an aerodynamic moment opposing the main control-surface hinge moment.

**10. Where is an internal balance located?**  
Inside the wing, where pressure differences acting on its balancing area oppose the main control hinge moment.

**11. How does a balance tab move and what is its disadvantage?**  
It moves opposite to the main surface to reduce hinge moment, but can slightly reduce control effectiveness.

**12. What happens with an anti-balance tab?**  
It moves with the main surface, increasing effectiveness, hinge moment, and cockpit control force.

**13. What makes a spring tab especially useful at high airspeed?**  
A spring in the linkage causes assistance to increase as the pilot's required stick force increases.

**14. When a servo-tab-controlled aircraft is stationary, what may move when the pilot moves the cockpit control?**  
The servo tab may move without the main control surface moving, because useful aerodynamic force is unavailable.

**15. Why is a control surface mass balanced?**  
To position its CG near or forward of the hinge as prescribed, reducing inertial tendencies that could develop into flutter.

**16. What must longitudinal control achieve across the operating envelope?**  
Balance at approved speeds/CGs/configurations plus an adequate pitch manoeuvre rate.

**17. Describe adverse aileron yaw.**  
The wing gaining lift gains induced drag, tending to yaw the aircraft opposite to the intended roll.

**18. Name four means of reducing adverse yaw.**  
Differential ailerons, Frise ailerons, aileron–rudder coupling, and roll spoilers used to supplement ailerons.

**19. Why might outboard ailerons be restricted at high speed?**  
Their large moment arm can impose high twisting and bending loads, reducing or even reversing effectiveness.

**20. What is a flaperon?**  
A trailing-edge surface that contributes to flap operation and can move differentially for roll control.

**21. Why does spoiler roll control create a net lift loss?**  
Spoilers reduce lift on one wing and cannot add an equivalent lift increase on the opposite wing.

**22. Give three advantages of spoiler roll control stated in the chapter.**  
No adverse yaw, reduced wing twist, and retained trailing-edge room for flaps. The text also lists good transonic effectiveness and no spoiler flutter.

**23. How does a mixer coordinate ailerons and spoilers?**  
It raises the spoiler associated with an upgoing aileron and holds the spoiler retracted when the aileron moves down.

**24. Why might speed brakes be deployed in flight?**  
To reduce speed quickly or increase descent rate, including in turbulence, ATC-directed descents, or emergency descents.

**25. What happens to wing-mounted spoilers when used as speed brakes?**  
They are raised symmetrically but can still move differentially to provide roll control.

**26. How do ground spoilers assist braking?**  
They reduce wing lift, put more aircraft weight onto the wheels, and increase aerodynamic drag, helping shorten the landing run.

**27. How do rudder ratio changing and variable stops differ?**  
Ratio changing keeps full pedal travel while reducing rudder response. Variable stops restrict available pedal travel and hence rudder travel as speed rises.

**28. At roughly what IAS does the textbook say variable rudder stops commonly begin restricting movement?**  
About **165 kt IAS**.

**29. What does it mean for an aircraft to be trimmed?**  
It can maintain the desired flight condition without continuous pilot holding force at the cockpit controls.

**30. Which conditions commonly require pitch-trim changes?**  
Changes in airspeed, power, or CG position.

**31. Why does a trim tab move opposite to the main control surface?**  
To produce a balancing hinge moment that cancels the main surface's holding-force requirement.

**32. What is special about a fixed trim tab?**  
It is adjusted on the ground rather than in flight, commonly to correct a persistent aileron trim tendency.

**33. State two advantages of variable-incidence tailplane trim.**  
Less trimmed-state drag and a larger retained elevator pitch-control range.

**34. Why must stabilizer take-off settings be correct?**  
Incorrect trim can cause excessive rotation pitch-up and tail-strike risk, or heavy rotation forces and longer take-off distance.

**35. Why can two switches be required to move electric stabilizer trim?**  
One enables power and the other commands direction; requiring simultaneous operation helps prevent unintended trim runaway.

**36. What does a spring-bias trim system change?**  
The force applied by an adjustable spring so that the pilot need not hold the corresponding force manually.

**37. How can fore–aft fuel transfer improve cruise range?**  
By moving the CG closer to the centre of pressure, reducing the control deflection and drag needed for pitch balance.

**38. What does artificial-feel trim adjust?**  
The reference/datum of an artificial control-force unit so its resistance disappears at the desired position.

**39. What is the purpose of Mach trim?**  
To compensate automatically for the nose-down pitching tendency as the centre of pressure moves aft at high-subsonic Mach numbers.

**40. What is used to show control positions on modern versus some older aircraft?**  
Modern transports typically use electronic systems displays; some older types have mechanical “baby aeroplane” indicators.
