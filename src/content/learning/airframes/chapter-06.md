# Chapter 6: Aircraft Brakes---

# 1\. Introduction to Aircraft Wheel Braking

## 1.1 How Aircraft Brakes Stop an Aircraft

Aircraft wheel brakes slow the wheels by producing **friction between a rotating surface and a fixed friction surface**. The process removes kinetic energy from the moving aircraft and changes that energy mainly into **heat** at the brakes. Consequently, a brake must do more than generate enough friction: it must also absorb and release the resulting heat without failing.

This becomes a major engineering challenge on large, fast aircraft. As aircraft mass and operating speed have increased, the energy to be removed during a landing or rejected take-off has grown considerably. Improvements in brake design and heat management have made these operations possible, but heat remains a critical brake limitation.

## 1.2 Why Wheel Brakes Are Still Necessary

In theory, sufficiently long runways could allow an aircraft to roll to a halt without substantial wheel braking. In practice, runways of **four or five miles** would be prohibitively expensive and impractical to provide routinely. Aircraft therefore rely on wheel brakes and other methods of deceleration.

* On **propeller-driven aircraft**, reverse-pitch propellers can contribute to slowing the aircraft.
* On **jet aircraft**, engine reverse thrust provides additional retardation.
* These systems help reduce the demand on wheel brakes but **do not eliminate the need for normal wheel braking**.

The brake system must therefore provide dependable deceleration while remaining within its structural and thermal limits.

# 2\. Plate or Disc Brakes

## 2.1 Basic Operating Principle

Modern aircraft use **hydraulically operated disc or plate brakes**. A friction surface that does not rotate presses against one or more plates rotating with the wheel. Contact between these surfaces generates the braking force.

The size of the aircraft and its wheel arrangement determine how many friction surfaces are needed:

* A **light aircraft** may use a relatively simple **single-disc brake**.
* A **large transport aircraft** usually requires a **multi-plate brake**, containing several friction interfaces in one unit.

Multiple interfaces greatly increase the total area available for braking without making the wheel assembly excessively large.

## 2.2 Rotors, Stators and the Disc Pack

The main elements of a multi-plate brake include:

|Component|Purpose|
|-|-|
|**Rotor**|Rotating friction plate connected to, and turning with, the wheel rim.|
|**Stator**|Non-rotating plate or friction assembly restrained by the fixed brake structure.|
|**Disc pack**|Alternating rotors and stators creating multiple friction interfaces.|
|**Pressure plate**|Transfers force from the hydraulic pistons to compress the pack.|
|**Thrust plate**|Provides the reaction surface against which the pack is squeezed.|
|**Operating pistons/cylinders**|Convert hydraulic pressure into the mechanical force needed to apply the brake.|
|**Torque plate and brake mounting structure**|Support and locate the brake parts and pass braking loads into the aircraft structure.|

When a pilot applies brake-pedal force, the hydraulic system drives pistons in the brake assembly. These move the pressure plate, compressing the rotor-and-stator stack against the thrust plate. As the force on the stack rises, so does the resisting torque acting on the wheel. The pilot can therefore vary the braking effect through brake demand.

The braking **reaction torque** is carried from the brake assembly to the **main landing-gear leg** through a torque rod or **brake bar**; the textbook refers back to the arrangement shown in **Chapter 3, Figure 3.2**.

## 2.3 Brake Materials and Fire Precautions

The textbook describes conventional brake friction pads as being made from an **inorganic friction material**, with the friction plates made of **heavy steel** and provided with a specially **case-hardened surface**.

Red-hot steel brake plates can be damaged catastrophically if exposed to **liquid extinguishing agent**. In the textbook's wheel/brake-fire guidance, **dry powder** is identified as the preferred extinguishant. This warning is particularly important because the brake and wheel assembly may already have absorbed an extremely large amount of energy.

## 2.4 Segmented Discs and Carbon Brakes

Brake designers have improved heat dissipation by replacing some continuous rotating discs with discs constructed from **multiple connected segments**. A segmented arrangement offers better heat dissipation and can increase the brake's effectiveness.

**Carbon brakes** provide another solution. Compared with comparable steel brake units, they offer:

* Better ability to **absorb braking heat**.
* Better **heat dissipation**.
* **Lower weight**.

The textbook also identifies disadvantages: **higher purchase cost** and **shorter service life** in its comparison. They are therefore particularly attractive for aircraft where the mass saving justifies the additional expense, such as long-haul aircraft.

## 2.5 Brake Fade

**Brake fade** is the reduction in braking effectiveness associated with excessive brake temperature. When a brake has become too hot, it cannot continue to absorb the required energy effectively, so its ability to slow the aircraft decreases.

Brake fade is therefore different from a failure to operate the pedals: the brake may still be applied, but the hot friction assembly provides less effective retardation.

# 3\. Brake Release

## 3.1 What Happens When Pedal Pressure Is Removed

Releasing the brake pedals removes the commanded hydraulic force. **Brake adjuster assemblies** then draw the pressure plate away from the rotor-and-stator stack, allowing the components to separate slightly and the wheel to rotate without unwanted friction.

The adjuster is constructed to retain a **nearly constant running clearance** when the brakes are off. It can also compensate automatically as friction material wears down, so the release gap does not need to increase continually with lining wear.

## 3.2 Brake Drag and Its Consequences

A **failed return spring** in an adjuster or **incorrect adjuster setting** can prevent the brake from retracting properly. The rotating and stationary friction members then continue rubbing despite the pedals being released. This is called **brake drag**.

Brake drag produces heat even when braking is not intended. As a result, the brake reaches an excessively high temperature sooner and **brake fade may develop earlier** than it otherwise would.

# 4\. Brake Wear and Inspection

## 4.1 Why Lining Thickness Matters

Brake units are designed to produce strong retardation without wearing their friction material excessively. Nevertheless, the **remaining brake-lining thickness must be monitored**.

If too little material remains:

* In a **single-disc brake**, the disc can become badly **worn or grooved**.
* In a **multiple-disc brake**, the reduced friction material may **overheat and erode rapidly**.

Inspection methods depend on the particular brake design; the methods below are the examples given in the chapter.

## 4.2 Retraction-Pin or Indicator-Pin Measurement

On many multi-disc brakes, the most widely used wear indication is the position of the **retraction pin**, sometimes called an **indicator pin**, in relation to its **spring housing**.

The measurement is performed **with the brakes applied**. As the friction stack wears, the indicator pin extends from or moves farther into its housing. A specified wear gauge or limit is used to decide whether the remaining lining is acceptable.

## 4.3 Measurement Without an Indicator Pin

If the brake assembly has no retraction/indicator pin, the remaining wear can be assessed by measuring the **clearance between the rear of the pressure plate and the brake housing**, again with the brakes **applied**.

## 4.4 Measuring a Single-Disc Brake

For a single-disc unit, apply the brakes and check the **distance between the disc and the brake housing**. The gap must **not be smaller than the specified minimum**. The chapter does not provide one universal numerical limit because the acceptable value depends on the brake type.

# 5\. Brake System Operation

## 5.1 Pedals and Hydraulic Pressure

Brake pedals on the flight deck control **hydraulic pressure** supplied to the brake mechanism. Pressurised fluid moves the pistons and pressure plate, bringing stationary friction components against the wheel's rotating brake discs. The friction produced slows the wheel.

The method of supplying that pressure varies with the aircraft:

* **Small aircraft:** Hydraulic pressure generated by pedal operation may itself be sufficient to stop the aircraft.
* **Large aircraft:** The force available from the pilot's feet is inadequate on its own. The wheel brakes are therefore supplied with pressure from the **main aircraft hydraulic system**.

# 6\. Brake Modulating Systems and Anti-Skid Protection

## 6.1 Why Brake Modulation Is Necessary

High landing speeds, substantial aircraft weight, and relatively low aerodynamic drag make efficient braking important, especially when a runway is **short or contaminated by adverse weather**.

The pilot may not recognise precisely when one wheel is locking. For this reason, a modulating brake system must provide **anti-skid protection**, controlling braking pressure before a wheel stops rotating while the aircraft is still travelling.

An effective brake works with **controlled slip** between the tyre and ground. A fully skidding wheel offers **very little useful braking effect**. In the systems described here, each wheel's **deceleration rate** is the principal measurement used to control braking torque.

## 6.2 Detecting Imminent Wheel Lock

The anti-skid system compares wheel deceleration with a reference chosen to be **greater than the maximum deceleration the whole aircraft could reasonably achieve**. The chapter uses approximately **18 ft/s² (6 m/s²)** as the reference magnitude.

If a wheel decelerates faster than the selected reference, the system interprets the change as an approaching skid and **reduces or temporarily releases hydraulic brake pressure**.

Depending on design, the system can also prevent brake application **before touchdown** or **while a wheel is bouncing**, when effective wheel-to-runway contact is absent.

The chapter distinguishes **mechanical** and **electrical/electronic** anti-skid designs. Mechanical versions have existed since the **early 1950s**, although electronic types are common on modern aircraft.

## 6.3 Effect on Take-Off and Landing Performance

Anti-skid protection can **reduce the stopping distance required during landing and rejected take-off** by preventing ineffective wheel lock.

If anti-skid is inoperative, the required landing or take-off distance increases; the relevant performance data must be used to establish the required runway length, particularly for an **aborted take-off**.

> \*\*Textbook operational note:\*\* The source specifically states that take-off on a \*\*wet runway is prohibited when anti-skid is inoperative\*\*. This is reproduced as the textbook's rule; actual dispatch decisions must follow the applicable aircraft and operator documentation.

# 7\. Mechanical Anti-Skid Systems

A mechanical anti-skid unit detects rapid wheel deceleration using the **inertia of a flywheel**.

Its operation can be understood in the following sequence:

1. The aircraft wheel drives a separate rotating member.
2. The driven member is connected to an internal **flywheel by a spring**.
3. When aircraft wheel speed changes quickly, the flywheel tends to continue at its previous speed because of inertia.
4. The resulting **relative displacement** between the flywheel and driven member becomes the control signal.
5. The signal operates a **hydraulic valve**, which releases or reduces brake pressure.

The sensing assembly may be fitted at the **wheel rim or axle**.

The key limitation is that the mechanical flywheel responds more slowly than an electrical signal and may not adjust pressure as accurately to the real condition of the runway.

\---

# 8\. Electronic Anti-Skid Systems

## 8.1 Advantages Over Mechanical Control

Electronic anti-skid systems can react more rapidly and can be adjusted for an aircraft's particular braking requirements through their control circuitry. Compared with a mechanical installation, the textbook attributes an improvement of approximately **15%** to electronic control.

The system can also be **function-tested before use**. Because electronic response characteristics and control biases can be modified, similar principles can be adapted to different aircraft types.

## 8.2 Three Principal Components

|Element|Function|
|-|-|
|**Wheel-speed sensor**|Measures the rotational speed of the wheel.|
|**Electronic control box**|Processes the wheel-speed information and determines when pressure must change.|
|**Servo/modulating valve**|Varies brake hydraulic pressure in response to the control box.|

A simple electronic control loop mainly improves the speed of pressure adjustments. More advanced arrangements include the **Adaptive Pressure Bias Modulation Circuit**.

## 8.3 Adaptive Pressure Bias Modulation

If a wheel is released by the **anti-skid unit (ASU)**, immediately restoring the original high brake pressure could cause the same wheel to skid again. Adaptive pressure bias reduces this risk by applying **less pressure immediately after the ASU release than had been applied immediately beforehand**.

The wheel can regain effective rotation and the system can then adjust pressure toward the best available braking condition, rather than repeatedly returning at once to the pressure that produced the skid.

# 9\. Specific Electronic Anti-Skid Functions

## 9.1 Touchdown Protection

**Touchdown protection** prevents the brakes being applied while the aircraft remains airborne.

The controller examines wheel speed and **air/ground logic**. Before touchdown, the wheels may not yet be spinning and the required signals are absent, so brake application is inhibited. As the wheels contact the runway and **spin up**, the controller receives the necessary indication and allows braking.

## 9.2 Skid Prevention

Each wheel is monitored for an abnormally rapid fall in rotational speed. If the measured deceleration indicates that wheel lock is becoming likely, the controller commands the valve to **reduce pressure at the affected brake**.

## 9.3 Locked-Wheel Protection

A wheel may stop turning after passing over a patch of **water or ice**. When a wheel becomes locked, the system may release pressure at that wheel **fully** until it spins up again, then reapply pressure.

## 9.4 Hydroplaning Protection

Certain systems compare **aircraft speed** with the wheel speeds across an entire **bogie**. If all the braked wheels are hydroplaning and stop rotating, there may be no usable wheel signal with which to restore normal modulation.

The textbook describes a typical solution for a **four-wheel bogie**:

1. If all four wheels lock, release pressure at **two of the wheels**.
2. Leave the other two covered by locked-wheel protection.
3. The released wheels can spin up and resume supplying useful wheel-speed information.
4. Once rotating, they can again receive locked-wheel protection.
5. If hydroplaning continues, the system can release pressure on the **other pair**.

The exact selection method varies from aircraft to aircraft.

## 9.5 Low-Speed Anti-Skid Cut-Out

To give the pilot full braking control for **taxiing and ground manoeuvring**, anti-skid modulation can be disabled manually or automatically when speed falls below approximately **20 mph** in the system example described in the text. At those speeds, the chapter assumes that a serious skid risk has substantially reduced.

This value should not be treated as a universal cut-out speed for all aircraft. The original Figure 6.5 illustrates a specific control sequence with its own speed labels.

# 10\. Typical Aircraft Wheel Brake System

## 10.1 Normal, Alternate and Accumulator Supply

The example brake system normally receives hydraulic power from **System 1**. If its pressure becomes too low, supply is transferred automatically to **System 2**, which provides alternate braking.

If **both normal and alternate hydraulic brake supplies are lost**, an **accumulator** is automatically selected to retain sufficient pressure for the parking brakes, according to the system arrangement described in the chapter.

## 10.2 Normal Brake Anti-Skid Operation

Hydraulic pressure reaches the anti-skid valves from either:

* The **normal brake metering valves**, when the pilot commands braking manually; or
* The **autobrake valves**, when automatic braking is operating.

The **anti-skid control unit** receives rotational-speed data from **wheel-speed transducers in the axles**. It sends electrical commands to the anti-skid valves, which alter the pressure reaching the individual brakes.

In **normal braking**, **each wheel receives individual anti-skid protection**. At the first indication of a skid, the control unit orders the relevant valve to reduce brake pressure.

The system can also compare wheel speed with **ground speed from the IRS (Inertial Reference System)** to provide touchdown-related protection.

When using the **alternate brakes**, the example system provides anti-skid protection to **wheel pairs rather than individual wheels**.

## 10.3 Brake Torque Limiting

Each wheel can have a **brake-torque sensor** to detect braking stresses that could damage the landing gear. This risk is noted as being especially important with **carbon brakes**.

If torque rises above the allowed value, the sensor signals the anti-skid system and brake pressure at the affected wheel is **released or reduced** to protect the gear structure.

# 11\. Autobrakes

## 11.1 Purpose and Availability

**Autobrakes** provide automatically controlled wheel braking during **landing rollout** or a **rejected take-off (RTO)**. In the described aircraft system they are available with the **normal braking system**, not the alternate hydraulic braking system.

The pilot can select a target deceleration rate for landing. The textbook mentions aircraft having **three or five selectable deceleration rates**, depending on type. **Anti-skid protection remains active** while the autobrake system is operating.

## 11.2 Autobrake System States

The chapter distinguishes the following conditions:

|State|Meaning|
|-|-|
|**Off**|Autobraking has not been selected.|
|**Armed**|The system is ready but awaits the necessary conditions before operating automatically.|
|**Activated**|The armed system has met its conditions and has been triggered.|
|**Operative**|The equipment is working properly and performs its intended function.|
|**Inoperative**|The system cannot perform its intended function correctly.|

An aircraft may use different signals to change from **armed** to **activated**; the triggering arrangement is aircraft-specific.

## 11.3 Landing Autobrakes

Before landing, the crew **arms** the system by choosing a required deceleration setting. With the appropriate **ground-mode indication** and **wheel spin-up at touchdown**, braking is applied automatically.

The system then maintains its target braking performance, and can continue toward a full stop unless the crew disarms it earlier. The selected landing deceleration can be **changed during operation** by moving the selector, without necessarily disarming the autobrakes.

## 11.4 Rejected Take-Off Autobrakes

When **RTO** is selected in the textbook's system example, closing **all thrust levers** while aircraft groundspeed is **above 85 knots** triggers **maximum automatic brake pressure**.

At **less than 85 knots**, the RTO autobrakes **do not activate** in that described arrangement.

These are values for the particular system used as the textbook's illustration, rather than rules for every transport aircraft.

## 11.5 Faults and Automatic Disarming

If a fault occurs while the landing autobrake system is armed, the system can **disarm immediately**, move the selector to **DISARM**, and show a **warning caption**.

The chapter also identifies these crew actions as causes of disarming during autobrake operation:

1. **Applying manual wheel braking**.
2. **Advancing any thrust lever after landing**.
3. **Moving the speed-brake lever to the DN (down) detent** after the speed brakes have deployed on the ground.
4. **Moving the autobrake selector to DISARM or OFF**.

Under the procedure described, the **non-handling pilot or flight engineer** normally disarms the autobrakes as speed reduces to approximately **20 knots**.

# 12\. Parking Brake

The parking brake retains braking pressure after the crew stops pressing the pedals.

In the illustrated arrangement, the parking brake handle operates a **shut-off valve in the hydraulic return line between the anti-skid valves and reservoir**.

The application sequence described is:

1. Press the **brake pedals** to apply hydraulic pressure.
2. Move the **parking-brake lever** to the applied position.
3. Release the brake pedals.

Because the return path has been closed, **hydraulic pressure is trapped in the brakes**. This arrangement is described as capable of holding the brakes applied during **overnight parking**, when necessary.

# 13\. Automatic Brake Application During Undercarriage Retraction

As the landing gear retracts, the system applies **reduced hydraulic brake pressure** to stop the wheels spinning before they are enclosed in the wheel wells.

Stopping rotation avoids the undesirable **gyroscopic forces** that would otherwise be produced by rotating wheels during gear movement.

# 14\. Brake Kinetic Energy and Heat Management

## 14.1 Why Brake Energy Must Be Assessed

Every braking event transfers a substantial amount of the aircraft's **kinetic energy** into the brakes as **heat**. The brake pack, wheel, and tyre assembly can only withstand a limited quantity of thermal energy before damage or failure becomes possible.

An assessment after a **landing**, **rejected take-off**, or even **ground movement** helps determine whether special inspections, cooling periods, fire precautions, or other actions are needed.

## 14.2 Using the Brake Kinetic Energy Graph

The chapter illustrates a **brake kinetic energy graph** (Figure 6.6), which estimates brake-energy absorption for an aircraft and classifies the result into operating zones.

The main inputs are:

* **Aircraft all-up weight** or relevant landing/rejected-take-off weight.
* **Speed when braking begins** (brake application speed).
* **Headwind or tailwind** component.
* **Number of serviceable engine thrust reversers**.
* **Airfield altitude**.

The graph also provides correction notes for particular combinations of **ambient temperature**, **landing configuration**, **rejected take-off**, and other special conditions.

The resulting figure is expressed in the source as **brake kinetic energy (KE)**, with a chart scale labelled **KE per brake**. The practical decision is made by finding which of the chart's **normal, caution, or danger zones** contains the result.

## 14.3 Cooling and Safety Zones

Figure 6.7 reproduces an example of the actions specified in an aircraft operations manual. The zone bands and instructions below are **source-specific examples**; they are not universal brake-cooling or fire-response procedures.

### Danger Zone — Above 29 KE

When the chart indicates **more than 29 KE**, the source manual calls for the following:

1. Vacate the runway as soon as practical and **alert fire services**.
2. Use only the **minimum required foot-brake pressure**; the tyres are expected probably to deflate.
3. Avoid the **parking brake** unless its use is essential.
4. Shut down engines that are not needed.
5. If tyres remain inflated, approach the wheel/tyre assembly only **with great caution from directly in front of or behind it**.
6. Unless the brake/wheel is burning, let the brakes cool **without applying extinguishing agent**.
7. If a brake/wheel assembly is on fire, apply **dry powder extinguishant** and withdraw from the area for **at least 15 minutes**.
8. Allow **2–3 hours of cooling** unless cooling air is used, and **replace the wheels and tyres** as directed by the cited manual.

### Caution Zone — 17 to 29 KE

For **17–29 KE**, the source checklist specifies:

1. Park the aircraft **without setting the parking brake**.
2. Do not approach the wheel assembly for **at least 30 minutes**.
3. Before the next take-off, inspect the brake/wheel assembly for damage and apply brake pressure to check for **brake-seal leakage**.
4. Operate the brakes and confirm that brake pressure is **maintained**.
5. Allow **5 minutes of cooling for every 1.0 KE above 5.0 KE**.

### Normal Zone — 5 to 17 KE

For **5–17 KE**, the source requires **5 minutes of cooling time for every 1.0 KE above 5.0 KE**.

### Below 5 KE

The source indicates **no brake-cooling delay is needed** and **no special instructions** apply.

# 15\. Brake Temperature Indicators

## 15.1 Where the Indication System Is Used

Large transport aircraft, including types such as the **B747, B777, A340 and A380**, may be fitted with **brake temperature indication systems**.

Sensors measure the brake temperature at **each individual wheel**. Depending on the installed cockpit panel, the crew can select the relevant wheel pair to display its readings.

## 15.2 Warning Levels

The system continuously watches brake temperatures and produces escalating indications:

1. If a brake exceeds a predetermined temperature, a **“HIGH TEMP”** indication illuminates.
2. Crew-operated selectors can identify the **specific wheel brake or brakes** responsible for the warning.
3. If the temperature reaches a higher threshold, an **“OVERHEAT”** caption illuminates.
4. The overheat condition is also repeated through the **Central Warning System**.

No universal temperature trigger is quoted for these warnings in the source text; the values depend on the installed system.

# 16\. Wing Growth During Ground Manoeuvring

## 16.1 Meaning of Wing Growth

**Wing growth** is a ground-manoeuvring clearance hazard associated with **swept-wing aircraft**. It does not mean that a wing physically lengthens; it describes the **larger-than-expected path traced by the outer wingtip as the aircraft turns**.

A large jet's effective **turning-circle centre** may be farther outboard than the **inboard oleo/undercarriage strut**. Together with the **swept-wing planform**, this makes the outer wingtip follow a wider circular arc than a pilot may initially expect.

Aircraft fitted with **body-gear steering** may experience a less severe clearance problem, depending on their arrangement.

## 16.2 Operational Significance

The outer wingtip can move closer to buildings, vehicles, other parked aircraft or other obstructions than its apparent starting position suggests. Therefore, **wide swept-wing aircraft require particular care when turning close to obstacles**.
