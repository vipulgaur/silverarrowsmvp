# 1\. Definitions: Loads Applied to Aircraft Structures

Aircraft structures experience forces that may pull, push or displace their components. Three fundamental forms of loading are **tension, compression and shear**. These basic loads also appear together in more complex structural conditions.

## 1.1 Tension

**Tension**, also called a **tensile load**, is a force that attempts to **stretch or lengthen a structural member**. A component designed specifically to withstand a tensile force is called a **tie**.

# 2\. Combination Loadings

Loads rarely act on an aircraft structure in isolation. A bending or twisting action can generate several types of stress in different parts of the same component.

## 2.1 Bending

**Bending** combines the three basic forms of loading:

* **Tension:** The material at the **outer edge** of the bend stretches.
* **Compression:** The material at the **inner edge** of the bend is squeezed together.
* **Shear:** Forces act **across the structure**, tending to split or slide portions relative to each other.

A bending member must therefore withstand these interacting effects rather than tension or compression alone.

## 2.2 Torsion

**Torsion** means twisting of a structure. According to the textbook, a torsional load produces:

* **Tension** toward the **outer edge**.
* **Compression** toward the **centre**.
* **Shear** across the structure.

Twisting forces are especially important when later considering how aircraft wings and other load-bearing structures are designed.

## 2.3 Stress

**Stress** is the **internal force per unit area** developed within a structural component because of externally applied loads.

* A **tensile force** creates **tensile stress**.
* A **compressive force** creates **compressive stress**.

Stress is defined as **force divided by area**. Units given in the textbook are **N/mm²** or **MN/m²** (both describe the same stress magnitude when their numeric values are equal).

## 2.4 Strain

When a sufficiently large external force acts on a structure, its dimensions change. The resulting **deformation** is known as **strain**.

Strain describes the dimensional change caused by stress and is commonly expressed as the **change in dimension as a percentage of the original dimension**. Thus, stress describes the internal loading, while strain describes the accompanying deformation.

For an **elastic material**, the relationship between stress and strain is generally characterized by a constant called **Young’s Modulus of Elasticity**.

## 2.5 Buckling

**Buckling** occurs when a structural member becomes unstable under compressive loading. The textbook specifically identifies:

* **Thin sheet materials** exposed to end loads.
* **Ties** subjected to **compressive forces**, rather than their intended tensile loading.

Because aircraft components experience multiple loads, they may **elongate, compress, bend, shear or twist** as the forces act upon them.

## 2.6 Elastic and Plastic Deformation

The response to loading depends on whether the material remains within its **elastic limit**:

* **Within the elastic limit:** The material deforms under load but **returns to its original dimensions** once the force is removed.
* **Beyond the elastic limit:** The material does not fully return to its original dimensions. The resulting **permanent deformation** is called **plastic deformation**.

This distinction is essential when deciding whether a loaded component can recover without permanent structural damage.

## 2.7 Dynamic and Static Loads

### Dynamic loads

**Dynamic loads** build up rapidly, particularly when flight conditions change. They are produced during **aircraft manoeuvres** and may cause additional loading of other aircraft components. The resulting loads can be **quite severe**.

### Static loads

**Static loads** are generally steady or increase slowly. An aircraft standing on the ground experiences static loading: its **weight acts downward**, while an **opposing upward force from the landing gear** must be carried by the wing/aircraft structure.

### Quick comparison

|Characteristic|Dynamic loading|Static loading|
|-|-|-|
|Typical development|Builds quickly|Generally steady or slow to build|
|Typical situation|Changes in flight condition; manoeuvring|Aircraft standing on the ground|
|Structural consideration|May induce severe additional loads elsewhere|Must support the aircraft's weight and reactions through the structure|

# 3\. Forces Acting on the Aircraft Structure

Structural loading occurs **both on the ground and in flight**. The type, location and magnitude of the force depend on the aircraft's operating condition.

## 3.1 Loads During Flight

During flight, **lift** acts on the wings and tends to **bend them upward**. As a result:

* The **upper wing surface** experiences **compression**.
* The **lower wing surface** experiences **tension**.
* Lift also creates **torsional (twisting) loading** in the wing.

**Drag** acts on exposed aircraft components such as the **landing gear**, tending to bend them **backward**. At the same time, the aircraft's **mass/weight** produces a downward load.

An aircraft maintaining **straight-and-level flight at constant speed** is described in the textbook as subject to **1 g**. Changes of aircraft attitude and manoeuvring alter the **g-loading** and therefore change the effective loading experienced by the structure.

## 3.2 Effect of Engine Failure on Structural Loads

An engine failure changes the aircraft's loading pattern, especially in a **twin-engine aircraft**:

1. The **operating engine** continues to produce thrust on one side of the aircraft.
2. This **asymmetric thrust** creates a **yawing moment**.
3. The pilot counters the yaw by applying **opposite rudder**.
4. These actions and changed force conditions produce additional loading in the **fin**, **fuselage** and wings.

The point is that a propulsion failure affects not just thrust and direction, but also the loads transmitted through the airframe.

## 3.3 Design Limit Load (DLL)

The **Design Limit Load (DLL)** is the **maximum load the designer expects an airframe or individual component to encounter during service**.

The textbook provides the following standard limit-load values, expressed as multiples of **g**:

|Aircraft category|Design Limit Load stated in the textbook|
|-|-|
|Transport aircraft|**+2.5 g and −1.0 g**|
|Utility aircraft|**4.4 g**|
|Aerobatic aircraft|**6 g**|

The source explains that these values are based on **g-forces** and developed using **experimental failure values established during design**. The latter two values are listed without a separate negative limit in this passage.

## 3.4 Design Ultimate Load (DUL)

The **Design Ultimate Load (DUL)** is calculated by multiplying the **Design Limit Load** by a **safety factor**.

\[
\\boxed{\\text{DUL}=\\text{DLL}\\times\\text{Safety Factor}}
]

The **minimum safety factor** specified in the textbook's design requirements is **1.5**. The structure must be capable of **withstanding the Design Ultimate Load without collapse**.

## 3.5 Safety Factor

The **safety factor (SF)** expresses the ratio between the ultimate load and the limit load:

\[
\\boxed{\\text{SF}=\\frac{\\text{DUL}}{\\text{DLL}}}
]

These three concepts must be kept distinct:

* **DLL:** Expected maximum in-service loading.
* **DUL:** Limit loading multiplied by the safety factor.
* **SF:** Ratio between ultimate and limit loading.

### Worked illustration using the textbook's stated figures

For the **+2.5 g transport-category DLL** and a **1.5 safety factor**:

\[
\\text{DUL}=2.5\\times1.5=3.75,g.
]

This calculation illustrates the formula; it does not replace any aircraft-specific certification limit.

# 4\. Design Philosophies

Aircraft manufacturers aim to build structures capable of handling the range of loading encountered during flight. **Design guidelines, mathematical formulae and accumulated experience** inform the choice of structures that can remain safe despite fatigue or localized damage.

The chapter introduces **safe-life**, **fail-safe** and **damage-tolerant** approaches.

## 4.1 Safe Life

**Safe life** is the **minimum established period of structural service during which catastrophic damage is not expected to occur**.

The usable life of a component or assembly may be recorded using several different measures:

* **Flying hours**.
* **Landing cycles**.
* **Pressurization events/cycles**.
* **Acceleration events**.
* **Calendar time**.

When the permitted **life count** or **fatigue-cycle count** is reached, the item is **replaced or overhauled**. Pressurization events and landings are common examples of the cyclic events counted.

During the aircraft's operational life, designers also use **fail-safe construction** or **damage tolerance** to reduce the risk of failure due to fatigue.

## 4.2 Fail-safe or Damage Tolerant Structure

Large modern aircraft employ designs described as **fail-safe** or **damage tolerant**. A fail-safe structure has an **alternative load path**: if one structural part breaks or develops a crack, an **adjacent component** can carry the loads for a limited period.

The aim is that, following **one structural-member failure or crack**, the remaining structure can still support **normal operating loads until the next scheduled inspection**.

Examples of **duplicated load paths** given in the textbook include:

* **Wing attachment structures**.
* **Vertical stabilizer attachment points**.
* **Horizontal stabilizer attachment points**.

### Inspection and fault detection

Fail-safe design depends on a **planned inspection programme** capable of discovering damage before it becomes critical.

* Some vulnerable areas require **partial dismantling** for access.
* **Non-destructive testing (NDT)** can be used in **less critical areas**, as described in the textbook.
* **True duplication** of load paths has a significant drawback: it is **structurally heavy**.

Modern aircraft often use **stressed-skin** or **semi-monocoque construction**. In these arrangements, multiple parts of the airframe contribute to **distributing loads**, so the structure can tolerate a certain amount of damage.

Inspection intervals are chosen on the assumption that **even if a detectable crack is missed during one inspection**, the structure will allow enough time for it to grow and still be found at a **later inspection before becoming critical**.

The **inspection-cycle criteria**, **Design Limit Loads** and **Design Ultimate Loads** are agreed during the aircraft's **certification** process.

## 4.3 Damage Tolerant Structure

The textbook contrasts heavily duplicated fail-safe members with a **damage-tolerant structure**, which spreads loading from a particular location **over a greater structural area** instead of relying on additional duplicated members.

The structure is arranged so that **damage can be detected during normal scheduled inspections before it leads to failure**. This reduces the weight penalty associated with extensive fail-safe duplication.

## 4.4 Fatigue

**Fatigue** occurs when a structure experiences **repeated or cyclic loading**, including repeated **reversals of load**. A structure can eventually fail under a load **lower than the load that would cause failure if applied steadily**.

The number of load reversals matters: reducing the applied stress may greatly increase the number of cycles the material withstands before failure.

The textbook's example states that:

* At **80% of ultimate stress**, a specimen is expected to fail after approximately **100 applications**.
* At **20% of ultimate stress**, failure is not expected until about **10 million applications**.

These illustrative figures show why repeated stresses can govern structural life even when each individual application is below the ultimate stress.

# 5\. Stress Concentration Factor

A **stress concentration** is a region of a component in which stress is **concentrated into a relatively small area**.

* A component is strongest when a force is **distributed evenly** across the available area.
* If the effective area is **reduced**, stress becomes **locally higher**.
* A **crack** is one cause of a reduced effective area and concentrated stress.
* A material can fail when a crack **propagates**, or grows through it.

Many materials naturally contain tiny **cracks, inclusions or contaminants** that act as stress concentrators. **Fatigue cracks can begin at these points**, so removing defects can **improve fatigue strength**.

# 6\. Station Numbers

Aircraft maintenance and repairs require a **consistent method for identifying the position of components**. Manufacturers use **reference lines, datum positions and station numbers** to locate parts of the fuselage, wings, empennage and other structures.

## 6.1 Fuselage Stations

The fuselage reference system begins from a manufacturer-defined **zero datum** located **at or near the forward part of the aircraft**, designated **fuselage station 0.00**.

The textbook expresses fuselage locations in **inches** relative to that datum:

* **Forward of the zero datum:** **Negative (−)** station measurement.
* **Aft of the zero datum:** **Positive (+)** station measurement.

The manufacturer determines the precise datum location.

## 6.2 Wing Stations

**Wing stations** are measured **outward from the aircraft centreline**, with distances expressed in **inches** to the **left or right** of that centreline.

## 6.3 Water Lines (WL)

Vertical positions can be described by **Water Lines (WL)**. These are measured as a distance in **inches above or relative to a ground/horizontal datum**.

The reference systems together allow maintenance personnel to locate aircraft parts using consistent **longitudinal, lateral and vertical** positions.

# 7\. Aircraft Structures

## 7.1 Fuselage

The **fuselage** is the aircraft's **main body and central structure**. Its functions include:

* Carrying the **payload**, including **passengers and/or freight**.
* Accommodating **flight crew and cabin staff** in safe and comfortable conditions.
* Providing the flight crew with a practical location from which to **operate the aircraft**.
* Housing the **controls, accessories and other equipment** needed for operation.
* Transferring structural loads **to and from the wings (mainplanes), tailplanes, fin, landing gear** and, for some configurations, the **engines/powerplants**.

The fuselage is therefore a load-carrying part of the complete airframe, not simply an enclosure for people and cargo.

## 7.2 Pressurized Aircraft

A pressurized fuselage must support additional forces created by the **internal pressure**. The textbook identifies two principal forms: **axial stress** and **hoop stress**.

### Axial Stress

**Axial**, or **longitudinal**, stress acts **along the fuselage length** and tends to **elongate the fuselage** when it is pressurized.

### Hoop Stress

**Hoop**, described in the textbook as **radial**, stress acts so as to **expand the fuselage cross-sectional area**. It is present in addition to axial stress.

The internal pressures producing these stresses may be as high as **65.5 kN/m² (9.5 psi)** in the example given.

# 8\. Fuselage Design

A fuselage can have several different **cross-sectional shapes**, each with particular advantages and disadvantages in construction, strength, weight, drag or space use.

## 8.1 Rectangular Cross-section

**Rectangular fuselages** are often used in **non-pressurized aircraft** because of **cost constraints**. They are relatively **easy to construct**, but have a **high weight-to-strength ratio**.

## 8.2 Circular Cross-section

A **circular fuselage** is particularly suitable for **pressurized aircraft** because **hoop stresses are distributed evenly through its structure**.

Additional advantages include:

* **Cheaper tooling**.
* **Relatively simple construction**.

Its disadvantage is that **substantial internal space may be wasted**, depending on how passengers or cargo need to be accommodated.

## 8.3 Oval Cross-section

An **oval** form is **less structurally efficient** than a circular shape. Nevertheless, it is commonly used to form the **pressure hull behind the rear bulkhead**.

## 8.4 Double Bubble Cross-section

A **double-bubble fuselage** resembles a **figure eight**. The design offers efficient accommodation of **passengers and cargo**, while avoiding the additional **drag** associated with a very large circular fuselage. It can also be **cost-effective**.

The textbook also describes newer **side-by-side bubble arrangements**:

* They can carry a **larger number of passengers for a given structural weight**.
* They are described as **efficient because of reduced drag**.
* The configuration discussed uses **rear-mounted engines**.

# 10\. Framework Construction

A **framework**, or **truss-type fuselage**, is made from **light-gauge steel tubes welded into a three-dimensional space frame**.

**Triangular structural units** are used because they provide a particularly rigid geometric arrangement. Each tube carries a particular load, and the magnitude of its load depends on whether the aircraft is **in flight or on the ground**.

Advantages of this basic structure include:

* **High strength**.
* **Ease of construction**.
* **Relatively trouble-free service**.

The framework is covered by a **lightweight aluminium-alloy skin or fabric covering** to form an **enclosed, aerodynamically efficient load-carrying compartment**.

# 11\. Monocoque Construction

In **monocoque construction**, the **outer skin carries the structural loads**. Only **light internal frames or formers** are provided to maintain the required fuselage shape.

The main limitations are:

* **Even minor skin damage** can seriously weaken the entire structure.
* Openings for **windows, doors and landing gear** weaken the shell and require **additional reinforcement**.
* The textbook therefore identifies pure monocoque construction as suitable mainly for **smaller aircraft**.

# 12\. Semi-monocoque Construction

As aircraft became larger and **aerodynamic loads increased**, a **pure monocoque shell** became inadequate. The structure was strengthened by adding **longitudinal load-bearing members**:

* **Stringers**, also described as **stiffeners**.
* **Longerons**.

These run **lengthwise along the fuselage** and connect its **frames**. The lightweight **aluminium-alloy skin** is attached to the frames and stringers using **rivets or adhesive bonding**.

**Stringers stiffen the skin** and help sheet metal carry **loads acting along its length**. The textbook gives **passenger-aircraft seat rails** as an example of longerons.

The arrangement is widely known as **semi-monocoque** or **stressed-skin construction** because the skin and internal framework work together to carry structural loads.

# 13\. Main Fuselage Structural Members

**Longerons** are longitudinal beams running through the fuselage **from nose to tail**. They are often positioned **under the floor** and carry significant **airframe bending loads**.

# 14\. Diagnostic Self-Test Questions — Part 1

These revision questions are based on the topics covered in **book pages 3–17** and follow the self-test style of the earlier Chapter 19 notes.

### Question 1

**What is the difference between tension and compression, and what are the corresponding load-resisting members called?**

**Answer:** **Tension** stretches/lengthens a component and is resisted by a **tie**; **compression** shortens a component and is resisted by a **strut**.

### Question 2

**Which three basic loads can occur together when a structure is bent?**

**Answer:** **Tension** at the outside of the bend, **compression** at the inside and **shear** acting across the member.

### Question 3

**How do stress and strain differ?**

**Answer:** **Stress** is force per unit area inside a material due to loading; **strain** is the resulting dimensional deformation, often expressed as a percentage of the original dimension.

### Question 4

**What is the difference between elastic and plastic deformation?**

**Answer:** Deformation within the **elastic limit** reverses when the force is removed. Deformation beyond that limit is **permanent (plastic)**.

### Question 5

**Why are the upper and lower wing surfaces loaded differently by lift?**

**Answer:** The wing bends upward, producing **compression in the upper surface** and **tension in the lower surface**.

### Question 6

**Define DLL, DUL and the safety factor.**

**Answer:** The **DLL** is the expected maximum in-service load. The **DUL** equals DLL multiplied by the **safety factor**, with **1.5** stated as the minimum design safety factor. **SF = DUL/DLL**.

### Question 7

**What is the essential principle of a fail-safe structure?**

**Answer:** If one load-carrying member cracks or fails, an **alternative path through an adjacent structure** can carry normal loads for a limited period, allowing damage to be found at inspection.

### Question 8

**What does the textbook's fatigue example show?**

**Answer:** Fatigue life increases greatly as alternating stress decreases: **80%** of ultimate stress corresponds to about **100 cycles**, compared with **20%** and about **10 million cycles** in the illustrative example.

### Question 9

**How are fuselage stations, wing stations and water lines referenced?**

**Answer:** Fuselage positions are given fore/aft of manufacturer-defined **station 0.00**; wing stations are measured left/right of the **aircraft centreline**; **water lines** specify vertical position relative to a datum. The source uses **inches**.

### Question 10

**Which two stresses arise in a pressurized fuselage?**

**Answer:** **Axial stress**, which tends to lengthen the fuselage, and **hoop stress**, which tends to enlarge its cross-section.

### Question 11

**How do monocoque and semi-monocoque construction differ?**

**Answer:** In **monocoque construction**, the skin carries the loads with light formers providing shape; in **semi-monocoque construction**, skin, frames, stringers and longerons share structural duties.

### Question 12

**What are longerons, frames and bulkheads used for?**

**Answer:** **Longerons** run along the fuselage and resist major bending loads; **frames** provide shape and carry loads; **bulkheads** are usually solid frame-like members that carry loads and can separate pressurized from unpressurized sections.

### Question 13

**Why might blow-out panels be fitted in a cabin floor?**

**Answer:** To **equalize pressure across the floor** during rapid decompression, reducing the risk of floor distortion.

### Question 14

**What is a doubler, and where is it used?**

**Answer:** A **doubler** is a reinforcing plate fitted around an opening or repair in stressed-skin structure to compensate for the weakening caused by a cut-out.

# 2\. Flight Deck and Passenger Cabin Windows



## 2.1 Flight Deck Windows



The windows of a **pressurized aircraft's flight deck** must withstand two important loading conditions:

* **Pressurization loads:** The difference between the internal cabin pressure and the outside atmosphere.
* **Bird-strike impact loads:** Sudden forces imposed if a bird hits the windscreen while the aircraft is moving at speed.

The textbook describes flight deck windscreens as **toughened-glass panels** bonded on either side of a **clear vinyl interlayer**. This layered arrangement provides both strength and impact resistance.

### Electrical Heating and Bird-Strike Resistance

An **electrically conducting coating** on the inside of the **outer glass panel** provides windscreen heating. This serves two purposes in the textbook's description:

1. **Prevents ice forming** on the window.
2. **Makes the window more resilient**, improving its ability to withstand a bird strike.

If a sufficiently severe bird strike shatters a glass panel, the **vinyl interlayer can stretch and deform**. Its ability to deform absorbs part of the shock loading and helps the layered screen resist penetration.

The windscreen is attached to the aircraft structure by **bolts passing through the edges of the windscreen** into its supporting frame.

### Required Bird-Strike Performance

The book specifies that the aircraft, including its windscreen, must remain capable of **continued safe flight and landing** after impact by a **4 lb (2 kg) bird**, under whichever of the following flight conditions is more critical:

* **At sea level:** Aircraft velocity equal to **(V\_C)**, the **design cruise speed**.
* **At 8,000 ft:** Aircraft velocity equal to **(0.85V\_C)**.

Under the applicable critical condition, the windscreen must withstand the strike **without penetration**.

# 3\. Aircraft Doors



## 3.1 Passenger Doors

Aircraft doors may open from the **side or top**. The textbook describes passenger doors on pressurized aircraft as **plug-type doors**.

In the plug-door principle, **internal cabin pressure helps hold the door shut** when it is closed. **Locking pins engage with the surrounding structure**, helping ensure the door cannot open in flight.

### Opening and Safety Features

The description of a traditional plug-door opening movement is:

1. The door is **pulled inward**.
2. It is then **rotated sideways** to open.

The text also notes that some doors **open outward** to improve access.

A passenger door on a pressurized aircraft must:

* Resist the **loads due to cabin pressurization**.
* Include an arrangement that **prevents pressurization when the door is unlocked**.
* Be **easy to open during an emergency**.
* Generally incorporate an **escape slide** as part of the door arrangement.
* Have a **visual inspection panel**.

Doors on **unpressurized aircraft** can be of **lighter construction**, because they do not have to carry the same pressurization loads.

## 3.2 Freight Doors

Some aircraft have cargo or **freight doors** in the side of the fuselage. The textbook describes these doors as generally:

* **Hinged at the top**, so they swing upward.
* Operated by an **electric motor** or a **hydraulic power pack**.
* Arranged so that structural **door loads are transmitted through the hinges**.

* **Wing spars**.
* **Interplane struts** connecting the wings.
* **Bracing wires**.

# 5\. Flutter and Resonance



## 5.1 Flutter

**Flutter** is an **uncontrolled oscillation** that can affect **fixed surfaces**, such as the wing, or **control surfaces**, including **ailerons and elevators**.

It results from the interaction of three factors:

1. **Aerodynamic forces**.
2. **Inertia forces**.
3. The **elastic properties** of the surface or structure.

If flutter becomes severe, the structure may suffer **catastrophic failure**.

## 5.2 How Resonance Can Develop

Aircraft wings have considerable flexibility. When the aircraft is on the ground, a wing may move vertically even under a push by hand. In cruise, by contrast, lift bends the wings **upward**.

If a **gust** disturbs the aircraft, the wings may oscillate up and down at a particular **frequency**. When this vibration approaches a **natural frequency** of the wing structure, **resonance** can occur. Resonance increases the oscillation amplitude, potentially intensifying flutter until the structure fails.

## 5.3 Preventing Flutter

**Flutter must not occur within the aircraft's normal flight operating envelope.** The textbook identifies the following measures and risk factors:

* **Control-surface mass balancing:** Changes the **moment of inertia** and the surface's period of vibration by bringing its **centre of gravity (C of G)** closer to the hinge.
* **Minimizing backlash or play:** Excessive clearance, wear or flexibility in a poorly maintained control system may permit flutter at speeds **below the normal limit airspeed**.
* **Wing-mounted engines as mass balances:** Engines on pylons **forward of the wing leading edge** may help prevent flutter of the mainplanes.

**Key distinction:** **Flutter** is the potentially destructive oscillation; **resonance** is the amplification that occurs when the excitation and structural vibration frequencies are similar.

# 7\. Materials Used



## 7.1 Selection of Aircraft Construction Materials

The textbook describes **aluminium and aluminium alloys** as the principal materials in many aircraft structures, together with smaller quantities of **steel and titanium** for major structural uses. **Composite materials** are used extensively for lighter-loaded components and increasingly for more substantial areas of modern aircraft.

Examples of components for which modern composites are used include:

* **Empennage**.
* **Cabin floor panels**.
* **Flying-control surfaces**.
* **Engine cowlings**.
* **Fairings**.

Materials are chosen according to properties such as **fatigue strength, wear resistance, strength-to-weight ratio and fire resistance**.

## 7.2 Aluminium Alloys and Duralumin



**Aluminium alloys** are widely used because of their useful **strength-to-weight ratio**.

The textbook particularly mentions **duralumin-type alloys**, which contain **copper** and offer good **fatigue resistance**. Their characteristics include:

* **Good strength relative to weight**.
* **Good fatigue resistance**.
* **Poor corrosion resistance** unless clad in **pure aluminium**, a protective method referred to as **Alclad**.
* **Good electrical and thermal conductivity**.
* **Difficulty with welding**.

## 7.3 Steel and Steel Alloys

**Steel** is used where **high strength is essential** and the extra **weight penalty** can be accepted.

## 7.4 Titanium

**Titanium** is lighter than steel and is useful where **fire protection** is required, particularly for **firewalls**.

The book states that it retains **strength** and **corrosion resistance** at temperatures **up to 400°C**.

## 7.5 Magnesium Alloys

The main advantage of **magnesium** is its low weight. The textbook notes that **aluminium is approximately one and a half times heavier** than magnesium, giving magnesium alloys a favourable **strength-to-weight ratio**.

Their **elastic properties are less satisfactory**, however, and this restricts their use in **primary aircraft structures**.

## 7.6 Composite Materials



A **composite** combines at least **two different constituent materials** to achieve properties different from those of the individual materials.

Most composites have two principal components:

1. **Matrix:** The bulk material that holds the structure together.
2. **Reinforcement:** Usually fibres added primarily to improve **strength and stiffness**.

### The Matrix

The matrix may be made from **epoxy** or **polyester resins**. On their own, these materials have relatively poor mechanical properties compared with metals, including comparatively poor **compressive/tensile properties, flexibility or hardness**. Their major benefit is that they can be **formed easily into complex shapes**.

### Reinforcing Fibres

The source lists **glass fibres, carbon fibres and Kevlar (aramid) fibres** as possible reinforcement materials. Together with a matrix, these fibres allow the composite to develop excellent structural properties.

The matrix:

* **Spreads applied loads among individual reinforcing fibres**.
* **Protects those fibres** from **impact and abrasion damage**.

### Corrosion, Fatigue and Electrical Properties

The textbook describes composites as having **good corrosion resistance**, but their fatigue behaviour differs from that of metallic structures.

* For the composites described, fatigue is **not generally considered significant at stresses below about 80% of ultimate stress**.
* **Metal structures** exposed to fatigue may retain their design strength until a critical stage, then fail **rapidly**.
* **Composites** are described as losing their structural properties **more gradually**.

Many composites are **poor electrical conductors**. **Special coatings** may therefore be applied to provide the required **electrostatic discharge** and **lightning-strike protection**.

### Engineering Benefits and Costs

Interest in composites continues because they offer:

* High **specific strength**.
* High **specific stiffness**.
* Retention of useful properties at **elevated temperatures**.
* The ability to **tailor strength to the direction of expected loading**.

Manufacture can be **labour-intensive, complex and expensive**, so **production costs may be high**. The textbook explains that lower **operating costs** can compensate for these disadvantages.

As an example, it describes aircraft such as the **Boeing Dreamliner** as being **approximately 20% lighter** in the relevant comparison, contributing to a substantial reduction in **fuel consumption**. This is retained as the textbook's stated example rather than a new independently verified specification.

## 7.7 Sandwich Construction



**Sandwich construction** is widely used on different types of aircraft, including:

* **Flight-control surfaces**.
* **Floor panels**.
* **Fuselage panels**.
* **Empennage skin**.
* **Engine soundproofing**.

The arrangement provides:

* Good **rigidity and strength**.
* A favourable **strength-to-weight ratio**.
* Particularly good strength in the **direction of the honeycomb openings**.

Where components must carry **concentrated loads**, **additional structural provision** is needed.

# 8\. Attachment Methods



Aircraft materials may be joined using a variety of methods. The five common methods listed are:

1. **Riveting**.
2. **Welding**.
3. **Bolting**.
4. **Pinning**.

**Riveting** is described as the most common traditional method of joining aircraft materials.

The method involves:

1. Making a **pre-drilled hole** through the materials.
2. Inserting a **rivet**.

**Welding** joins two metallic components by **fusing them together** to act as one.

* **Electric-arc welding**.
* **Spot welding**.

**Bolting** is used where **large shear or tensile loads** have to be carried. Most examples use **steel bolts**, which must be prevented from loosening during service.

Typical locking methods identified in the text include:

* **Locking wire**.
* **Split pins**.
* **Special locking nuts**.

## 8.4 Pinning



**Pinning** uses pins of different designs to hold materials or components together.

## 8.5 Adhesive Bonding



The textbook names **Redux bonding** as a common adhesive-bonding technique.

A **sheet of adhesive** is positioned between the materials and **heat is applied** to cure the adhesive, forming a strong bond.

# 9\. Corrosion



## 9.1 Introduction: What Is Corrosion?



**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of metal through **electrochemical action**. The source refers to this process as **electrolytic corrosion** and notes the continual research aimed at improving methods of corrosion prevention. Despite such work, corrosion remains a major problem in aviation structures.

## 9.2 Why Metals Corrode



Most metals are **chemically unstable** compared with the compounds from which their ores originate. Corrosion is described as the tendency for the metal to return to a more stable form.

The original metal can be converted into compounds such as:

* **Oxides**.
* **Hydroxides**.
* **Carbonates**.
* **Sulphates**.
* Other **salts**.

Corrosion is largely **electrochemical**. It can develop when **small electrical/electrolytic cells** form within or on a metal surface in the presence of an **electrolyte**.

Potential differences may occur:

* Between different constituents of the **same alloy**.
* Where **dissimilar metals touch one another**.

## 9.3 Oxidation and Protective Oxide Films



When metal is exposed to air, atmospheric **oxygen** reacts with the bare surface to form a layer of **metal oxide**.

An oxide film that **adheres closely** can act as a **barrier**, separating the metal from the surrounding atmosphere and limiting further oxidation. For certain metals, this provides sufficient surface protection.

For other metals, the oxide may:

* **React chemically** with the environment.
* Combine with **water** into a less protective surface film.
* **Allow additional oxygen to pass through**.
* **Crack or flake away**, exposing fresh metal.
* **Volatilize at elevated temperatures**.

When this happens, the metal is exposed to renewed oxidation and continued deterioration.

## 9.4 Water, Contamination and Electrolytic Corrosion



Apart from the separate process of oxidation, the book describes corrosion as generally occurring when the metal contacts **water**, either as a **liquid** or atmospheric **moisture**.

Corrosion may develop in different patterns:

* Spread over a **large area of a surface**.
* Concentrated in **local deep pits**.
* Along **grain boundaries inside the material**.

Weakening can be made worse by stresses from:

* **External loads** on the aircraft structure.
* **Residual stresses** introduced during **manufacture**.
* Stresses resulting from the **assembly method**.

## 9.7 Surface Corrosion



**Surface corrosion** is a relatively **uniform external attack** that gradually decreases the **cross-sectional thickness** of a structural member, thereby reducing its strength.

Common signs are **surface etching** or **pitting**. The appearance of the corrosion products depends on the metal:

### Steel

Most **ferrous metals**, apart from **stainless steel**, develop a **reddish-brown powder** commonly called **rust**.

### Aluminium and Magnesium

These metals may develop **powdery white-to-grey deposits**. Magnesium corrosion may also be:

* **Deeply pitted**.
* **Fluffy**.
* **Granular**.

### Copper Alloys

A common corrosion product on **copper alloys** is a **blue-green salt deposit**.

Surface corrosion is regarded by the book as the **least damaging form** because it normally leaves **visible evidence** that allows detection and repair **at an early stage**.

## 9.8 Intergranular (Inter-crystalline) Corrosion



**Intergranular corrosion** attacks the **interior of a metal along its grain boundaries** rather than appearing simply as widespread damage on the exposed surface.

The textbook explains that **grain-boundary material is commonly anodic relative to grain centres**, which makes corrosion concentrate along the boundaries.

Characteristics and consequences include:

1. The attack can spread **through the interior** of the metal.
2. Its progress is **not limited by a shortage of oxygen** in the same way as some surface processes.
3. **Applied or residual stresses** can accelerate the attack.
4. Repeated **fluctuating or tensile stresses** separate grain boundaries and further promote corrosion.
5. The remaining sound material carries higher stress, leading to **cracking**, crack growth and possible **complete failure**.

### Why Detection Is Difficult

The book treats intergranular corrosion as **possibly the most dangerous form** because a component can suffer **severe internal weakening** while providing little external evidence.

The only visible clue may be **very fine surface cracks**, often requiring a **magnifying glass** to identify.

The text states that there is **no effective method of reliably establishing or limiting the resulting loss of structural strength** in an affected part. Accordingly, **parts showing this type of corrosion must be immediately rejected**.

## 9.9 Stress Corrosion



**Stress corrosion cracking (SCC)** develops when **steady tensile stress** acts together with a **corrosive environment**. The textbook describes this combined effect as a form of metal-fatigue-type failure.

Stresses may originate from:

* **Manufacture** of the component.
* **Assembly** of the structure.
* **Operational loads**.
* Other **structural loads**.

### Development of a Stress-Corrosion Crack

The described sequence is:

1. A metal already under stress corrodes more rapidly than a comparable unstressed part.
2. **Pitting** begins on the surface.
3. Removal of metal from the pit causes a local **stress concentration**.
4. A **crack forms** at the stressed pit.
5. The crack extends under the combined action of **tension and corrosion** until the component may fail.

The danger is that **little corrosion may be visible**, and there may be **no obvious general loss of metal** even while a serious crack develops.

# 1\. Structural Limitations



Structural mass limits describe the maximum aircraft masses permitted at different stages of operation. They are **different limits with different purposes**, so the applicable limit must be selected for the stage of flight or ground operation being considered.

## 1.1 Maximum Structural Taxi Mass / Maximum Ramp Mass

**Maximum Structural Taxi Mass** is also called **Maximum Ramp Mass**. It is the aircraft's maximum structurally permissible mass **at the start of taxi**, when leaving the loading gate for departure.

Fuel is normally burned during taxi. Consequently, the aircraft may start taxi at a mass greater than its take-off mass, then burn enough fuel to reach an acceptable **Take-off Mass (TOM)** before the take-off run.

* **Reference point:** Start of taxi / departure from the loading gate.
* **Reason for being distinct from MTOM:** Taxi fuel is consumed before take-off.
* **Limitation type:** Structural, rather than simply a desirable operational target.

## 1.2 Maximum Take-off Mass (MTOM)

**Maximum Take-off Mass (MTOM)** is the **maximum permissible total mass of the aeroplane at the start of the take-off run**, including everything and everyone aboard.

Therefore, the mass considered includes the aircraft together with occupants, baggage, cargo, fuel and other carried items. The limit applies at the **beginning of take-off**, not at the beginning of taxi.

## 1.3 Maximum Structural Landing Mass (MSLM)

**Maximum Structural Landing Mass (MSLM)** is the **maximum permissible total aircraft mass for landing in normal circumstances**.

The key point is that **normal landing structural capability** is assessed separately from taxi and take-off loading. An aircraft can therefore have distinct structural mass limits for these different stages.

## 1.4 Maximum Zero Fuel Mass (MZFM)

The textbook defines **Maximum Zero Fuel Mass (MZFM)** as the **maximum permissible mass of an aeroplane with no usable fuel**.

This limit is closely connected with **wing-root bending loads**:

1. In flight, aerodynamic lift tends to bend the wings upward.
2. The mass of fuel carried in wing tanks contributes downward loading along the wings.
3. That downward fuel load helps oppose some upward wing bending.
4. As wing fuel is used, the quantity of fuel in the wings, denoted in the book as (m\_{mF}), decreases.
5. With less wing fuel available to provide bending relief, **the wing-root bending moment is higher for an otherwise comparable loading condition**.
6. Therefore, a structural mass limit is required for the case in which there is **no usable fuel**: **MZFM**.

The source's central relationship is that **wing-root bending moment is greatest when the quantity of fuel in the wings is least**, under the loading comparison being discussed.

# 2\. Heavy Landings and Associated Structural Damage



## 2.1 Heavy Landings



Landing gear is designed to absorb landing loads **within specified aircraft mass and vertical descent-velocity conditions**. The textbook gives a maximum vertical descent velocity of **10 ft/sec or 3.15 m/sec** at **maximum landing weight**.

> \\\*\\\*Source-value note:\\\*\\\* Both values above are retained as printed in the textbook. They are not an exact unit conversion: \\\*\\\*10 ft/s ≈ 3.05 m/s\\\*\\\*. Use the applicable aircraft-approved maintenance and operating data rather than treating the rounded textbook pair as a universally applicable limit.

If the aircraft lands at a mass or vertical descent speed exceeding its design conditions, the landing gear and adjacent supporting structure **may be damaged or overstressed**. Because loads pass through the attachment structure, they can also reach the **fuselage and mainplanes (wings)**.

Damage is not limited to landing with a high vertical speed. Excessive structural stresses may also arise during:

* A landing made **with drift**, adding abnormal load components.
* A landing at an **abnormal aircraft attitude**.
* The **nose wheel** contacting the runway before the main wheels.
* A **tail wheel** contacting the runway before the main wheels, where applicable.

### Heavy-Landing Indications and Flight-Crew Report

Some aircraft have **heavy-landing indicators** providing a visual indication that specified **g-force thresholds** have been exceeded. Nevertheless, **all suspected heavy landings require relevant information to be passed to maintenance**, even when an indicator is not available.

The flight crew should report:

1. **Aircraft weight / landing mass**.
2. **Fuel distribution** around the aircraft.
3. **Landing conditions** and how the landing occurred.
4. Any **abnormal noises** that could indicate structural damage or failure.

These details help maintenance personnel identify what loads may have been transmitted to which structural areas.

### Expected Damage Locations

Following a heavy landing, the source identifies likely **primary damage areas** as:

* The **landing gear** itself.
* **Landing-gear supporting structure**, either within the wings or fuselage.
* **Wing attachment points**.
* **Tailplane attachment points**.
* **Engine mountings**.

There may also be **secondary damage** to the **upper and lower fuselage skin and supporting structure**, depending on aircraft configuration and loading.

## 2.2 Inspection After a Heavy Landing



The inspection pattern can depend on the aircraft type. For some aeroplanes:

* **If the primary areas show no damage**, the maintenance instructions may permit the secondary areas to remain uninspected.
* **If damage is found in a primary area**, the inspection must continue into the secondary areas.

There is **no single universal inspection sequence** described for every aircraft. The precise inspection locations and requirements must be taken from the aircraft's **applicable maintenance manual**.

## 2.3 Nose Wheel Landing



Landing with excessive loading on the **nose wheel** can cause significant forward-fuselage damage.

The structure or components at particular risk are:

* The **front pressure bulkhead** of the fuselage.
* The **nose-wheel strut**.
* The **drag link**, which may also sustain defects or deformation.

A sufficiently severe event may result in **nose-wheel collapse**. The principal lesson is that an abnormal nose-wheel touchdown is a **structural event**, not merely a possible tyre or wheel problem.

## 2.4 Tail Strike



The textbook identifies two conditions that increase the likelihood of a **tail strike**:

* An approach and landing at an airspeed **below (V\_{REF})**.
* **Over-rotation during the landing flare**.

A tail strike can damage:

* The **empennage**, or tail-unit structure.
* The **rear pressure bulkhead** in the fuselage.

Because contact may involve the rear fuselage and tail attachments, the effects can extend beyond any visible external scrape or impact mark.

# 3\. Failure Statistics — EASA CS-25 Safety Philosophy



The following section is based on the **EASA CS-25 regulatory material reproduced inside the original textbook**, headed **“Failure Statistics”**. The excerpt is identified as **CS-25 Book 2**, **Annex to ED Decision 2007/010/R**, **Amendment 3**. It describes the relationships between **failure-condition severity**, **acceptable likelihood**, **fail-safe design**, and **demonstrating compliance**.

The regulatory content below is a study-oriented restatement of the **book's historical excerpt**, not a claim that the same edition is the current certification standard.

## 3.1 Background: Why Probability Depends on Severity



Early aircraft systems were commonly evaluated against **specific design requirements**, a **single-fault criterion**, or the principle of **fail-safe design**.

As aircraft developed, more **safety-critical functions** became necessary. The systems performing them became increasingly **complex and interconnected**, so a failure in one system could influence other functions or other systems. Consequently, it became essential to consider:

* Loss of **one or more required functions**.
* Incorrect system function or **malfunction**.
* The hazards those failures might create for the **aircraft or occupants**.
* **Interactions between systems** performing different functions.

The general safety principle is an **inverse relationship** between a failure's **severity** and its **acceptable probability**: a more serious consequence must be correspondingly **less likely to occur**.

### Historical Probability Argument in the Source

The reproduced CS-25 guidance derives an approximate numerical target from historical evidence:

1. Serious accidents attributable to **operational and airframe-related causes** were historically estimated at roughly **one per million flight hours**, equivalent to **(10^{-6}) per flight hour**.
2. The guidance attributed approximately **10%** of that total to **aircraft-system Failure Conditions**.
3. It therefore suggested that accidents due to **all system-related Failure Conditions combined** should not exceed about **one per ten million flight hours**, or **(10^{-7}) per flight hour**, on a new aircraft design.
4. The guidance assumed, for apportioning that overall target, approximately **100 potentially Catastrophic Failure Conditions** across the aircraft.
5. Dividing the overall probability allowance equally gives **(10^{-7}/100=10^{-9}) per flight hour per Catastrophic Failure Condition**.
6. This **(10^{-9})** target is associated with the upper approximate numerical meaning of **“Extremely Improbable”** for catastrophic system failures.

The excerpt also explains the difficulty with setting only a whole-aircraft numerical target: **one cannot know whether it has been achieved until all relevant systems have been collectively analysed**. The individual allocation was therefore a means of distributing the risk target across potential failures, using the stated **arbitrary 100-condition assumption**.

**Less serious Failure Conditions** can have **higher acceptable probabilities** than catastrophic ones.

### Probability Calculation Check

\[
10^{-6}\\times 0.10=10^{-7}\\quad\\text{and}\\quad \\frac{10^{-7}}{100}=10^{-9}.
]

The resulting values are design-assessment targets in the reproduced guidance, **not probabilities to assign automatically to any individual real aircraft failure**.

## 3.2 Fail-Safe Design Concept



Part 25 airworthiness standards incorporate **fail-safe principles**: when designing an aircraft system, engineers consider the **effect of failures and combinations of failures**, rather than assuming everything always works correctly.

### Two Fundamental Failure Assumptions

1. **Single-failure assumption:** For any flight, designers should assume failure of any individual element, component or connection in a system or subsystem, **regardless of the calculated probability**. A single failure **must not produce a Catastrophic Failure Condition**.
2. **Subsequent and combined failures:** Additional failures during that same flight must also be considered, whether **detected** or **latent (undetected)**. They may be disregarded as a combination only where the joint probability with the first failure is demonstrated to be **Extremely Improbable**.

### Intended Probability Levels

The fail-safe approach aims to ensure that:

* **Major Failure Conditions** are **Remote**.
* **Hazardous Failure Conditions** are **Extremely Remote**.
* **Catastrophic Failure Conditions** are **Extremely Improbable**.

The reproduced guidance cautions that **one fail-safe technique alone is seldom sufficient**; a combination of two or more design measures is normally used.

### Eleven Fail-Safe Principles / Techniques

The excerpt enumerates the following approaches:

1. **Designed Integrity and Quality:** Provide suitable intrinsic reliability and structural or equipment quality, **including life limits**, so intended functions are achieved and failures are prevented.
2. **Redundancy / Backup Systems:** Arrange for continued functionality after failure of one, or another defined number, of components or systems. The source cites **multiple engines, hydraulic systems and flight-control systems**.
3. **Isolation / Segregation:** Keep systems, elements or components sufficiently separate that failure of one **does not cause another to fail**.
4. **Proven Reliability:** Make **multiple independent failures during the same flight** unlikely.
5. **Failure Warning / Indication:** Make failures **detectable** through appropriate warnings or indications.
6. **Flight-Crew Procedures:** Establish corrective actions **after a failure has been detected**.
7. **Checkability:** Provide the capability to **check a component's condition**.
8. **Designed Failure-Effect Limits:** Allow the structure or system to **sustain certain damage** and contain or limit the resulting safety consequences.
9. **Designed Failure Path:** Control and direct how a failure progresses so that its **safety impact is limited**.
10. **Margins / Factors of Safety:** Allow for adverse circumstances that are **not fully defined or foreseeable**.
11. **Error Tolerance:** Account for reasonably foreseeable errors made during **design, testing, manufacture, operation and maintenance**.

**Distinction:** Redundancy provides another way for a function to continue; **segregation** reduces the possibility that one fault defeats several otherwise redundant ways of providing that function.

## 3.3 Highly Integrated Systems



Increasing use of **electronics, software, highly integrated functions and interacting systems** created new challenges for traditional safety assessments.

### Why Conventional Methods May Be Insufficient

Methods effective for **deterministic risks or simpler conventional systems** may not provide adequate coverage where:

* Functions are **complex and interrelated**.
* Software and electronic technologies implement many of those functions.
* Errors in requirements or design can produce **integration or interaction effects**.
* Evaluating every individual subsystem separately may fail to capture **whole-aircraft hazards**.

### Additional Assurance Methods

The text identifies additional ways to increase confidence in system safety, including:

* **Development assurance**, combining **process assurance** and **verification coverage criteria**.
* **Structured analysis / assessment** at the **aeroplane level**, where needed.
* Analysis across **integrated or interacting systems** rather than only isolated components.

Systematic application of these methods helps identify and correct:

* Errors in **requirements**.
* Errors in **design**.
* Effects resulting from **integration or system interactions**.

### Purpose of the AMC Revision

The reproduced Acceptable Means of Compliance (**AMC**) was updated to reflect revisions to **CS 25.1309** and to provide both **qualitative and quantitative approaches** to assessing the entire aeroplane and its systems.

The guidance also explains how to decide **when or whether** particular analyses or development-assurance activities should be performed within the overall **development and safety-assessment processes**.

Numerical meanings are assigned to regulatory probability terms when quantitative analysis is used. Such numerical tools **supplement rather than replace** qualitative assessments based on **engineering and operational judgement**.

# 4\. Failure Condition Classifications and Probability Terms



A **Failure Condition** is classified according to its potential effect on the aircraft, on its occupants and on the crew's ability to operate safely. The source describes **five severity classifications**, followed by **qualitative probability descriptions** and **approximate probability-per-flight-hour ranges**.

## 4.1 Classifications by Severity



### 4.1.1 No Safety Effect

Such a failure **does not influence safety**. For example, it does not reduce the aircraft's **operational capability** or increase **flight-crew workload**.

### 4.1.2 Minor

A **Minor Failure Condition** does not substantially reduce aircraft safety. Any crew actions required remain comfortably **within normal crew capability**.

Effects can include:

* A **small reduction in safety margins** or functional capability.
* A **slight increase in flight-crew workload**, for instance an ordinary flight-plan change.
* Some **physical discomfort to passengers or cabin crew**.

### 4.1.3 Major

A **Major Failure Condition** reduces aircraft capability or the crew's ability to deal with difficult operating conditions more substantially than a Minor failure.

Examples of effects include:

* A **significant reduction** in safety margins or functionality.
* A **significant increase** in flight-crew workload.
* Conditions that **impair crew efficiency**.
* **Flight-crew discomfort**.
* **Physical distress** to passengers or cabin crew, potentially including injuries.

### 4.1.4 Hazardous

A **Hazardous Failure Condition** creates a more severe reduction in aircraft capability or crew coping ability, including one or more of the following:

1. A **large reduction in safety margins or functional capability**.
2. Flight-crew **physical distress or such excessive workload** that accurate and complete task performance **cannot be relied upon**.
3. **Serious or fatal injuries** to a **relatively small number of occupants other than the flight crew**.

### 4.1.5 Catastrophic

A **Catastrophic Failure Condition** produces **multiple fatalities**, ordinarily involving **loss of the aircraft**.

> \\\*\\\*Terminology note from the source:\\\*\\\* Earlier versions of the rule and advisory material used a definition based on a failure that \\\*\\\*prevents continued safe flight and landing\\\*\\\*. The excerpt specifically notes that prior wording.

## 4.2 Qualitative Probability Terms



Qualitative descriptions help engineers judge likelihood without necessarily computing a precise numerical rate for every event.

|Term|Meaning in the excerpt|
|-|-|
|**Probable**|Expected to occur **one or more times during the operational life of each individual aircraft**.|
|**Remote**|Unlikely over the entire life of **one particular aircraft**, but may occur **several times across the accumulated lives of many aircraft of that type**.|
|**Extremely Remote**|Not expected during the total life of an individual aircraft, but could occur **a few times across all aircraft of that type**.|
|**Extremely Improbable**|So unlikely that it is **not expected to happen throughout the entire operational life of all aircraft of one type**.|

The difference is between the lifetime of **one aircraft** and the **combined operational lives of the whole type fleet**.

## 4.3 Quantitative Probability Terms



The excerpt uses **Average Probability per Flight Hour** when describing numerical likelihood. The boundaries are approximate **orders of magnitude** used to support engineering judgement.

|Quantitative term|Approximate Average Probability per Flight Hour|
|-|-|
|**Probable**|Greater than approximately **(1\\times10^{-5})**.|
|**Remote**|Around **(10^{-5})** or below, but above roughly **(10^{-7})**.|
|**Extremely Remote**|Around **(10^{-7})** or below, but above roughly **(10^{-9})**.|
|**Extremely Improbable**|On the order of **(10^{-9})** or less.|

These values are **not interchangeable with the severity classifications**. Severity answers **“How serious is the effect?”**; probability answers **“How likely is it?”**. Safety objectives link them.

# 5\. Safety Objective and the Probability–Severity Relationship



## 5.1 Overall Safety Objective

The aim of **CS 25.1309**, as described in the excerpt, is to establish an **acceptable safety level for equipment and systems installed on an aircraft**.

To do so, an **inverse relationship** must be maintained between:

* The **average probability per flight hour** of the failure; and
* The **severity of the resulting Failure Condition**.

### Permitted Severity / Probability Relationships

1. **No Safety Effect:** **No probability limitation** is required on safety grounds.
2. **Minor:** May be **Probable**.
3. **Major:** Must be **no more frequent than Remote**.
4. **Hazardous:** Must be **no more frequent than Extremely Remote**.
5. **Catastrophic:** Must be **Extremely Improbable**.

The essential principle is that a system must be **less likely to produce a failure** as the consequences become **more serious**.

## 5.3 Demonstrating the Catastrophic Safety Objective



The safety objective associated with a **Catastrophic Failure Condition** may be met by showing **both** of the following:

1. **No single failure** causes that Catastrophic Failure Condition.
2. **Each Catastrophic Failure Condition is Extremely Improbable**.

Both criteria are needed: a system's overall statistical probability alone does not remove the requirement to consider single failures.

## 5.4 Exceptional Alternative Demonstration



The reproduced guidance allows a special approach **where meeting the numerical Catastrophic probability criterion is technologically or economically impracticable**. All three of the following are required:

1. Use **well-proven design and construction methods**.
2. Establish the **Average Probability per Flight Hour of each Failure Condition** using structured analysis methods such as **Fault Tree Analysis**, **Markov Analysis**, or **Dependency Diagrams**.
3. Demonstrate that the **sum** of the average probabilities per flight hour for **all Catastrophic system-caused Failure Conditions** is on the order of **(10^{-7})** or lower, consistent with the earlier whole-aircraft argument.

The original describes this as an **exceptional** means of meeting the safety objective, not as a routine exemption from safety analysis.

Equipment, systems and installations covered by the cited **CS 25.1309(a)(1)** should be shown to function correctly **after being installed in the aircraft**.

The aircraft's systems and their components must be evaluated **both individually and in relation to other systems**.

Demonstration of compliance is normally by **analysis**, supplemented where needed by **ground tests, flight tests and simulator tests**.

1. **Functional and physical interrelationships** among systems.
2. The detailed **means of compliance**, potentially including **Development Assurance** techniques.
3. How the applicant will **show that the plan has been accomplished**.

The AMC acknowledges that the industry already uses several accepted safety-assessment techniques. It **does not require every applicant to use a particular referenced document** as the only route to meeting the safety objective.

* **Markov Analysis**.
* **Fault Tree Analysis**.

## 6.6 Development Assurance Methods



A failure assessment must consider not only physical component faults, but also the possibility of errors introduced through **requirements, design or implementation**.

### Traditional Verification

For simpler systems, errors have traditionally been found through:

* **Exhaustive testing** of systems and components.
* **Direct inspection**.
* Other **direct verification techniques** capable of establishing all relevant operating characteristics.

Such approaches may remain practical for a simple system with **few functions and limited integration**.

### Complex, Integrated Systems

For complex equipment, exhaustive testing may be:

* **Impossible**, because the complete collection of potential system states cannot be established; or
* **Impracticable**, because the number of required tests is too large.

In such cases the excerpt permits **Development Assurance** as part of demonstrating compliance.

# 7\. Hard Time and On-condition Maintenance



The textbook finishes its technical discussion by distinguishing two philosophies for determining **when an item must be removed from service or allowed to remain installed**.

## 7.1 Hard Time Maintenance

Under **Hard Time Maintenance**, an item is **removed from service at or before a predetermined scheduled maintenance period** for required inspection, repair or other specified maintenance action.

This approach is based on a **fixed service limit or interval**, rather than waiting for evidence that an item is deteriorating in use.

**Essential feature:** The item must be removed **before the scheduled time limit is exceeded**.

## 7.2 “On-condition” Maintenance

**On-condition Maintenance** uses inspections or functional checks to establish an item's **actual service condition**. Results may indicate that the item should be **removed before it fails in service**.

This approach is appropriate where continued **airworthiness can be evaluated without dismantling or overhauling the item**, using, as applicable:

* **Visual inspection**.
* **Measurements**.
* **Functional testing**.
* Other suitable forms of assessment.

The item may be monitored **continuously or at specified intervals**. Its condition or performance is compared with an **appropriate standard**, and that comparison determines whether it can **remain in service**.

### Comparison

|Point|Hard Time Maintenance|On-condition Maintenance|
|-|-|-|
|**Main basis**|Pre-established maintenance/removal interval|Actual condition and performance against an accepted standard|
|**Timing**|Remove at or before specified time|Inspect/test periodically or continuously and remove when condition warrants|
|**Inspection concept**|Scheduled action at a prescribed limit|Condition established by inspection, measurement or test without necessary overhaul/disassembly|
|**Main decision**|Has the specified period been reached?|Is the item still fit for continued service?|

## 9.2 Explanation of Each Answer

1. **a — Wing main spar:** The answer grid selects resistance to **bending and torsional loads**, consistent with the spar's major load-bearing role in the wing.
2. **b — Wing ribs:** Ribs preserve **aerofoil shape** and help support the wing skin and its structural members.
3. **c — Stringers:** Stringers **stiffen the skin**, preventing local instability or buckling while helping carry longitudinal loads.
4. **d — Ultimate strength:** **Design Ultimate Load = Design Limit Load × 1.5** in the textbook's standard design example.
5. **b — Frames/formers:** The major functions include giving the fuselage its **cross-sectional shape** and carrying loads, including relevant pressure-related loads.
6. **b — Bending relief:** **Aileron up-float** and keeping fuel in wing tanks for longer reduce the upward bending moment on the wing.
7. **c — Safe life:** The source answer selects **2, 3 and 4**, relating to expected fatigue life, programmed inspection and replacement before the limit. **Question wording is imperfect:** the earlier chapter primarily defines safe life by a **pre-established service/fatigue limit**, whereas periodic crack detection is emphasized in its fail-safe/damage-tolerance discussion. Retain the original answer key, but recognize this overlap.
8. **b — Fail-safe structure:** It relies on **planned inspection** and sufficient **alternative/redundant structural capability** after limited damage.
9. **d — Pressurized stressed skin:** The skin carries a substantial fraction of the **structural loads**.
10. **b — Fuselage:** Its principal role is to house the **crew and payload**, while also connecting major structural systems.
11. **a — Location system:** **Stations and water lines** identify the locations of structural members and other aircraft components.
12. **b — Flight-deck windows:** The layered **strengthened-glass and clear vinyl-interlayer** construction provides strength and impact absorption.
13. **b — Cantilever wing:** Its structure carries the loads without external bracing from wires or struts.
14. **b — Torsion box:** Wing **spars, skin, ribs and (as discussed in the text) stringers** form a load-carrying structure resisting bending and torsion.
15. **d — Lightening hole:** The selected option reflects the ability of a suitably formed opening to **save mass while maintaining structural stiffness**.
16. **c — Flutter:** It is a potentially **destructive aerodynamic/structural vibration** that must not arise inside the approved operating envelope.
17. **d — Mass balance:** Moving the control surface's **centre of gravity closer to its hinge** changes inertia and vibration characteristics.
18. **a — Damage tolerance:** Structural loads are distributed through an area able to tolerate **limited, inspectable damage** before failure becomes critical.
19. **c — Structural material:** **Aluminium alloys** and rivets are typical major materials, with **titanium or steel** where special strength is required.
20. **b — MZFM:** The definition is **maximum permissible mass with no usable fuel**.
