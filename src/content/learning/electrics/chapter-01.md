# Chapter 1 — DC Electrics: Basic Principles

# 1\. Introduction: Where Electricity Comes From

## 1.1 Matter, atoms and electric charge

Electrical behavior begins with **electrons**, the negatively charged constituents of atoms. An electric current in a conducting material occurs when electrons move through that material in an organized way.

All everyday materials consist of **atoms**. An atom contains a central **nucleus**, made up of **protons** (positively charged) and **neutrons** (uncharged), surrounded by electrons. Different elements have different atomic structures and numbers of electrons. In the simplified orbital model used by the textbook, electrons are shown travelling around the nucleus; this is a conceptual illustration rather than a literal modern representation of electron paths.

* **Electron:** negative electric charge.
* **Proton:** positive electric charge.
* **Neutron:** electrically neutral.
* **Electrically neutral atom:** contains equal quantities of positive and negative charge.

Attraction between the positive nucleus and negative electrons helps keep the atom together. Electrons associated with the outer regions of some atoms are less tightly held than the inner electrons. They can transfer between neighboring atoms and are commonly described in elementary electrical theory as **free electrons**. It is the movement of these available charge carriers that makes metallic conductors useful.

## 1.2 Positive ions, negative ions and moving charges

An atom's electrical state changes when it exchanges electrons:

* **Losing an electron** leaves more positive than negative charge; the atom becomes a **positive ion**.
* **Gaining an electron** creates excess negative charge; the atom becomes a **negative ion**.

When free electrons are made to drift in a common direction through a material, that directed charge movement constitutes an **electric current**. Random thermal motion of electrons by itself is not the same as the directed flow responsible for circuit current.

## 1.3 Conductors and insulators

The ease with which current can pass depends on how freely charges can move inside the material.

|Material group|Basic characteristic|Examples given in the textbook|
|-|-|-|
|**Conductor**|Contains mobile charge carriers; current passes comparatively easily|**Copper, silver, aluminium**|
|**Insulator**|Very few charges are free to move; strongly opposes current|**Wood, rubber, glass, plastics**|

The distinction matters in wiring: the metallic wire carries current, whereas its insulation reduces unwanted current paths and helps keep the electrical system safe.

## 1.4 Why electrons travel through a circuit

Connecting a source across a conducting path establishes an electrical potential difference. In the basic metallic-circuit explanation:

1. The **negative terminal** repels free electrons.
2. The **positive terminal** attracts electrons.
3. Electrons drift through the external conductor **from negative toward positive**.
4. To sustain current, the electrical source must provide an internal means of carrying charge so the overall path remains complete.

Merely shifting electrons toward the positive terminal would not maintain current indefinitely. A supply—such as a battery or generator—must keep the driving potential difference available, and the circuit must remain **closed**. An open circuit interrupts the continuous current path.

## 1.5 Electron flow and conventional current

Circuit conventions were established before the movement of electrons was understood. Engineers originally chose **positive-to-negative** as the direction of current. That convention remains the standard for circuit equations and diagrams, even though electrons in a metal drift in the opposite direction.

|Description|Direction through the external wire|
|-|-|
|**Electron flow**|Negative terminal → positive terminal|
|**Conventional current**|Positive terminal → negative terminal|

These are two ways of assigning direction to the same electrical behavior, not two unrelated currents travelling in a normal wire.

## 1.6 Six ways to develop an electrical driving force

The textbook identifies six physical effects that can create the electrical potential difference necessary to make charge move:

1. **Friction:** separation of charge that produces **static electricity**.
2. **Chemical action:** electrical energy supplied by **cells and batteries**, including primary and secondary cells.
3. **Magnetism:** the principle used in **generators and alternators**.
4. **Heat:** a voltage can develop at the junction of unlike metals, as in a **thermocouple**.
5. **Light:** a **photoelectric cell** converts the effect of light into an electrical output.
6. **Pressure:** certain **piezoelectric crystals** produce an electrical effect under applied force or pressure.

The chapter emphasizes that, among these six mechanisms, **chemical cells/batteries and magnetic generation** are the methods used for substantial everyday electrical power supplies.

# 2\. Electromotive Force (EMF) and Voltage

## 2.1 What drives electrical current?

An electrical circuit needs a driving influence just as flowing water needs a pressure difference. The electrical driving influence is called **electromotive force (EMF)**. A difference in electrical potential between two points is a **potential difference (PD)**.

* **Voltage** measures EMF or potential difference.
* The unit is the **volt (V)**.
* Circuit voltage is commonly represented by **$V$** or **$E$**.

The water analogy helps explain the effect: increasing the pressure difference in a suitable water line tends to increase the flow; similarly, **for unchanged resistance**, increasing circuit voltage increases current. Reducing the voltage decreases current. Electrical systems therefore normally aim to maintain the voltage needed for proper equipment operation.

## 2.2 Voltage sources

Two supply examples are central to the chapter:

* **Battery:** a chemical source whose ability to provide electrical energy is limited by its stored charge/energy; its terminal voltage can change as it discharges.
* **Generator:** a source used to supply electrical power and help maintain an appropriate, essentially steady system voltage during operation.

The electrical voltage is measured **between two points**, not at a single isolated point without a reference.

# 3\. Electrical Current

## 3.1 Meaning, symbol and unit

**Current** expresses how much electric charge passes a chosen point in the circuit per unit time. The basic textbook description is the number of electrons passing a point each second; quantitatively, current is charge flow per second.

* Circuit current: **$I$**.
* Unit of current: **ampere (A)**, commonly called an **amp**.
* **Ammeter:** instrument for measuring current.

To measure circuit current, the ammeter must be placed **in series** so that the circuit current also passes through the instrument. This differs from a voltmeter, which is placed across two points.

## 3.2 Small-current units

|Quantity|Symbol|Equivalent|
|-|-|-|
|Microampere|**µA**|$1\~\\mu\\mathrm{A}=10^{-6}\~\\mathrm{A}$|
|Milliampere|**mA**|$1\~\\mathrm{mA}=10^{-3}\~\\mathrm{A}$|
|Ampere|**A**|Standard unit|

## 3.3 Three effects of current

A current travelling through a circuit can give rise to three practically important effects:

### Heating effect

Current through a conductor or resistance develops heat. Practical uses and examples include **electric heaters/fires, irons, filament light bulbs and fuses**. Excessive current may cause damaging temperatures.

### Magnetic effect

Any current-carrying conductor has an associated **magnetic field**. This behavior is fundamental in **motors, generators and transformers**.

### Chemical effect

Current passed through certain conducting liquids—**electrolytes**—can produce chemical changes in the liquid and in immersed electrodes. Examples are **battery charging** and **electroplating**.

# 4\. Resistance

## 4.1 What resistance does

A complete electrical path does not necessarily permit unlimited charge movement: materials and circuit elements resist current. That opposition is called **electrical resistance**.

With the same applied voltage:

* **Less resistance** → **more current**.
* **More resistance** → **less current**.

With resistance unchanged:

* **Higher voltage** → **more current**.
* **Lower voltage** → **less current**.

A known resistance can therefore be deliberately inserted into a circuit to control its current.

## 4.2 Four factors affecting the resistance of a conductor

1. **Material:** substances differ in conductivity; **silver** is a better conductor than **copper**, for example.
2. **Length:** a **longer** wire presents **more resistance** if material and thickness stay the same.
3. **Cross-sectional area:** a **thicker** wire has **less resistance** when the remaining factors are fixed.
4. **Temperature:** a material's resistance may rise or fall as its temperature changes.

### Temperature coefficients

The **temperature coefficient**, represented by **$\\alpha$ (alpha)**, describes the resistance change associated with temperature.

* **Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC):** resistance **increases** when temperature increases.
* **Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC):** resistance **decreases** when temperature increases.

Devices using these characteristics can form part of **aircraft temperature-measurement systems**.

## 4.3 Ohms and resistance prefixes

Resistance is measured in **ohms**, symbol **$\\Omega$**. A component has a resistance of **one ohm** when an applied **one volt** results in a current of **one ampere** under the stated conditions.

|Unit|Symbol|Value in ohms|
|-|-|-|
|Micro-ohm|**µΩ**|$10^{-6}\~\\Omega$|
|Milliohm|**mΩ**|$10^{-3}\~\\Omega$|
|Ohm|**Ω**|$1\~\\Omega$|
|Kilohm|**kΩ**|$10^{3}\~\\Omega$|
|Megohm|**MΩ**|$10^{6}\~\\Omega$|

**Take care with case:** `m` denotes *milli* and `M` denotes *mega*. Confusing these prefixes introduces a factor of a billion.

## 4.4 Fixed and variable resistors

A **resistor** is a circuit component selected for a particular resistance. A **fixed resistor** maintains approximately one designed value during normal operation; a **variable resistor** allows the effective resistance to be adjusted, enabling controlled changes to circuit current or voltage distribution.

# 5\. Ohm's Law

## 5.1 Relationship between voltage, current and resistance

**Ohm's Law** expresses the relationship of three basic electrical quantities in an ohmic circuit:

$$
V=IR
$$

where **$V$** is voltage in volts, **$I$** is current in amperes and **$R$** is resistance in ohms.

Rearrange the same relationship to calculate the unknown quantity:

$$
I=\\frac{V}{R}
\\qquad\\text{and}\\qquad
R=\\frac{V}{I}
$$

This means that, with fixed resistance, current varies **directly** with voltage. With constant voltage, current varies **inversely** with resistance.

**Example:** Across a $6\~\\Omega$ resistor connected to $12\~\\mathrm{V}$, the current would be

$$
I=\\frac{12\~\\mathrm{V}}{6\~\\Omega}=2\~\\mathrm{A}.
$$

This extra numerical check illustrates the same rule without changing the source's worked circuit problems later in the chapter.

# 6\. Electrical Power and Heating

## 6.1 Work and power

In mechanics, **work** is done when a force causes displacement; **power** tells us how quickly work is done. An electrical source provides energy to move charge through a circuit, where it may produce heating, magnetic effects or chemical effects.

* Electrical power is measured in **watts (W)**.
* One watt represents one joule of energy transferred each second.
* The basic circuit relationship is power = voltage × current.

$$
P=VI
$$

The textbook also uses **$W$** as a symbol for power in watts; these notes use **$P$** for the quantity and **W** for its unit, to avoid confusing them.

## 6.2 Three useful power formulas

Combining $P=VI$ with Ohm's Law yields:

$$
\\boxed{P=VI}
\\qquad
\\boxed{P=I^{2}R}
\\qquad
\\boxed{P=\\frac{V^{2}}{R}}
$$

Use the form that matches the quantities known:

|Known quantities|Select|
|-|-|
|Voltage and current|$P=VI$|
|Current and resistance|$P=I^{2}R$|
|Voltage and resistance|$P=V^{2}/R$|

## 6.3 Current and resistor heating

A resistance converts electrical energy into heat. In particular, resistive heating follows **$P=I^{2}R$**. Therefore, if resistance stays constant, raising current increases heating according to the **square** of current, not merely in direct proportion.

For instance, **doubling current** at the same resistance multiplies heat-producing power by **four**. This is why even a comparatively modest current increase can have major thermal consequences. Excessive current can overheat and ultimately damage or melt a resistor or conductor.

## 6.4 Component ratings and circuit protection

Every electrical component has an allowable **power rating**, representing how much power it can safely dissipate or use under specified conditions (for example, a **60-watt light bulb**). Exceeding that capability may produce excessive temperature.

Aircraft circuits are fitted with devices such as **fuses** and **circuit breakers**. Their purpose is to disconnect a circuit under excessive-current conditions and limit the risk of overheating and damage; the protection rating must be appropriate for the circuit and its equipment.

# 7\. Resistors Connected in Series

## 7.1 How a series circuit works

**Series** means components are connected **one after another in the same current path**. Each component carries the same current. Adding resistance to a series circuit reduces its current for an unchanged supply voltage, and correspondingly reduces the total power delivered by that supply to the circuit.

The limitation is operational: the individual loads cannot generally be operated independently through that common path; if any series element opens, the current path is broken and **all downstream series operation stops**.

$$

A series connection is sometimes useful for controlling current, but the shared current path and lack of independent operation make it unsuitable for many aircraft loads.

# 8\. Resistors Connected in Parallel

## 8.1 What is different in a parallel circuit?

Components in **parallel** have individual branches connected to the same two supply points. Consequently:

* Every branch experiences the **same voltage**.
* Branches can be **controlled independently**, provided the installation has suitable switching.
* If one branch becomes open-circuit, the other complete branches can still operate.
* **Total supply current** is the sum of the branch currents.

For these reasons, **most aircraft electrical loads are arranged in parallel**, rather than all in one series chain.

## 8.2 Total parallel resistance

Parallel equivalent resistance is calculated by adding **reciprocals**:

$$
\\frac{1}{R\_{\\mathrm{T}}}
=\\frac{1}{R\_1}+\\frac{1}{R\_2}+\\frac{1}{R\_3}
$$

# 10\. Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's laws describe how **currents join/split** and how **potential differences add** in circuits. Their significance extends beyond a single series or parallel resistor calculation.

## 10.1 First law — currents at a junction

At a circuit junction, **the current entering equals the current leaving**. The source illustrates two incoming currents combining into a single outgoing current:

$$
\\boxed{I\_3=I\_1+I\_2}
$$

For example, if $I\_1=0.5\~\\mathrm{A}$ and $I\_2=1.2\~\\mathrm{A}$, the outgoing current is $I\_3=1.7\~\\mathrm{A}$. This is a simple demonstration of current balance at a node.
