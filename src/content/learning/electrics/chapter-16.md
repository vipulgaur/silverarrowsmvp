# Chapter 16: AC Electrics — Semiconductors

## 1\. An Introduction to Semiconductors

### 1.1 Why semiconductor technology matters

A small electronic calculator can perform operations that once required equipment occupying far more space. The enormous reduction in size and cost of electronic devices has been made possible by **transistors** and the progressive **miniaturization of electronic circuits**. Even inexpensive calculators demonstrate how much computing capability can be accommodated in a compact device; the textbook compares their capabilities with the electronics available during the Apollo Moon missions.

Aircraft have undergone a similar transformation. Electronic systems now contribute to many important flight-deck functions, making an understanding of the basic electrical behavior of **semiconductors** valuable to a pilot studying aircraft systems. The chapter develops that understanding in stages: first by comparing conducting and insulating materials, then by examining modified semiconductor materials, junction diodes, and finally transistors.

The diagram of a hydrogen atom introduces this arrangement. A neutral atom contains equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so its total charge is zero. Larger atoms can have multiple electron shells, and the arrangement of electrons in the **outermost shell** is especially important in determining how the material behaves electrically.

### 2.2 Bonding and free electrons

Atoms in a solid are held together by bonds involving electrons in their outer shells, known as **valence electrons**. The atoms occupy a regular, repeating arrangement called a **lattice**.

Electrons associated with inner shells are generally held more firmly by their parent atoms. Outer-shell electrons may be less tightly bound, and some can become **free electrons**, capable of moving through the material instead of remaining associated with a single atom. Their mobility is a major factor in electrical conduction.

### 2.3 Conductors

A **conductor** offers relatively little opposition to an electric current because mobile charge carriers are readily available. The source describes the bonding in good conductors using the term **electrovalent bonding** and relates their good electrical performance to the availability of free electrons.

The practical characteristics are:

* **High electrical conductivity:** charge moves through the substance comparatively easily.
* **Low resistivity:** the material naturally offers limited opposition to current flow.
* **Numerous available free electrons:** mobile electrons provide the mechanism for current transport.

Examples given are **gold, silver, and copper**.

### 2.4 Insulators

An **insulator** strongly opposes electron movement. Its electrons are much less available to act as charge carriers because they remain associated with the bonding structure; the text characterizes this through **covalent bonds**.

An insulating material consequently has **low conductivity** and **high resistivity**. In normal use, an applied electrical potential does not produce significant current compared with a conducting material. **Mica** is the example used in this chapter.

|Property|Conductor|Insulator|
|-|-|-|
|Availability of free electrons|Relatively high|Very low|
|Ease of current flow|High|Low|
|Conductivity|High|Low|
|Resistivity|Low|High|
|Examples|Gold, silver, copper|Mica|

## 3\. Semiconductors

### 3.1 Electrical properties between those of conductors and insulators

A **semiconductor** has electrical properties between those of a good conductor and a good insulator. The two examples considered here are **silicon** and **germanium**.

Their atoms are joined by **covalent bonds**. At normal temperatures they contain some available charge carriers, although not enough to conduct as readily as a metal. With an **electromotive force (EMF)** applied across an untreated specimen, the current is therefore greater than that expected through a good insulator but less than through a good conductor under comparable conditions.

Within the semiconductor's atomic lattice, neighboring atoms share valence electrons to complete their outer bonding arrangements. The diagram emphasizes these **covalent bonds** by enclosing selected shared pairs of electrons.

### 3.3 Doping: controlling conductivity

The electrical behavior of silicon or germanium can be changed deliberately by adding very small, carefully controlled quantities of another element. This treatment is called **doping**.

Doping introduces atoms whose number of outer-shell electrons differs from that of the semiconductor atoms. Depending on which impurity is used, the modified crystal has either **additional free electrons** or **additional holes** available to carry current. The two resulting classes of material are designated **N-type** and **P-type**.

## 4\. N-Type Material

### 4.1 Producing an electron-rich semiconductor

An N-type region is formed by adding a **pentavalent impurity**—an atom with **five electrons in its outer shell**—to silicon or germanium. The examples given are **arsenic** and **antimony**.

The textbook uses a very low impurity concentration, of the order of **one dopant atom per 10⁸ semiconductor atoms**, to illustrate that a substantial change in electrical behavior requires only a small amount of added material.

### 4.2 Why conductivity increases

Four of the dopant's five valence electrons participate in covalent bonds with its neighboring lattice atoms. The **fifth electron** is not required to complete these four bonds. It can therefore act as an additional **free electron**, increasing the number of charge carriers and improving electrical conductivity.

The name **N-type** refers to the prevalence of **negatively charged mobile electrons**, not to the material having an overall negative charge.

### 4.3 Electrical neutrality

Each extra mobile electron is associated with an impurity atom that becomes a **fixed positive ion** after supplying the electron. The negative charge on the mobile electron and the positive charge on the stationary ion balance one another.

Consequently, an N-type semiconductor is **electrically neutral overall**, despite its relatively large population of free electrons.

## 5\. P-Type Material

### 5.1 Producing a hole-rich semiconductor

P-type material is created by doping silicon or germanium with an impurity whose outer shell contains only **three valence electrons**. The examples given are **aluminium** and **indium**. The textbook applies the same approximate **1:10⁸ doping ratio** as for N-type material.

A trivalent dopant cannot supply all four electrons needed for the normal surrounding covalent bonds. One bond position therefore lacks an electron, leaving a **hole**.

### 5.2 How holes move

A hole should be understood as an **unoccupied electron position**, not as a separate physical particle. An electron in a neighboring bond can move into the vacancy. When it does, the original hole disappears but a new hole is left at the electron's former position.

As this process repeats, the **hole appears to move through the crystal** in a direction opposite to the individual electrons filling successive vacancies. Hole movement increases the material's ability to carry electrical current.

### 5.3 Why the material remains neutral

In P-type material, the mobile carriers are described as **positive holes**. The dopant atom that has accepted an electron becomes a **fixed negative ion**. The number of these negative ions balances the hole charges, so the specimen does not acquire a net positive electrical charge merely because it is P-type.

## 6\. Current Flow Through Doped Semiconductors

### 6.1 Current in N-type material

When a voltage is connected across N-type semiconductor material, the free electrons are attracted toward the **positive terminal** and move away from the **negative terminal**.

Electrons arriving at the positive connection are removed from the material while other electrons enter at the negative connection. These transfers sustain the electron flow without upsetting the overall charge balance between the mobile electrons and the fixed positive ions.

Thus, the physical electron movement is **negative to positive**, opposite to the conventional direction of current.

### 6.2 Current in P-type material

In P-type material, the process can be described using the motion of holes:

1. Electrons are drawn out through the positive connection, creating empty electron positions in that region.
2. Neighboring electrons successively fill the vacancies, leaving replacement vacancies elsewhere.
3. In this way, holes effectively travel through the material **toward the negative terminal**.
4. Electrons entering from the negative terminal finally occupy the holes that arrive there.

Accordingly, **hole flow follows conventional current direction—from positive to negative**—even though the electrons themselves move in the opposite direction. The balance of charges and fixed negative ions remains maintained as current passes.

## 7\. The P-N Junction

### 7.1 Combining two doped regions

A **P-N junction** is formed by bringing a P-type region into intimate contact with an N-type region. The original description refers to joining the material by a process similar to welding.

Immediately after contact:

* Some **free electrons** leave the N-type region and diffuse into the P-type region.
* Some **holes** effectively diffuse from the P-type region toward the N-type region.
* Electrons meeting holes at the boundary **recombine**, reducing the population of mobile carriers near the interface.

### 7.2 Depletion layer and barrier potential

The loss of mobile carriers around the interface leaves behind charged, relatively immobile ions. This narrow boundary region is known as the **depletion layer**.

The separated charges create an internal electric field, described in the chapter as a **barrier potential**. This potential opposes further unrestricted diffusion of electrons and holes across the boundary.

A battery symbol can be used to represent this internal electrical barrier schematically, but it does **not** mean that the entire P-N device carries a net electric charge. Charge separation occurs locally at the junction; the combined piece of material remains electrically neutral overall.

## 8\. Reverse Bias

### 8.1 Applying voltage in the blocking direction

The behavior of a P-N junction changes according to the **polarity of the external EMF**. Under **reverse bias**, the applied voltage increases the junction's existing electrical barrier.

For the arrangement shown in **Figure 16.6 A**, the connections are such that majority carriers are pulled **away from the junction**. Additional charge separation develops near the boundary, making the depletion region **wider** and making it more difficult for electrons and holes to cross.

As a result, reverse bias largely prevents conduction. The source notes that a small **leakage current** can still pass, typically on the order of **microamperes (µA)**; the reverse-biased junction is therefore not an ideal open circuit.

## 9\. Forward Bias

### 9.1 Applying voltage in the conducting direction

Under **forward bias**, the external voltage acts in opposition to the built-in barrier and encourages the majority carriers to approach the junction. In the orientation of **Figure 16.6 B**:

* Holes in the **P-type material** drift toward the right-hand junction.
* Free electrons in the **N-type material** drift toward the left-hand junction.
* Electrons and holes meet and recombine in the boundary region.

This reduces the effective barrier and allows appreciable current to pass through the junction. The same semiconductor connection that blocked current when reverse biased therefore becomes conducting when forward biased.

## 10\. The Junction Diode

### 10.1 A junction that conducts in one direction

The difference between forward and reverse bias gives the P-N junction a valuable property: it allows substantial current in **one direction** while strongly opposing current in the other. A device that exploits this property is called a **junction diode**.

Its one-way conducting action makes it useful as a **rectifier**, where an alternating input can be allowed through in the permitted direction while the opposite polarity is blocked. The textbook compares this characteristic with that of an older **thermionic diode (valve)**.

## 11\. The Bipolar or Junction Transistor

### 11.1 Three-layer construction

The **bipolar junction transistor (BJT)** is described in the chapter as a device formed by arranging semiconductor material so that it contains **two P-N junctions**. Unlike the simple two-region diode, it has **three connected regions** and can control a relatively large current using a much smaller controlling current.

Two layer sequences are possible:

* **N-P-N transistor:** a narrow **P-type** region lies between two **N-type** regions.
* **P-N-P transistor:** a narrow **N-type** region lies between two **P-type** regions.

The central region is deliberately thin. The typical thickness cited is **25 µm** (25 micrometres).

The external connections on either arrangement are named:

* **Emitter (E)**
* **Base (B)**
* **Collector (C)**

The **base** is the thin middle region; the **emitter** and **collector** are the regions on either side.

### 11.2 Identifying a transistor from its circuit symbol

The conventional symbols for NPN and PNP transistors are similar except for the direction of the **emitter arrow**, which shows the direction of **conventional current** at that terminal.

* **NPN:** the arrow points **outward**, in the conventional-current direction from the base toward the emitter.
* **PNP:** the arrow points **inward**, toward the base, corresponding to conventional current from the emitter toward the base.

Remembering the direction of this arrow makes the two devices easy to distinguish in electrical diagrams.

## 12\. Operation of an N-P-N Transistor

### 12.1 Collector-emitter circuit without base control

In the basic NPN arrangement, applying the main supply across the **collector-emitter path** does not by itself establish the substantial conduction illustrated in the chapter. The appropriate **base-emitter bias** must also be present.

The left-hand circuit in **Figure 16.9** represents the non-conducting condition. The collector-base junction is reverse biased, so the transistor does not carry the large controlled current shown in the companion circuit.

### 12.2 Applying base-emitter bias

The right-hand circuit adds a **bias supply** between base and emitter, making their junction **forward biased**. Free electrons are then supplied from the N-type emitter into the very thin P-type base.

The simplified operating sequence is:

1. The applied **base-emitter bias** permits electrons to enter the base from the emitter.
2. The base is thin, so only a small proportion of these electrons recombine with holes in it.
3. Most continue through the base into the collector region.
4. The collector supply attracts and removes these electrons, sustaining the main emitter-to-collector electron movement.
5. Electrons involved in recombination are replaced through the base connection, producing a comparatively small **base current**.

The result is a **large collector-emitter current controlled by a much smaller base-emitter current**. The chapter intentionally limits the treatment to this basic explanation rather than developing the detailed semiconductor physics.

### 12.3 Relationship between controlling and controlled currents

The transistor therefore provides a way to influence a larger current without requiring the controlling circuit to carry that entire current itself. In the chapter's NPN explanation, **electron movement** is described from **emitter to collector**; this should not be confused with the opposite direction used when discussing **conventional current**.

## 13\. Operation of a P-N-P Transistor

A **PNP transistor** works on the same general principle as an NPN device: control of the base-emitter junction permits a larger current through the collector-emitter path.

The essential difference is the **reversed polarity of the external supplies**. The bias and collector-emitter connections must be appropriate to the PNP layer arrangement rather than copied directly from an NPN circuit.

## 14\. Summary and Applications

### 14.1 Transistors as switches

The control current at the **base-emitter junction** can be deliberately enabled or disabled. A transistor can therefore act as an electronically operated **switch**: a small control signal determines whether a much larger current is permitted through the main circuit.

### 14.2 Transistors as amplifiers

A transistor can also operate as an **amplifier**. When a small varying electrical signal is applied together with a suitable **bias voltage**, the resulting variation in the main collector-emitter current can be much larger than the controlling input variation. The textbook describes this as superimposing an alternating signal on the bias supply.

### 14.3 Combining components in practical circuits

Semiconductor devices become especially useful when combined with other fundamental circuit components, including:

* **Junction diodes**
* **Resistors**
* **Capacitors**
* **Inductors**
* **Transistors**

Their combined behavior allows the construction of many different forms of electronic circuit.

### 14.4 Integrated circuits and miniaturization

Manufacturing techniques can introduce dopants into very accurately defined areas of semiconductor material. The chapter refers to **photo-etching** techniques, through which many components can be created and interconnected within a **single small silicon chip**.

This is the basis of the familiar **integrated circuit**, including the computer chips found in electronic equipment. Improvements in manufacturing permit increasingly sophisticated and powerful circuitry to occupy smaller packages. Continued progress in this area supports the growth of electronic systems in modern airliners.

## 15\. Key Revision Points

* **Conductors** have many available charge carriers, high conductivity and low resistivity; **insulators** have comparatively few available carriers, low conductivity and high resistivity.
* **Silicon** and **germanium** are semiconductors. Their conductivity can be altered deliberately by **doping**.
* **N-type material** is formed with pentavalent impurities such as arsenic or antimony and provides additional free electrons.
* **P-type material** is formed with trivalent impurities such as aluminium or indium and provides holes.
* Doping does not give the complete specimen a net charge: mobile carriers are balanced by **fixed ions**.
* In **N-type material**, electrons move toward the positive terminal. In **P-type material**, the apparent hole movement follows conventional current direction toward the negative terminal.
* Joining P-type and N-type regions forms a **P-N junction** with a **depletion layer** and **barrier potential**.
* **Reverse bias** widens the depletion layer and leaves only a small leakage current; **forward bias** reduces the barrier and permits substantial conduction.
* A **junction diode** rectifies by preferentially conducting in one direction.
* **NPN** and **PNP** transistors have three regions: **collector, base, emitter**. The very thin middle region is the **base**.
* On a transistor symbol, the **NPN emitter arrow points outward** and the **PNP arrow points inward**.
* A relatively small **base-emitter current** controls a much larger **collector-emitter current**, allowing transistor **switching** and **amplification**.
* Precise doping and **photo-etching** enable many components to be combined into a compact **integrated circuit**.

**10.** The depletion region becomes **wider**, increasing the barrier. Significant forward-style conduction is blocked, although a very small **leakage current** remains.

**11.** The junction conducts substantially under **forward bias** but strongly opposes conduction under **reverse bias**, allowing current preferentially in one direction.

**12.** **Emitter**, **base** and **collector**.

**13.** The **emitter arrow** points **outward** on an **NPN** symbol and **inward** on a **PNP** symbol.

**14.** A small base-emitter control current can regulate a larger collector-emitter current. Turning the control current on and off provides **switching**; applying a small changing signal to the bias condition provides **amplification**.

**15.** Precisely controlled **doping** and **photo-etching** allow devices such as **transistors and diodes**, together with **resistors, capacitors and inductors**, to be fabricated and interconnected in compact integrated circuits.
