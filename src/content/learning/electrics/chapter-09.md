# Chapter 9: DC Electrics — Bonding and Screening

## 1\. Why Aircraft Need Bonding and Screening

An aircraft can acquire **static electrical charge** as it travels through the atmosphere. At the same time, certain items of onboard electrical equipment can create unwanted electrical disturbances. These are related problems but are dealt with by different arrangements:

* **Bonding** provides electrical continuity between aircraft parts so that they remain at substantially the same electrical potential. This reduces the possibility of a spark jumping between components.
* **Static dischargers (static wicks)** allow an accumulated charge to disperse from the airframe into the surrounding atmosphere.
* **Conductive tyres** help transfer residual aircraft static charge to the ground when the aeroplane touches down.
* **Screening** limits interference that electrical equipment might otherwise introduce into radio circuits.

A useful distinction for examination purposes is that **bonding addresses differences of electrical potential**, whereas **screening addresses electromagnetic or electrical interference**. Static wicks and conductive tyres deal with the accumulated charge itself, but at different stages of flight.

\---

## 2\. Bonding

### 2.1 What bonding does

**Bonding** is the deliberate electrical connection of separate aircraft components. Conductive links enable electrons to pass between metal parts instead of allowing substantial differences in static potential to develop between them.

An aircraft may become electrically charged in flight. If two nearby components reach different potentials, a discharge may occur between them as a **spark or arc**. Where flammable gases or materials are present, that spark represents a **fire hazard**. Bonding reduces the risk by keeping connected structural items at essentially the same potential.

The aim is **electrical continuity**, not electrical insulation. Separate pieces of the aircraft must not be left as isolated conductors capable of retaining significantly different charges.

### 2.2 How the connections are made

Metallic sections of the aircraft structure, together with the relevant aircraft components, are joined by **flexible conductive wire strips or bonding straps**. Flexibility allows the connections to remain effective between parts that may experience relative movement or vibration.

The effectiveness of a bonding connection depends on maintaining a **low-resistance conducting path**. In particular, the contact surfaces must be clean. Materials or surface conditions that would interrupt metal-to-metal conductivity include:

* **Anodized or otherwise insulating surface coatings**.
* **Paint** between the electrical contact surfaces.
* **Grease** at the connection.
* **Oxide deposits** or corrosion products.

The textbook also highlights the importance of avoiding **electrolytic corrosion**, which may increase the resistance of an electrical joint. The key maintenance principle is that a bonded connection should retain dependable electrical contact rather than become insulated by contamination or deterioration.

### 2.3 Additional functions of bonding

Besides controlling static potential differences, bonding has two further functions identified in the chapter:

1. **Earth-return route:** On an aircraft using a **unipole (single-pole) electrical system**, bonded structure may form part of the return path for electrical current.
2. **Reduction of radio interference:** Preventing local static discharges also reduces the electrical noise those discharges could produce in radio equipment.

The chapter's examination questions additionally identify the importance of a **low-resistance route for local static charges and lightning-strike currents**. This does not mean that bonding prevents an aircraft from being struck by lightning; its contribution is the provision of suitable conductive paths through the structure.

## 3\. The Static Discharge System or Static Wicks

### 3.1 Purpose

Even with effective bonding between components, the airframe may still carry an accumulated **overall static charge**. A **static discharge system** is provided to encourage this charge to escape into the atmosphere instead of building to a level at which uncontrolled discharges could occur.

Its discharge devices are commonly called **static wicks** or **static dischargers**. The original textbook emphasizes their role in reducing static accumulation and, consequently, **radio interference**.

### 3.2 Where the wicks are installed

The locations identified in the chapter are exposed extremities of the aircraft, particularly:

* The **trailing edges of flight-control surfaces**.
* The **tips of wings**.
* The **tips of stabilizing surfaces**.

Their purpose at these positions is to provide points from which excess charge can disperse away from the airframe.

### 3.3 Construction and discharge action

The book describes early wicks as **cotton devices approximately as thick as a cigarette**. The free end could become spread out, or **“teased”**, allowing a **brush-discharge action** that released charge into the air.

Modern designs are described as several possible forms:

* A small **barbed-antenna-like element**.
* A **fine wire brush**.
* A **straight metal wick**.

Although their physical appearances differ, the intended function is the same: **controlled dispersion of accumulated static electricity into the atmosphere**.

### 3.4 Connection with radio reception

A localized, uncontrolled static discharge can produce radio noise. By providing dedicated points for charge release, the static discharge system helps to **minimize interference with aircraft radio equipment**.

This is the central distinction tested in the chapter: static wicks are **not installed to smooth generator output**, **prevent tyre bursts**, or **act as the ordinary return conductor in a single-pole electrical circuit**.

## 4\. Discharge of Static on Touchdown

### 4.1 Why landing creates another discharge requirement

The aeroplane must not remain dangerously charged after landing. Static charge remaining on the aircraft could create an ignition risk if a discharge occurs near flammable materials. The chapter therefore describes an arrangement for **rapidly connecting the main bonded structure to the earth at touchdown**.

### 4.2 Conductive tyres

One method is to use aircraft tyres whose rubber contains a **high proportion of carbon**. The carbon makes the tyre sufficiently conductive to assist the discharge process.

Depending on the aircraft configuration, the conductive tyres may be fitted to the:

* **Nose wheel**;
* **Tail wheel**; or
* **Main wheels**.

### 4.3 Electrical route to the ground

The path described in the textbook is:

**Aircraft main bonding system → wheel bearing → carbon-containing tyre → ground.**

Once the wheel contacts the runway, residual charge has a path to **earth**, reducing the possibility that the aircraft will retain a hazardous static potential after touchdown.

The discharge function on landing should not be confused with the function of **static wicks in flight**. Both address accumulated static charge, but they provide different ways for it to leave the aircraft.

## 5\. Screening

### 5.1 What screening is intended to prevent

**Screening** is used to keep electrical disturbances generated by onboard equipment from interfering with **radio circuits**. Certain electrical components create unwanted electrical activity during operation, especially where they repeatedly make and break current or use rotating electrical contacts.

The textbook describes screening as a means of dealing with unwanted electrical energy so that it does not affect communications equipment.

### 5.2 How interference is limited

The arrangements described are:

1. **Interference suppressors:** Components fitted to cables connected to the equipment responsible for the disturbance.
2. **Continuous metallic sheathing:** A metal screen enclosing the relevant cables to limit the passage of interference to nearby circuits.

The important word is **continuous**: the book specifically calls for a continuous metal sheath around the cables, rather than isolated short pieces of screening material.

### 5.3 Equipment identified as requiring screening

The chapter lists these sources or operating conditions:

|Equipment or operation|Screening criterion stated in the textbook|
|-|-|
|**Ignition systems**|Screening is required.|
|**DC generators**|Screening is required for these commutator machines.|
|**DC motors**|Screening is required for these commutator machines.|
|**Slip-ring machines**|Screening is required when operating at **more than 200 RPM**.|
|**Make-and-break electrical equipment**|Screening is required where circuits are made and broken at a frequency of **more than 10 Hz**.|

Do not confuse the two numerical criteria: **200 RPM applies to slip-ring machines**, while **10 Hz applies to the rate at which equipment repeatedly makes and breaks a circuit**.

### 5.4 Bonding compared with screening

|Feature|Bonding|Screening|
|-|-|-|
|Principal problem addressed|Different electrical potentials and local static discharges|Electrical disturbance affecting radio circuits|
|Main arrangement|Conductive connections between aircraft structures or components|Interference suppressors and continuous metallic cable sheath|
|Direct safety benefit|Reduces the chance of arcing between aircraft parts|Reduces unwanted radio interference|
|Additional role|May contribute to the earth-return path in unipole systems|Prevents interference from reaching sensitive radio wiring|

Both are relevant to electrical reliability, but **they are not interchangeable**. Bonding connects components electrically; screening surrounds or suppresses sources of unwanted interference.

## 6\. Quick Revision — Four Systems, Four Functions

|System|Essential exam answer|
|-|-|
|**Bonding**|Keeps metallic aircraft parts at a common electrical potential through low-resistance connections; reduces dangerous static arcing.|
|**Static wicks**|Release accumulated charge from the airframe into the atmosphere; minimize static-related radio noise.|
|**Conductive tyres**|Permit charge from the bonded structure to pass to earth at touchdown.|
|**Screening**|Reduces electrical interference reaching radio circuits through suppressors and metallic cable enclosures.|

**Numerical facts:** Slip-ring machinery operating at **over 200 RPM** and circuit-making/breaking equipment operating at **over 10 Hz** are among the screening applications specified in the textbook.

# 7\. Chapter Practice Questions — Rewritten From the Original Seven

The seven questions below test the **same knowledge points** as the source chapter. Their wording has been rewritten rather than copied. Correct choices follow the textbook answer key (**C, D, B, C, D, D, D**).

### Question 1 — Static dischargers

**What is the principal reason for installing static wick dischargers on an aircraft?**

A. To regulate and smooth the electrical output of a generator.  
B. To prevent tyres from failing at touchdown.  
C. To reduce interference affecting radio equipment.  
D. To provide the normal current-return path in a single-pole circuit.

**Answer: C.** Static wicks provide dedicated points for the gradual discharge of accumulated charge into the atmosphere, helping to limit static-related radio noise.

### Question 2 — Prevention of static arcing

**How does aircraft bonding reduce the danger of static sparks between structural components?**

A. By using the metal skin only as a return wire for equipment.  
B. By shortening every negative electrical conductor.  
C. By deliberately maintaining different voltages on the individual panels.  
D. By keeping the connected metal parts at substantially the same electrical potential.

**Answer: D.** A common potential prevents large potential differences from developing between bonded components, reducing the likelihood of an arc.

### Question 3 — Why static can cause a fire

**What makes accumulated static electricity a possible fire hazard on an aircraft?**

A. Static charge causes all nearby metal parts to become hot enough to burn fuel.  
B. A potential difference can produce a spark capable of igniting flammable gas or material.  
C. Static discharge occurs only when the wrong colour of conductor is fitted.  
D. Static charge inevitably causes a tyre to burst at landing.

**Answer: B.** An electrical discharge across a gap can provide an ignition source if combustible material is present.

### Question 4 — Equalizing charge across the structure

**Which aircraft installation allows static electrical charges and currents to be equalized between structural parts?**

A. Structural hardening.  
B. Radio screening.  
C. Electrical bonding.  
D. Surface anodizing.

**Answer: C.** Bonding connects the metallic parts to provide low-resistance charge redistribution paths.

### Question 5 — Screening of aircraft electrical components

**Why are interference-producing electrical components and their wiring screened?**

A. To ensure the equipment can ignite fuel vapour safely.  
B. To stop every form of electrical discharge from occurring.  
C. To replace the aircraft's short-circuit protection devices.  
D. To reduce their effect on nearby radio equipment.

**Answer: D.** The screen and associated suppressors restrict unwanted electrical interference rather than serving as fuses or bonding straps.

### Question 6 — Fundamental purpose of bonding

**What condition should electrical bonding establish between metallic aircraft components?**

A. Protection from engine-compartment heat.  
B. A stronger positive electrical reaction in each part.  
C. Different electrical potentials for different structure sections.  
D. Substantially equal electrical potential across the connected components.

**Answer: D.** The common potential is what minimizes the risk of sparking due to charge differences.

### Question 7 — Overall bonding function

**Which statement best describes aircraft electrical bonding?**

A. It is fitted solely to stop magnetic-compass errors.  
B. It increases the anodizing effect on structural connections.  
C. It electrically isolates metal components so that charge remains trapped.  
D. It gives low-resistance return and discharge paths, including for local static charges and lightning-related currents.

**Answer: D.** Bonding provides electrical continuity, can serve return-path purposes, and helps conduct potentially hazardous currents through suitable connected parts of the airframe.
