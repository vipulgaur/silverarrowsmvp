## 1\. Magnetism: Meaning and Observable Properties

A **magnet** creates a region around itself in which magnetic forces can act. This effect can be detected even when the magnet is not touching the object being influenced.

Three key characteristics help identify a magnet:

1. **Attraction of magnetic materials:** A magnet can pull small pieces of iron or steel toward itself and may lift them.
2. **North–south alignment:** When allowed to rotate freely, a magnet tends to settle along a north–south direction.
3. **A surrounding magnetic field:** The space around the magnet is influenced by its magnetic force; this region is called the **magnetic field**.

The field is invisible, but its overall arrangement can be demonstrated by placing a sheet of paper over a magnet and spreading **iron filings** on the paper. The filings align into a recognisable pattern, revealing the shape of the field.

### 1.1 Magnetic Flux Lines

Diagrams represent the magnetic field with **lines of force**, also called **magnetic flux lines**. They are not physical strings: they provide a way of describing the field's direction and distribution.

* **Outside a magnet**, flux lines are drawn leaving its **north (N) pole** and entering its **south (S) pole**.
* Arrowheads indicate the direction a small compass needle would point when placed at a location in the field.
* Although a printed illustration may show only part of a line, the complete magnetic flux pattern forms **closed, continuous paths**.
* **Flux lines do not intersect**. At any one position, the magnetic field has a definite direction.

A field diagram also helps show **flux density**: closely spaced lines indicate a stronger magnetic field than widely spaced lines. The effects are generally concentrated near a magnet's poles, typically near its ends.

### 1.2 Interaction Between Magnetic Fields

When magnets are brought close together, the surrounding fields influence one another.

* **Fields acting in the same direction reinforce each other**, increasing the local concentration of flux.
* **Fields acting in opposing directions tend to weaken or cancel**, reducing the local field strength.

The final pattern depends on how the magnets are positioned and which poles face each other.

### 1.3 North and South Poles

A freely suspended magnet has two poles. The end that settles toward the Earth's magnetic north is called its **north-seeking pole**, or **N pole**; its opposite end is the **S pole**.

The essential attraction-and-repulsion rule is:

|Poles brought together|Result|
|-|-|
|North and north|Repulsion|
|South and south|Repulsion|
|North and south|Attraction|

**Remember:** Like poles **repel**; unlike poles **attract**. Both poles belong to the same magnetic system, and a magnet's magnetic action is associated with both of them.

## 2\. Temporary and Permanent Magnets

The material used to make a magnet determines how readily it becomes magnetised and how long it retains magnetic properties once the magnetising influence has been removed.

### 2.1 Temporary Magnets

**Soft iron** is used when a magnetic effect is needed only while an external magnetising influence is present. It responds readily to a magnetic field, but most of that magnetisation disappears after the influence is removed.

This combination makes soft iron suitable for the magnetic parts of electrically operated devices that need to **switch on and off**.

### 2.2 Permanent Magnets

The chapter identifies **hard alloy steels** as materials used for permanent magnets. They generally require greater effort to magnetise than soft iron, but they hold their magnetic properties much more effectively once magnetised.

### 2.3 Comparing Magnetic Materials

|Characteristic|Soft iron / temporary magnet|Hard alloy steel / permanent magnet|
|-|-|-|
|Response to an applied magnetising field|Readily magnetised|Less readily magnetised|
|Magnetism remaining afterward|Usually small|Remains comparatively strong|
|Suitability|Temporary magnetic action|Long-lasting magnetic action|
|Retentivity|Low|High relative to soft iron|

## 3\. Permeability, Retentivity and Residual Magnetism

### 3.1 Permeability

If unmagnetised **soft iron** is placed inside a magnetic field, magnetic flux becomes concentrated through the iron. The iron is also magnetised by that field, contributing to the overall magnetic effect.

The ability of a substance to provide an effective path for magnetic flux—and thereby increase the concentration of magnetic flux in the region—is expressed through its **permeability**.

**Practical point:** A soft-iron core can increase the strength of an electromagnet's field without requiring a different external field direction.

### 3.2 Retentivity

**Retentivity** concerns how well a material keeps its magnetism after the original magnetising influence is removed.

* **Soft iron:** magnetises easily but has **low retentivity**.
* **Permanent-magnet materials:** retain magnetisation much more effectively.

### 3.3 Residual Magnetism

Removing soft iron from an applied magnetic field does not necessarily make its magnetism exactly zero. The small remaining magnetic effect is called **residual magnetism**.

The same idea applies when the electric current in an electromagnet is switched off: most of its field disappears, but some magnetism may remain in the core.

### 3.4 Terms to Distinguish

|Term|Key meaning|
|-|-|
|**Permeability**|Ability to conduct/concentrate magnetic flux within the material|
|**Retentivity**|Ability to keep magnetisation after the magnetising influence is removed|
|**Residual magnetism**|Magnetisation still present after removal of the external influence|
|**Saturation**|Condition beyond which the material cannot acquire significant further magnetisation in the chapter's model|

## 4\. Methods of Removing Magnetism

The text identifies **three ways to demagnetise magnetic material**:

1. **Heat it:** Sufficient heating disrupts the existing magnetised alignment.
2. **Hammer it:** Mechanical shock can disturb the internal magnetic arrangement.
3. **Apply an alternating magnetic influence:** Place the material in a **solenoid supplied with alternating current (AC)**. The changing magnetic field can progressively remove retained magnetisation.

These methods act on the internal magnetic state rather than merely changing the magnet's position.

## 5\. The Molecular Structure of Magnets

The textbook explains magnetic alignment through a simplified picture of internal **magnetised molecules**. Its diagrams compare three conditions.

### 5.1 Unmagnetised Material

The internal magnetic elements are pictured in **closed chains** or irregularly directed groups. Their magnetic effects do not combine to form a strong overall external magnet.

### 5.2 Magnetised Material

An applied magnetic field encourages the internal magnetic elements to **align with the field**. With more of the elements directed together, the material develops an observable magnetic effect.

### 5.3 Magnetic Saturation

The material approaches **saturation** as more of these internal elements become aligned. In the simplified model used here, once they are all aligned, further magnetising action cannot produce additional magnetisation.

This explains the conceptual progression:

**Unmagnetised → magnetising field applied → increased alignment → saturation.**

## 6\. Magnetic Effect of an Electric Current

Magnetism is not restricted to permanent magnets. **Current flowing through a conductor also produces a magnetic field.**

Around a **straight current-carrying wire**, the magnetic field takes the form of **concentric circular loops** surrounding the conductor.

The field's direction depends on the **direction of current**. Reversing the current also reverses the circulation direction of the magnetic field around the conductor.

This is the foundation of electromagnets, solenoids, relays and electric motors.

## 7\. The Corkscrew Rule

The **right-handed corkscrew rule** gives the direction of magnetic flux around a straight conductor carrying current.

Imagine turning a conventional, right-handed corkscrew so that it advances along the wire in the **direction of conventional current**. The direction in which the corkscrew rotates shows the **direction of the circular magnetic field**.

### 7.1 Current Into and Out of the Page

In circuit illustrations, a conductor may be viewed end-on:

* A **cross in a circle** represents a direction **into the page**.
* A **dot in a circle** represents a direction **out of the page**.

These symbols are useful because the actual conductor may be perpendicular to the drawing surface.

**Important distinction:** Apply the corkscrew rule to **conventional current**, not to the opposite direction used for electron flow.

## 8\. Combination of Magnetic Fields

When two current-carrying conductors are close enough for their fields to overlap, the individual field patterns are altered by one another.

The important idea is **superposition of magnetic effects**:

* Where their fields have the **same direction**, the combined field is strengthened.
* Where they have **opposite directions**, the combined effect is weaker.

The resulting lines curve around the conductors according to their relative current directions and positions. Field lines still do not cross one another.

## 9\. Magnetic Field Produced by a Coil

A straight wire produces circular magnetic field lines around itself. When the wire is **wound into multiple turns**, the fields from the different turns interact.

Inside the coil, the combined field becomes substantially more directed along the **length of the coil**. The overall field pattern resembles that of a **bar magnet**, with north and south ends and external returning flux paths.

This principle leads directly to the solenoid.

## 10\. Solenoids and Electromagnets

A **solenoid** is a coil made up of many turns of **insulated wire**. When current passes through it, the magnetic fields of the individual turns combine to create the field of an **electromagnet**.

In the textbook's explanation, the fields between adjacent turns tend to cancel locally, while their overall combined pattern resembles the field surrounding a bar magnet.

This controllable magnetic action is central to the operation of numerous **aircraft electrical circuits and switching devices**.

### 10.1 Right Hand Grasp Rule

The **right hand grasp rule** is used to identify the poles of a current-carrying solenoid:

1. Imagine holding the solenoid with the **right hand**.
2. Curl the fingers in the direction of **conventional current** flowing around its turns.
3. The extended **thumb points toward the north pole** of the solenoid.

The thumb therefore identifies which end acts as **N**; the opposite end acts as **S**.

**Do not confuse the two rules:** The corkscrew rule gives field circulation around a straight conductor; the right-hand grasp rule establishes the north-pole end of a coil.

### 10.2 Increasing Solenoid Field Strength

The chapter identifies **three principal methods** for increasing the magnetic field produced by a solenoid:

|Change|Effect|
|-|-|
|Add more turns to the coil|Increases the field produced by the winding|
|Increase the current through the winding|Strengthens the electromagnetic effect|
|Insert a soft-iron core|Concentrates and increases the magnetic flux|

When the current is interrupted, the main magnetic field collapses. A soft-iron core can still hold a small amount of **residual magnetism** afterward.

## 11\. Solenoids and Relays as Remote Switching Devices

A small electrical control current can be used to operate a device whose contacts switch a **much larger current**. Solenoids and relays make this possible without forcing the high-current circuit to pass directly through a cockpit switch.

The chapter uses the **starter solenoid in a piston-engine starting circuit** as a practical aviation example.

### 11.1 Solenoid Operation

A solenoid has a **moving magnetic core**. Energising its coil creates a magnetic attraction that moves the core and operates the associated contacts or mechanism.

### 11.2 Relay Operation

A relay employs a **stationary core** and a separate **attracted armature**. When the winding is energised, the armature moves and changes the state of the controlled contacts.

### 11.3 Isolation of the Control and Power Circuits

In both devices:

* The **coil winding is insulated**.
* The winding does not make a direct electrical contact with the circuit whose load is switched.
* The **low-current operating circuit** controls the operation of the contacts.
* The **higher-current circuit** is opened or closed by those contacts.

This separation permits remote operation of high-current aircraft equipment.

### 11.4 Solenoid Versus Relay

|Feature|Solenoid|Relay|
|-|-|-|
|Core described in the chapter|**Moving**|**Stationary**|
|Output movement|Core itself moves|An attracted armature moves|
|Low-power input|Energised coil|Energised coil|
|Controlled operation|Movement/contacts enable load switching|Armature changes electrical contacts|
|Example application|Starter circuit solenoid|Remote switching of electrical loads|

## 12\. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor in a Magnetic Field

A wire carrying current has its **own magnetic field**. If the wire is placed within the field between magnetic poles, the two fields interact.

On one side of the wire, the fields can **reinforce** one another; on the opposite side, they can **oppose** each other. This produces an uneven magnetic field around the wire and a resulting **force on the conductor**.

If the conductor is free to move, it moves in the direction of the resultant force.

This interaction is the fundamental physical effect used by an **electric motor**.

### 12.1 Direction of Motion

The direction of motion can be determined by **Fleming's Left Hand Rule**. The textbook introduces that rule here and develops it further in the motor chapter; this section focuses on the interaction of the conductor's field with the external magnetic field.

### 12.2 Lorentz Force

The magnetic force acting on a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field is referred to in the chapter as **Lorentz force**.

The essential sequence is:

**Current in conductor → surrounding magnetic field → interaction with external magnetic field → unequal field strengths → force and motion.**
