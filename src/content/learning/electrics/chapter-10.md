# Chapter 10 — DC Electrics: Specimen Questions

# 1\. Quick Reference: Knowledge Tested in This Chapter

The two question sets assess the practical application of **DC electrical fundamentals** and the aircraft systems covered in the preceding chapters. The recurring subjects are:

* **Circuit protection:** Correctly rated fuses, spare fuses, circuit breakers, and responses to an electrical overload.
* **DC power generation:** Generator output, voltage regulation, generator cut-outs, load sharing, and parallel operation.
* **Distribution and load management:** Busbars, bus-tie arrangements, circulating currents, and load shedding.
* **Batteries:** Battery voltage checks, series connections, capacity in ampere-hours, and nickel-cadmium temperature hazards.
* **Electric motors:** The relationship between speed and back electromotive force (back EMF).
* **Aircraft wiring and protection:** Earth-return systems, two-wire (dipole) circuits, electrical bonding, and electromagnetic screening.
* **Electrical calculations:** Ohm's law, power, resistance-unit conversions, and battery discharge rates.

## 1.1 Formula Reference

|Quantity|Relationship|Meaning|
|-|-|-|
|Voltage|**V = I × R**|Potential difference equals current multiplied by resistance.|
|Current|**I = V / R**|Current depends on applied voltage and circuit resistance.|
|Power|**P = V × I**|Electrical power equals voltage multiplied by current.|
|Resistive power|**P = I² × R**|Power dissipated by a resistor from current and resistance.|
|Resistive power|**P = V² / R**|Power dissipated by a resistor from voltage and resistance.|
|Battery capacity|**Capacity (Ah) = current (A) × time (h)**|Approximate discharge relationship under the capacity's stated test conditions.|
|Megohm conversion|**1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω**|One megohm equals one million ohms.|

> \*\*Accuracy note:\*\* The voltage/current relationships assume the resistance stays constant. Actual batteries, generators, and protective devices are subject to their operating characteristics and aircraft-specific procedures.

## 6.1 General 1 — Detailed Answers

**Question 1 — Carriage of Spare Fuses:** **Answer: C.** The question tests the requirement for a defined supply of replacement fuses where applicable. The quantity and applicability must be established from the rules relevant to the aircraft and operation; this is not a discretionary cockpit decision.

**Question 2 — Selecting the Correct Fuse:** **Answer: D.** The normal equipment demand determines the expected operating current, which is fundamental to choosing circuit protection. The fuse must also be compatible with the circuit's approved specifications; physical fit alone is insufficient.

**Question 3 — Differential Cut-Out:** **Answer: A.** The device senses the voltage relationship between the generating side and battery/distribution side so that the generator is connected under suitable conditions and reverse current is prevented.

**Question 4 — Circulating Current:** **Answer: C.** A voltage mismatch between parallel sources can drive unwanted current from one source toward another. Good voltage regulation and correct parallel operation minimize this effect.

**Question 5 — Resistance Units:** **Answer: C.** The prefix *mega* denotes one million, so **1 MΩ = 10⁶ Ω**.

**Question 6 — Load Shedding:** **Answer: C.** Load shedding disconnects selected loads, generally giving priority to essential services when available electrical power is limited.

**Question 7 — Generator Ammeter:** **Answer: D.** When the instrument is the generator-output ammeter, it indicates generator loading. It should not automatically be interpreted as a battery charge/discharge indication.

**Question 8 — Electrical Power Equations:** **Answer: A.** Beginning with **P = V × I** and using **V = I × R**, substitute either voltage or current to obtain **P = I²R** or **P = V²/R**. The other option groups include expressions with incorrect dimensions or relationships.

**Question 9 — Blown Fuse in Flight:** **Answer: D.** The source question assumes that a single replacement with the approved rating is allowed; repeated replacement or increasing the rating can defeat protection. **In actual operation, follow the aircraft's approved checklist and maintenance limitations—do not treat this exam answer as universal authority to replace a fuse in flight.**

**Question 10 — Voltage and Current:** **Answer: A.** From **I = V/R**, if **R** does not change, doubling **V** doubles **I**.

**Question 11 — Resistor Power Calculation:** **Answer: C.** Use **P = I²R**:

**P = 4² × 5 = 16 × 5 = 80 W.**

The 20 value represents **V = I × R = 20 V**; it is not the power.

**Question 12 — Checking a Lead-Acid Battery:** **Answer: B.** A loaded measurement provides information about how the battery behaves when supplying current. Actual battery serviceability checks depend on the approved battery testing procedure; open-circuit measurements may still be useful for other purposes.

**Question 13 — Batteries in Series:** **Answer: D.** Series connection adds battery voltages. With identical batteries, the same current flows through each, so the pack's rated **Ah** capacity remains that of an individual battery (while total energy at the higher voltage increases).

**Question 14 — Battery Capacity and Discharge Rate:** **Answer: C.** At the specified 10-hour rate, **I = 60 Ah / 10 h = 6 A**. A 60 A discharge for one hour would have the same arithmetic product but is **not guaranteed to yield the nominal rated capacity**, because usable battery capacity depends on discharge rate and conditions.

**Question 15 — Nickel-Cadmium Battery Temperature:** **Answer: A.** Excessive heating can indicate **thermal runaway**, in which the battery's temperature and charging behavior reinforce each other. This requires the response specified by the aircraft's procedures.

**Question 16 — Parallel Generator Voltage:** **Answer: B.** Parallel connection requires compatible voltage levels; mismatch may produce circulating current or uneven loading.

## 6.2 General 2 — Detailed Answers

**Question 1 — DC Generator Voltage Regulation:** **Answer: A.** Altering field current changes magnetic-field strength and therefore generated EMF. This allows regulation despite changes in rotational speed and electrical demand.

**Question 2 — Function of the Generator Cut-Out:** **Answer: D.** If the generator's EMF drops below battery voltage, reverse current could cause the generator to behave as a motor or otherwise discharge the battery. The cut-out prevents this unwanted backfeed.

**Question 3 — Generator Cut-Out Contacts:** **Answer: B.** Once generator voltage is sufficient relative to the bus/battery, the cut-out can connect it to the system.

**Question 4 — Indications of Generator Failure:** **Answer: A.** The generator stops contributing useful current, and the electrical load may transfer to another source such as the battery. Indication specifics vary by aircraft.

**Question 5 — One Generator Fails on a Twin-Engine Aircraft:** **Answer: D.** Loss of a generator does not, by itself, imply engine shutdown. Electrical-system consequences must still be managed.

**Question 6 — Earth-Return Wiring:** **Answer: A.** The airframe provides the return conductor when bonded to the negative side of the electrical supply.

**Question 7 — Fault in a Two-Wire Circuit:** **Answer: D.** A direct connection between opposite conductors constitutes a short circuit. Correctly selected protection is intended to disconnect the fault.

**Question 8 — Overloaded Circuit Breaker:** **Answer: A.** The intended principle is to **clear the overload rather than continually re-energize a faulted circuit**. Actual circuit-breaker reset authorization depends on the aircraft's published procedures; a tripped breaker must not be repeatedly reset merely to restore equipment.

**Question 9 — Replacement Fuse Rating:** **Answer: D.** A higher-rated fuse may fail to protect the wiring, while a lower-rated one can cause unwanted operation. Correct type and rating are necessary.

**Question 10 — Back EMF and Motor Speed:** **Answer: C.** A faster rotating armature generates a stronger opposing EMF. Back EMF limits armature current as speed increases.

**Question 11 — Output of a Shunt-Wound Generator:** **Answer: B.** The examination material treats output as remaining substantially constant under the assumed operating arrangement. **Qualification:** An *unregulated* shunt-wound DC generator does normally experience terminal-voltage droop as load grows; an approximately constant output voltage requires suitable regulation and operating conditions. The source does not spell out that distinction in this question.

**Question 12 — Load Shedding Revisited:** **Answer: D.** Loads of lower priority can be shed to keep essential equipment powered when supply is constrained.

**Question 13 — Inertia Switch:** **Answer: C.** An inertia switch is intended to detect a sufficiently severe impact event and trigger the associated protective function.

**Question 14 — Electrical Bonding:** **Answer: D.** Bonding reduces hazardous potential differences between metal parts and enables intended electrical continuity. It also contributes to static and lightning protection.

**Question 15 — Electrical Screening:** **Answer: B.** Screening limits electromagnetic interference from electrical wiring and components, helping to protect sensitive radio and communication systems.
