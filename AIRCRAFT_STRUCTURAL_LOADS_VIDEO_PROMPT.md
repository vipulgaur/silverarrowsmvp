### Stage 6: Stress, Strain, Young's Modulus, Deformation & Buckling
* **Duration:** 2:15 – 3:05
* **Educational Objective:** Define Stress ($\text{Force}/\text{Area}$ in $\text{N/mm}^2$ or $\text{MN/m}^2$), Strain ($\%$ dimensional change), Young's Modulus, Elastic vs. Plastic Deformation, and Compressive Buckling.
* **Scene Visual Prompt:**
  ```text
  Split-screen 3D materials-testing animation.
  Left Viewport: A metallic aircraft test bar with a highlighted cross-sectional grid (Area A in mm²) is pulled by Force F, displaying the equation "Stress = Force / Area (N/mm² = MN/m²)" and a dimensional percentage gauge showing "Strain = (ΔL / L₀) × 100%".
  Right Viewport: A live glowing Stress-Strain curve plots upward along a straight line labeled "Young's Modulus of Elasticity". First, the load stops below the glowing "Elastic Limit" dot and releases—the bar returns 100% to its original length ("Elastic Deformation"). Second, the load pushes beyond the Elastic Limit into the curved zone and releases—the bar remains permanently elongated ("Plastic Deformation").
  Bottom Inset: A thin aluminum sheet panel and a slender tie rod are pushed from both ends under compression; they suddenly bow sideways into an unstable wave, labeled "BUCKLING — Compressive Instability in Thin Sheets & Ties".
  ```
* **On-Screen HUD Callout:**
  * `2.3 STRESS = Internal Force per Unit Area = Force / Area (Units: N/mm² or MN/m²)`
  * `2.4 STRAIN = Dimensional Change Expressed as % of Original Dimension (Young's Modulus)`
  * `2.5 BUCKLING = Unstable Bowing of Thin Sheet Materials or Ties Under Compression`
  * `2.6 ELASTIC DEFORMATION (Returns to Original Size) vs. PLASTIC DEFORMATION (Permanent Change Beyond Elastic Limit)`
* **Voiceover Script:**
  > "Externally applied loads create internal stress—defined as force divided by area and measured in Newtons per square millimeter or Meganewtons per square meter. When stress changes a component's dimensions, that deformation is called strain, expressed as a percentage of the original dimension. For an elastic material, stress and strain follow a constant known as Young's Modulus of Elasticity. If the load stays within the elastic limit, the material returns to its original dimensions once unloaded. Exceeding the elastic limit causes permanent plastic deformation. And when thin sheet materials or tensile ties are subjected to compressive end loads, they become unstable and bow outward in a phenomenon called buckling."

* **Duration:** 3:05 – 3:45

  Right Scene (Dynamic Loading & Engine Failure): The same aircraft in flight executes a rapid pull-up manoeuvre through gusty air as a live G-meter spikes rapidly, sending glowing shockwaves of dynamic load across the airframe.   ```
* **On-Screen HUD Callout:**
  * `2.7 STATIC LOADS: Steady or Slow to Build (Parked Aircraft: Downward Weight vs. Upward Landing Gear Reaction)`
  * `2.7 DYNAMIC LOADS: Build Up Rapidly During Flight Manoeuvres & Changing Flight Conditions`
  * `3.2 ENGINE FAILURE (Twin-Engine): Asymmetric Thrust → Yawing Moment → Opposite Rudder → Fin & Fuselage Loading`
* **Voiceover Script:**
  > "Structural loads are further divided into static and dynamic loads. Static loads are steady or build slowly—such as a parked aircraft whose downward weight is opposed by upward reaction forces from the landing gear. Dynamic loads build up rapidly during flight manoeuvres or sudden changes in flight condition. For example, if one engine fails on a twin-engine aircraft, asymmetric thrust from the live engine creates a yawing moment; when the pilot applies opposite rudder to counter the yaw, severe additional loads are transmitted through the fin, fuselage, and wings."

### Stage 8: Certification Limits — Design Limit Load (DLL), Design Ultimate Load (DUL) & Safety Factor
* **Duration:** 3:45 – 4:30
* **Educational Objective:** Define DLL, DUL, and Safety Factor ($\text{SF} = 1.5$), including the textbook $g$-limits for Transport, Utility, and Aerobatic aircraft and the worked $+2.5g \times 1.5 = +3.75g$ calculation.
* **Scene Visual Prompt:**
  ```text
  Grand finale 3D animation inside an aerospace structural certification test rig. Hydraulic harnesses pull upward on both wings of a transport aircraft while a giant holographic load table and G-meter animate in the foreground.
  First, the wings flex to the Design Limit Load (DLL) marker as three category badges illuminate:
  - Transport Aircraft: +2.5 g and -1.0 g
  - Utility Aircraft: 4.4 g
  - Aerobatic Aircraft: 6 g
  Next, the holographic equation "DUL = DLL × Safety Factor (1.5)" and "SF = DUL / DLL" animate on screen. For the Transport category (+2.5 g), the hydraulic rig pulls 1.5 times harder up to +3.75 g (Design Ultimate Load). The wings bend dramatically upward under glowing orange stress lines but hold intact without structural collapse.
  ```
* **On-Screen HUD Callout:**
  * `3.3 DESIGN LIMIT LOAD (DLL) — Maximum Load Expected in Normal Service:`
    * `Transport: +2.5 g / -1.0 g | Utility: 4.4 g | Aerobatic: 6 g`
  * `3.4 DESIGN ULTIMATE LOAD (DUL) = DLL × Safety Factor (Must Withstand Without Collapse)`
  * `3.5 SAFETY FACTOR (SF) = DUL / DLL (Minimum Design Requirement = 1.5)`
  * `Worked Example (Transport): DUL = 2.5 g × 1.5 = 3.75 g`
* **Voiceover Script:**
  > "Finally, how much load must an airframe be built to handle? The Design Limit Load—or DLL—is the maximum load the designer expects the aircraft to encounter in normal service: plus 2.5g and minus 1.0g for transport aircraft, 4.4g for utility aircraft, and 6g for aerobatic aircraft. To ensure an extra margin of safety, engineers multiply the Design Limit Load by a minimum Safety Factor of 1.5 to establish the Design Ultimate Load, or DUL. For a transport aircraft rated at plus 2.5g, the Design Ultimate Load is 2.5 times 1.5—equal to 3.75g—which the airframe must withstand without structural collapse."

## 4. Quick Reference Summary Table (For End-Screen Graphic)

| Concept / Term | Core Definition | Key Component / Formula / Value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Tension (Tensile Load)** | Force that attempts to **stretch or lengthen** a member | Resisted by a **Tie** |
| **Compression** | Force that pushes ends together to **shorten** a member | Resisted by a **Strut** |
| **Shear** | Force that tries to **slide one face over an adjacent face** | Resisted by **Riveted Joints** |
| **Bending** | Combination of **Tension** (outer edge), **Compression** (inner edge), and **Shear** (across structure) | Wing lift: Upper skin = Compression, Lower skin = Tension |
| **Torsion** | **Twisting** load: Tension at outer edge, Compression toward centre, Shear across structure | Occurs alongside bending in aircraft wings |
| **Stress** | Internal force per unit area developed inside a loaded component | $\text{Stress} = \text{Force} / \text{Area}$ ($\text{N/mm}^2$ or $\text{MN/m}^2$) |
| **Strain** | Dimensional deformation expressed as a **percentage of original dimension** | Governed by **Young's Modulus of Elasticity** |
| **Buckling** | Unstable sideways bowing under **compressive loading** | Affects **thin sheet materials** and **ties** under compression |
| **Elastic vs. Plastic** | **Within Elastic Limit:** returns to original size. **Beyond Elastic Limit:** permanent **plastic deformation** | Critical boundary for permanent structural damage |
| **Static vs. Dynamic** | **Static:** steady/slow (parked weight vs. gear reaction). **Dynamic:** rapid build-up (manoeuvres, engine-out yaw) | Twin-engine failure causes yaw & rudder fin loading |
| **DLL / DUL / SF** | **DLL:** Max expected service load. **DUL:** $\text{DLL} \times \text{SF}$ (no collapse). **SF:** $\text{DUL} / \text{DLL}$ | Min $\text{SF} = 1.5$; Transport $\text{DLL} = +2.5g/-1.0g \rightarrow \text{DUL} = 3.75g$ |
